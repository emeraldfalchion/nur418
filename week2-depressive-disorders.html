Depressive Disorders

Background & Epidemiology

FactorWhat Is Known
ScopeAbout 20 million people in the US affected; roughly half the population is affected over a lifetime.
TreatabilityResponds very well to treatment: 80%–90% of adults and 70%–80% of children respond.
ComorbidityRarely occurs alone. Frequently accompanies substance use, eating disorders, and personality disorders; anxiety with depression is the most common presentation.
SexWomen twice as often as men.
RaceOccurs more often in Caucasians, but is more severe in African Americans.
SeasonalityIncreased incidence where sunlight is decreased — depression with a seasonal onset, formerly called seasonal affective disorder.
Socioeconomic statusMore frequent in lower-income areas; the direction of causation is unclear — low status may lead to depression, or depression may limit the ability to reach a higher status.

Etiology & Contributing Factors

No single cause. Identifying the causal factors is what directs treatment toward therapy, medication, or both.

Biological Factors

  • Genetic: runs in families.
  • Neurotransmitters: serotonin and norepinephrine, sometimes dopamine, glutamate, and acetylcholine.
  • Electrolyte disturbances, and cortisol is higher in depressed individuals.
  • Hormonal imbalance: the estrogen-to-progesterone ratio. Thyroid plays a huge role — about 25% of people with depression have a hypoactive thyroid, so T3, T4, and TSH are checked on admission.
  • Inflammatory process.

Psychological Factors

  • Cognitive theory: thoughts lead to emotions. Negative or unrealistic expectations of the environment, the self, and the future lead to dissatisfaction.
  • Learning theory — learned helplessness: repeated exposure to uncontrollable stressors, where nothing the person tries helps, leads to failing to use any coping skills at all.

Contributing Factors Across the Lifespan

StageContributing Factors
ChildrenDetachment from a primary caregiver, physical or emotional — a secure attachment with one primary caregiver is protective. Parental separation, divorce, or foster placement; death of a loved one, including a pet; relocation; academic failure; physical illness.
TeensAdult and child factors apply, plus conflict between independence and maturation, and role confusion. Grief and loss — including a relationship breakup — is a frequent precursor.
Older adultsSocietal attitudes that value money, youth, and productivity over wisdom. Financial problems; job loss or retirement, which also severs social connections; relocation; chronic illness and chronic pain; bereavement overload from many losses over a lifetime; decreased functional ability.

Major Depressive Disorder

All the depressive disorders share sadness, emptiness, irritability, somatic concerns, and impaired thinking, and all impair the ability to function.

Adults

  • Five or more symptoms present daily in the same 2-week period, and at least one must be depressed mood or anhedonia (loss of interest or pleasure in almost all activities).
  • Weight or appetite change · sleep disturbance · fatigue · psychomotor agitation or retardation · worthlessness or guilt · loss of ability to concentrate · recurrent thoughts of death.
  • Symptoms cause marked impairment in social or occupational functioning.
  • Not attributable to a substance, medication, or medical condition.
  • No manic or hypomanic episode. If one ever occurs, the MDD diagnosis is dropped and replaced by a bipolar diagnosis.

Children & Teens

  • The two sources give different counts. The pre-lecture material requires five or more symptoms in a 2-week period; the live review requires three or more of the top criteria. Both agree that BOTH depressed or irritable, cranky mood AND anhedonia are required.
  • Plus any three of: significant weight loss or decreased appetite (more than 5% of body weight in a month, or failure to meet expected weight gain) · insomnia or hypersomnia · psychomotor agitation or retardation · fatigue · worthlessness or guilt · decreased concentration or indecisiveness · recurrent thoughts of death or suicide.
  • Also: frequent vague physical complaints, school absence and poor performance, boredom (which may be anhedonia), alcohol or substance use, increased anger or hostility, reckless behavior.
  • Assessment relies on behaviors and statements, plus collateral from parents and teachers — children may not have the words for how they feel.

About 20% of MDD becomes chronic, lasting more than two years. Recurrent episodes are the norm rather than the exception, and most recurrences happen within the first year. Specifiers carry that detail in the chart — most often chronic, episodic, and first episode.

The Other Depressive Disorders

DisorderDefining Features
Disruptive Mood Dysregulation Disorder (DMDD)Diagnosed only in children 6–18, with onset before age 10. Core feature is chronic, severe, persistent irritability: verbal or behavioral temper outbursts at least 3× weekly for at least a year, in at least 2 settings (home, school, with peers), plus a persistently irritable or angry mood between the outbursts. Occurs in 2%–5% of children; more common in males. Created in 2013 because children were being given a bipolar diagnosis and strong medications for what was a dysregulation of mood — most converted to MDD or an anxiety disorder in adulthood. Comorbid with oppositional defiant disorder, ADHD, and MDD.
Persistent Depressive DisorderFormerly dysthymia. Low-level depressed feelings most of the day, the majority of days, for at least 2 years in adults — an underlying depressed feeling, less severe than MDD but ongoing. Requires two or more of: decreased appetite or overeating, insomnia or hypersomnia, low energy, poor self-esteem, difficulty thinking or deciding, hopelessness. About two-thirds respond to antidepressants, with MAOIs and SSRIs more effective than tricyclics.
Premenstrual Dysphoric DisorderAffects about 5% of women. Severe mood changes in the week before menstruation: mood swings, irritability, depression, anxiety, feeling overwhelmed, difficulty concentrating; plus low energy, overeating, hypersomnia or insomnia, breast tenderness, aching, bloating, weight gain. Symptoms decrease significantly or disappear once menstruation starts. Uniquely, medication is not taken continuously — an SSRI such as fluoxetine is taken for the premenstrual week only.
Substance-Induced Depressive DisorderThe person does not experience depressive symptoms in the absence of drug or alcohol use or withdrawal.
Depressive Disorder Due to Another Medical ConditionSymptoms result from a medical diagnosis or its medications — kidney failure, Parkinson's disease, and Alzheimer's disease, for example — and are not counted as major depressive disorder. History, physical exam, and labs distinguish the two.

Assessment & the Clinical Judgment Model

StepWhat It Involves
Recognize cuesAssess affect, mood, thought process and content, judgment and insight, communication, and physical behavior and activity.
Analyze cuesRisk for suicide — safety is always the highest priority. Also hopelessness, ineffective coping, social isolation, and self-care deficit. Hopelessness and loss of hope make self-harm more likely.
Prioritize hypothesesRank the problems by urgency, complexity, and time.
Generate solutionsUse the recovery model: healing is possible and attainable, focus on the patient's strengths, develop goals mutually from their needs and values, and make outcomes measurable — specific, patient-centered, and with a time frame ("sleeps six uninterrupted hours two nights running").
Take actionTherapeutic communication; teach challenging and restructuring negative thinking; administer medications and provide education; observe for safety; help the patient list three people they can call with phone numbers, kept somewhere visible; teach coping strategies; establish a sleep routine; encourage group therapy.

Screening Tools

  • PHQ-9 (Patient Health Questionnaire) — the one used in clinical assessment.
  • Zung Depression Scale · Hamilton Depression Rating Scale · Geriatric Depression Scale · Beck Depression Inventory · Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale.
  • Why screening matters: people do not seek help for depression — they present to primary care with the somatic complaints that go with it, such as insomnia, fatigue, or weight loss. Hence the push for depression screening at every visit; the sooner treatment starts, the better the outcome.

Psychomotor Retardation vs. Agitation

Psychomotor Retardation

  • Visible slowing of physical activity — movement or speech.
  • Slow talking, long pauses before speaking, taking a long time to cross a room, slow chewing with long waits between bites.
  • Associated with severe depression.

Psychomotor Agitation

  • An increase in activity brought on by mental tension.
  • Restlessness, pacing, tapping fingers or feet, abruptly starting and stopping tasks, meaninglessly moving objects around.
  • Associated with agitated depression.

Antidepressant Principles

Every antidepressant below is also gathered by class, with the other Week 2 drugs, in Medications

Antidepressant therapy is effective for most cases of MDD, especially severe ones, but psychotherapy combined with an antidepressant is superior to either alone. All antidepressants increase the availability of neurotransmitters and have similar efficacy in trials; they differ in adverse effects, cost, and safety.

Physiological symptoms improve before psychological symptoms. Energy returns while mood is still depressed — and that is exactly when the risk of acting on suicidal thoughts is highest. Teach patients, caregivers, and family members this.

General Antidepressant Education

  • Symptom improvement may not appear for 4–6 weeks.
  • Look first for improved sleep, less daytime fatigue, less crying and irritability, and increased frustration tolerance.
  • Side effects are usually handled by adjusting the dose or switching to another drug in the same class.
  • Do not stop when feeling better — that causes relapse. Medication is needed 6–9 months past symptom relief, so most patients take it 12–24 months before tapering.
  • Antidepressants are not addictive, but stopping abruptly causes withdrawal: nausea, anxiety, insomnia, flu-like symptoms.
  • No alcohol. Alcohol is a stronger depressant than any available antidepressant.
  • Watch for emerging mania. A shift to elevated mood or markedly increased energy soon after starting an antidepressant is a warning sign, not improvement.

Order of Drug Classes Tried

SSRIs / SRIs

First-line. If one fails, a second drug in the same class is tried before moving on.

Tricyclics (TCAs)

Second choice. Cheaper, but anticholinergic, cardiotoxic, and lethal in overdose.

MAOIs

Third choice. Reserved for treatment-resistant depression because of the food and drug restrictions.

← safest, tried firstmost restricted, tried last →

Nursing Implications

  • Effectiveness takes 4–8 weeks; most side effects diminish in 4–6 weeks.
  • Monitor for suicidal ideation, extreme agitation, fever, increased blood pressure, and manic symptoms.
  • Teach sleep hygiene and relaxation techniques.
  • Abrupt discontinuation may be mild or severe: "electrical surges" or "brain shivers" in the head or body, pins-and-needles sensations on the skin, blackouts, short-term memory loss, and feeling on the verge of unconsciousness.

Serotonin Syndrome

Rare but potentially life-threatening. 60% of patients who develop it do so within 6 hours of a first dose, a change in dosing, or an intentional overdose. If the serotonergic medication is not discontinued it progresses to worsening myoclonus, hypertension, rigor, acidosis, respiratory failure, and rhabdomyolysis, and it can be fatal through hyperpyrexia or cardiovascular shock.
SystemSymptoms
Mental statusAgitation, confusion, restlessness, lethargy, delirium, irritability, dizziness, hallucinations.
AutonomicDiaphoresis, flushing, fever, tachycardia, mydriasis.
NeuromuscularMyoclonus (muscle twitching or jerks), hyperreflexia, tremors.
GastrointestinalNausea, vomiting, diarrhea.

Treating Serotonin Syndrome

  • Stop or reduce the contributing medications, depending on symptom severity and as directed by the prescriber. This is the first action.
  • Benzodiazepines — diazepam or lorazepam — for agitation, seizures, and muscle stiffness.
  • Avoid antipsychotics such as haloperidol for the agitation — they can worsen hyperthermia and contribute to a neuroleptic malignant syndrome–like reaction. The agitation here is an internal restlessness, not outward aggression.
  • Oxygen to maintain normal levels; IV fluids for dehydration and fever; symptomatic and supportive care.
  • For tachycardia or hypertension: esmolol or nitroprusside. For hypotension: phenylephrine or epinephrine.
  • If other treatments are not working, a serotonin-production blocker: cyproheptadine HCl.
  • Symptoms usually resolve within 24 hours, but can take several weeks to clear completely.

Tricyclic Antidepressants

imipramine · desipramine · doxepin · amitriptyline. Block the reuptake of norepinephrine and serotonin at the presynaptic neuron in the CNS, which is thought to elevate mood. Results take 10–14 days to appear and full effects 4–8 weeks; start low and go slow. Much less expensive than SRIs, and returning to use after several failed SSRI trials.

Deadly in overdose, through fatal CNS depression — and equally so combined with alcohol or other CNS depressants. The cardiovascular effects are the serious ones: dysrhythmias, tachycardia, MI, and heart block. Contraindicated after an MI or with other cardiovascular disease, in narrow-angle glaucoma, with any history of seizures, and in pregnancy.

Before and During Treatment

  • Check an ECG for cardiac disorder before initiating.
  • Check for a history of seizure disorder.
  • Alpha-adrenergic blockade produces orthostatic hypotension and tachycardia — dizziness and falls are the worry in older adults. TCA use is considered risky in older adults and in anyone with cardiac disease.
  • Often given at night; the sedation aids sleep, and the side effects fall during sleeping hours.
  • Warn about early morning awakening, feeling worse in the morning, worry and anxiety early in treatment — these ease as the day goes on.

Managing Anticholinergic Effects

  • Dry mouth: sugar-free hard candy or gum.
  • GI upset: take the medication with food.
  • Diarrhea: frequent small meals.
  • Constipation: increase fiber, fluids, and exercise.
  • Insomnia: sleep hygiene; change the dosing time — take in the morning if it causes insomnia.
  • Orthostasis: stay hydrated; get up slowly.
  • Sexual side effects: erectile dysfunction medications.
  • Urinary hesitancy: run water while voiding; measure the amount.
Anticholinergic side effects. Hot as a hare — increased temperature · Dry as a bone — dry mouth and eyes, urinary retention, constipation · Blind as a bat — mydriasis, dilated pupils, blurred vision · Red as a beet — flushed face · Mad as a hatter — confusion, delirium. Also sedation and weight gain. Usually not serious and often transitory, but urinary retention or severe constipation needs immediate medical attention.

MAOIs & Hypertensive Crisis

phenelzine · isocarboxazid · tranylcypromine · selegiline (Emsam), a transdermal patch. Block monoamine oxidase, the enzyme that metabolizes serotonin, norepinephrine, dopamine, and tyramine, so the mood-elevating neurotransmitters increase. Usually the third choice for depression; also used for panic disorder, social phobias, generalized anxiety disorders, and bulimia. Effective for unconventional depression — a depression that can lift with positive news or events, where the arms and legs feel heavy as lead, with oversleeping and overeating. The Emsam patch has far fewer dietary restrictions and drug interactions.

StageSigns of Hypertensive Crisis
EarlyIrritability, anxiety, flushing, sweating, and a severe headache.
EstablishedOccipital headache, palpitations, nausea and vomiting, hypertension but with orthostatic hypotension on standing, neck stiffness or soreness, dilated pupils, photophobia, blurred vision, chest pain, tachycardia or bradycardia, disturbed cardiac rate and rhythm, dyspnea, mental status changes.
Life-threateningSevere fever, seizures, then coma or death. Can also produce intracranial hemorrhage.

Prevention & Treatment

  • Monitor blood pressure routinely, especially during the first 6 weeks. Crisis occurs 15–90 minutes after ingesting a contraindicated substance.
  • Avoid Demerol, and be very careful with OTC cold medications.
  • Rarely used with an SSRI — the combination commonly causes serotonin syndrome.
  • Limit caffeine, chocolate, and avocados.
  • Treatment: phentolamine, an alpha-adrenergic blocker and vasodilator; sublingual nifedipine, a calcium channel blocker that relaxes cardiac muscle; plus symptomatic and supportive care.
Tyramine-rich foods to avoid. Aged, smoked, fermented, marinated, and processed meats (pepperoni, bologna, salami, pickled herring, liver, frankfurters, bacon, ham) · aged cheeses (blue, cheddar, provolone, brie, Romano, parmesan, swiss) · overripe fruits and vegetables (bananas, sauerkraut) · beans (fava, Italian, Chinese pea pod, tofu) · condiments (soy sauce, bouillon cubes, meat tenderizers) · beer, ales, liquors, red wine, and non-alcoholic wine or beer.

Antidepressants in Children & Teens

  • SSRIs carry a black box warning for increased risk of suicidality — suicidal thinking or attempts — in children and adolescents.
  • After the warning, prescribing fell and suicide attempts increased: the risk of suicide is greater in depressed children and adolescents who do not take antidepressants. Part of the reason is the same energy-before-mood gap — physiological symptoms improve in 1–2 weeks while mood lags behind.
  • fluoxetine is first-line; venlafaxine is used off label. Both have low side effects — mild nausea, headache, stomachache.
  • Full effect can take up to 12 weeks, and improvement is gradual enough that patients often only notice after one to two months. Usually given for 6–24 months.
  • TCAs are avoided: more side effects, potential for dysrhythmias, lethal in overdose, and children and adolescents can be more impulsive.

What is anhedonia?
Loss of interest or pleasure in almost all activities.
Define psychomotor retardation.
A visible slowing of physical activity — slow speech, long pauses, taking a long time to cross a room or to chew. Associated with severe depression.
Define psychomotor agitation.
An increase in activity brought on by mental tension: restlessness, pacing, tapping fingers or feet, abruptly starting and stopping tasks. Associated with agitated depression.
How many symptoms over what period are required for major depressive disorder, and which must be present?
Five or more in the same 2-week period, and at least one must be depressed mood or anhedonia.
Name the anticholinergic effects of a tricyclic antidepressant.
Dry mouth, blurred vision, constipation, and urinary retention — retention is the one to report promptly.
What is the first action when serotonin syndrome is suspected?
Stop or reduce the medications contributing to it, as directed by the prescriber.
What must a patient taking an MAOI avoid to prevent hypertensive crisis?
Tyramine-rich foods: aged, smoked, fermented, and processed meats; aged cheeses; overripe fruits and vegetables; certain beans; condiments; and beer, ale, liquor, and red wine — including non-alcoholic beer and wine.
Which two medications treat a hypertensive crisis?
Phentolamine, an alpha-adrenergic blocker and vasodilator, and sublingual nifedipine, a calcium channel blocker.
Why does suicide risk rise early in antidepressant treatment?
Physiological symptoms improve before psychological ones, so energy returns while mood is still depressed and the person can act on suicidal thoughts.
Which antidepressant is first-line for children and teens?
Fluoxetine.
Which antidepressant is contraindicated in eating disorders and any history of seizures?
Bupropion.
Analyzing cues on a newly admitted depressed patient, which one takes priority?
Safety — whether the patient has thoughts of self-harm. It is assessed before any other symptom.
