Anxiety & OCD Disorders

Anxiety, Fear and Anxiety Disorder

Anxiety is a universal human experience and the most basic of emotions: a feeling of apprehension, uneasiness, uncertainty or dread from a real or perceived threat, accompanied by an autonomic response. The source is often unknown, vague or conflictual, and it produces physical, emotional, cognitive and behavioral symptoms. It is the evolutionary "fight or flight" response.

Anxiety vs Fear

  • Fear: a reaction to a specific, immediate danger.
  • Anxiety: the stress response produced by thoughts alone, from a real or perceived threat.
  • Normal anxiety is necessary for survival.

Stress vs Anxiety

  • The two are not the same, though often used interchangeably.
  • Stress, or a stressor: an external pressure.
  • Anxiety: the subjective emotional response to that stressor.

Anxiety

Faced daily. Adaptive, and a motivation for survival.

Anxious Personality

Anxiety Disorder

Persistent anxiety that impairs functioning.

When Anxiety Becomes a Disorder

  • Anxious despite no real threat, or a threat that passed long ago still impairs functioning.
  • Adaptive coping replaced by maladaptive coping.
  • Greater intensity than expected for the context, and lasts longer than expected for the precipitating stress.
  • Flashbacks, obsessions or compulsions.
  • Anxiety curtails daily or social activities.

Theories of Anxiety Disorders

FactorWhat Contributes
GeneticAnxiety disorders cluster in families, and genetic variants associated with increased risk have been identified.
NeurobiologicalThe amygdala alerts the brain to danger and brings about fear or anxiety. Neurochemicals regulating anxiety: epinephrine, norepinephrine, dopamine, serotonin and GABA.
PsychologicalFreud: unconscious childhood conflicts, with anxiety as repressed ideas nearing the conscious mind. Sullivan: early needs going unmet or disapproval. Behavioral: anxiety is learned. Cognitive: caused by distortions in thoughts and perceptions.
CulturalSome cultures express anxiety through somatic symptoms, others through cognitive symptoms. Panic attacks in Latin Americans and northern Europeans often involve choking, smothering, numbness and fear of dying, and in other groups fear of magic or witchcraft. Social anxiety in Japanese and Korean cultures may center on one's blushing, eye contact or body odor offending others.
EnvironmentalExcess caffeine or tobacco, and prolonged exposure to a stressful event or abuse.

In mild and moderate anxiety, the anxiety can alert the person that something is wrong and prompt an action plan. Severe and panic levels both prevent problem-solving, and unproductive relief behaviors perpetuate a vicious cycle.

Intervening by Level

Mild to Moderate

  • Help the person identify the anxiety and anticipate what provokes it.
  • Nonverbal interest: eye contact, how you sit.
  • Encourage talking about and focus on their feelings and concerns. Clarify what is said.
  • Identify thoughts and feelings before the onset of anxiety.
  • Encourage problem-solving and alternative solutions.
  • Provide outlets for working off excess energy.
  • Prevent escalation: a calm presence, recognizing distress, willingness to listen, exploring what relieved anxiety in the past, and offering alternative coping strategies.

Severe to Panic

  • Provide safety in all areas, personal and environmental: warmth, fluids, elimination, pain relief, family.
  • Calm manner. Clear, simple statements, repeated if necessary. Low-pitched voice, speaking slowly.
  • Move to a quiet environment with minimal stimulation.
  • Reinforce reality if distortions such as delusions or hallucinations are present.
  • Assess the need for medication.

Severe and Panic Anxiety

Always remain with the individual experiencing severe or panic anxiety, and assess the risk for suicide.

The Anxiety Disorders

A group of conditions with persistent anxiety that cannot be dismissed. Coping mechanisms are ineffective, the anxiety interferes with activities of daily living, and a threat is perceived even when none exists. Every disorder's symptoms cluster around excessive, irrational fear and dread.

Anxiety Disorders

  • Generalized anxiety disorder (GAD)
  • Separation anxiety disorder
  • Social anxiety disorder, or social phobia
  • Phobias
  • Panic disorder and panic attack

Obsessive-Compulsive and Related Disorders

  • Obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD)
  • Body dysmorphic disorder
  • Hoarding disorder
  • Trichotillomania
  • Excoriation disorder
FeatureDetail
ScopeThe most common mental health problems, affecting about 1 in 5 U.S. adults. Women 2:1 over men. Highly treatable, yet only 36.9% receive treatment. Tend to be chronic and persistent.
Basic principlesStrong genetic predisposition. Symptoms often begin in childhood or early adulthood. The person often recognizes the thoughts and behaviors are irrational and the emotion an overreaction. Behaviors manage overwhelming anxiety, so allow the behavior to continue until other strategies are in place.

Separation Anxiety Disorder

Separation anxiety is normal in infancy: it begins around 8 months, peaks at 18 months, then declines. The disorder is developmentally inappropriate concern over being away from a significant other, with fear that something horrible will cause permanent separation. It interferes with normal activities and causes sleep disturbance, nightmares and physical symptoms. In adults it appears as harm avoidance, worry, shyness, uncertainty, lack of self-direction and impaired social and occupational functioning.

Generalized Anxiety Disorder

One of the most common mental health disorders: excessive, persistent worry about everyday circumstances that the person usually knows is out of proportion but cannot get rid of.

DSM-5 Criteria

  • Excessive worry and anxiety more days than not for at least 6 months, about several events or activities.
  • The worry is difficult to control.
  • At least 3 of 6 symptoms for at least 6 months, and only 1 in children: restlessness or feeling keyed up, being easily fatigued, difficulty concentrating or mind going blank, irritability, muscle tension, sleep disturbance.
  • Clinically significant distress or impairment in social, occupational or other functioning.
  • Not due to a substance, another medical condition or another mental disorder.
FeatureDetail
Epidemiology3% of adults and 1% of adolescents. Twice as many women as men. Genetics account for about one-third of the risk.
Risk factorsUnresolved conflicts, cognitive misinterpretations, life stressors, genetic predisposition, and behavioral inhibition: shyness, fear or withdrawal in unfamiliar situations.
ComorbidityMost common: major depressive disorder and other anxiety disorders. Alcoholism can be a significant problem. 66% have at least one concurrent psychiatric diagnosis.
Clinical courseChronic, with fluctuating severity. About half report onset in childhood or adolescence, though onset after 20 is also common. Often presents in primary care with somatic complaints: muscle aches, soreness, GI complaints. Also an exaggerated startle response, mild depressive symptoms and fear of imminent disaster.
Children and adolescentsThe expression is consistent across the lifespan, and the content of the worry differs. Adults worry about family well-being and their own health. Children worry about social acceptance, family, finances and their abilities. May be perfectionistic and seek excessive reassurance. Treated with CBT, antidepressants and anti-anxiety medication.

Treating Anxiety Disorders

A combination of pharmacotherapy and psychotherapy, and co-occurring conditions must also be treated.

Medication Classes

Classes Used

  • Antidepressants: SSRIs, SNRIs, tricyclics and MAOIs.
  • Anti-anxiety drugs: benzodiazepines.
  • Also: beta blockers, antihistamines, anticonvulsants and antipsychotics, often added if the first course is ineffective.

Considerations

  • SSRIs and SNRIs are first line in most anxiety disorders, because of safety risks with the others.
  • Assess for suicide risk.
  • Monitor 4 to 8 weeks for efficacy after starting. If it fails: increase the dose, switch drug or class, or add a second drug.

MAOIs (phenelzine, tranylcypromine, isocarboxazid) are used less often, typically for refractory and atypical depression. Tyramine-rich foods such as aged cheese, smoked meats, yeast and red wine cause a hypertensive crisis, and the class has significant drug interactions.

MAOIs and hypertensive crisis are taught in full in Depressive Disorders

Benzodiazepines

Used in the initial stages for their quick onset, hours not weeks. They enhance GABA, calming anxiety, promoting sleep and relaxing muscle, and they promote amnesia. They show no long-term effectiveness and carry a risk of dependence and abuse, so use them only for a short period, until other treatments reduce symptoms.

Benzodiazepine Safety

Ingestion with alcohol can be fatal. Never discontinue abruptly. Taper, because abrupt stopping triggers severe withdrawal and intense rebound anxiety. Teratogenic: avoid in pregnancy. Antidote: flumazenil.

Guidelines

  • Lowest dose that relieves symptoms.
  • Monitor sedation and injury risk, especially fall risk and at discharge. Caution with machinery.
  • Avoid caffeine, alcohol and other sedatives. Take with food.
  • Use cautiously with a history of substance use: high dependency potential.

Other Medications

Options for those who cannot tolerate SSRIs or cannot safely be prescribed benzodiazepines.

Does not tend to affect libido or sexual function, so it is often added to other antidepressants to counter theirs. Side effects: seizure, agitation, headache, dry mouth, constipation, weight loss, GI upset, dizziness, tremor.

Teaching About Antianxiety Medications

  • Do not change the dose or frequency without the provider's approval.
  • May make it unsafe to operate mechanical equipment.
  • Alcohol and other antianxiety drugs potentiate depressant effects. Caffeine and nicotine decrease the desired effect.
  • Avoid pregnancy on benzodiazepines (congenital anomalies). Drugs are excreted in breast milk.
  • MAOIs require a special diet. Stopping benzodiazepines or SSRIs may cause withdrawal.
  • Take after meals to avoid GI symptoms. Watch for interactions: antacids delay absorption, and Tagamet interferes with benzodiazepine metabolism.

Behavioral Therapies and Relaxation

Behavioral therapies decrease anxious or avoidant behavior: modeling, systematic desensitization, flooding and thought stopping. CBT aims for insight into what causes the anxiety, and its primary focus is identifying and changing faulty thinking.

Systematic Desensitization

  • Gradual introduction to the feared object or experience, in steps from least to most frightening.
  • A relaxation technique is used at each step when anxiety becomes overwhelming.

Flooding

  • Exposure to a large amount of the undesirable stimulus to extinguish the anxiety response.
  • Through prolonged exposure, the person learns that survival is possible and the anxiety diminishes.

Non-pharmacological relaxation: self-care activities, deep breathing, physical exercise, progressive muscle relaxation, mental imagery, meditation and biofeedback. For full benefit, practice relaxation daily.

Specific Phobias and Agoraphobia

A specific phobia is a persistent irrational fear of a specific object, activity or situation that almost always provokes immediate fear and is avoided. The fear is out of proportion to the actual danger, and usually recognized as such. Phobias are always anticipated, never unexpected, and how disabling one is depends on how central it is in the person's life.

Medications have not proven successful as the only treatment. The treatment of choice is psychotherapy, systematic desensitization or flooding, and a combination of medication with those therapies.

AgoraphobiaDetail
DefinitionThe most severe and persistent phobic disorder. Intense fear of places or situations where escape might be difficult or embarrassing, or help might not be available. The places are avoided to control the anxiety.
Commonly avoidedBeing alone outside or at home, traveling by car, bus or airplane, being on a bridge, riding an elevator, being in a crowd.
EpidemiologyNearly 2% of adolescents and adults. Usually begins in late adolescence or early adulthood. Female to male 2:1.
Associated withAdverse childhood events, stressful life events, overprotective and emotionally cool families, and a genetic component.
TreatmentSSRIs, benzodiazepines, systematic desensitization, flooding and CBT.

Social Anxiety and Panic Disorder

Social Anxiety Disorder, or Social Phobia

Severe anxiety or fear provoked by a social or performance situation that could be evaluated negatively: intense fear of criticism, humiliation and embarrassment, and of being watched and judged. Fear of public speaking is the most common manifestation. The person may worry for days or weeks beforehand and withdraw or endure intense discomfort. Physical symptoms: blushing, profuse sweating, trembling, nausea, difficulty talking.

FeatureDetail
EpidemiologyWomen and men equally likely. Usually begins in childhood or early adolescence.
Risk factorsChildhood mistreatment and adverse childhood events, shyness, and shy parents, through genetics and modeling.
ConsequencesCan interfere with work, school and friendships. Increased risk of major depressive disorder and substance use disorders, often through self-medication.
TreatmentSSRIs and benzodiazepines.

Panic Disorder

A chronic condition with several exacerbations, characterized by panic attacks: the sudden onset of extreme apprehension or fear with impending doom. Attacks come "out of the blue," last minutes and subside. The terror suspends normal functioning, severely limits the perceptual field and may distort reality.

During an Attack

  • Chest pain, palpitations, pounding or racing heart, sweating, trembling.
  • Shortness of breath, smothering, choking.
  • Nausea, dizziness or faintness, chills or heat.
  • Paresthesia: numbness or tingling.
  • Derealization or depersonalization.
  • Fear of losing control, "going crazy," or dying.

Two Psychological Symptoms

  • Anticipatory anxiety: fearful expectation of the onset of panic.
  • Avoidance anxiety: personal strategies to increase feelings of control and decrease the risk of panic.

The person comes to fear the fear, avoiding pleasurable activities and obligations.

FeatureDetail
EtiologyGenetics and family history. Early childhood stress is linked to adult-onset anxiety disorders. Alcohol or substance use is common, to self-medicate.
EpidemiologyHighly associated with depression, hypertension, cigarette smoking and marijuana use. Women are more likely to have panic disorder with agoraphobia.
TreatmentCBT is first line, reducing fearful thinking and the stress response. Also distraction, positive self-talk, flooding and relaxation. Antidepressants: SSRIs, SNRIs, TCAs, MAOIs. Benzodiazepines are second line.

Emergency Care During a Panic Attack

The person is in crisis. First rule out a life-threatening illness, especially a cardiac event. Stay with the patient and reassure them you will not leave. Give clear directions, move to an environment with minimal stimulation, walk with the patient, and give PRN antianxiety medication.

Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder

Obsessions

Unwanted, intrusive, persistent thoughts, impulses or images that recur and cannot be dismissed despite trying. Often senseless to the person and inconsistent with their self-perception, causing extreme distress.

Examples: fear of contamination, need for symmetry, thoughts of hurting someone.

Compulsions

Behaviors performed repeatedly, in a ritualistic fashion, to prevent or relieve the anxiety the obsessions cause. Relief is only temporary, so the act must be repeated.

Examples: hand washing, touching things in sequence, counting, locking and unlocking doors.

Healthy people have rituals too. In OCD they interfere with daily life and the repetition is distressing. Trying to ignore the obsessions only increases distress, driving more compulsions: the vicious cycle of OCD. Most adults recognize the behavior is senseless, but some adults and most children do not.

FeatureDetail
Clinical pictureGradual onset. Obsessions and compulsions are time-consuming or cause significant distress or impairment. Humiliation or shame about the behaviors. Cognition may be impaired. Stress increases symptoms.
Risk and comorbidityChildhood sexual or physical abuse or trauma increases the risk. Strong genetic association. Occurs with anxiety disorders 76% of the time.
EpidemiologyExists along a continuum. 12-month prevalence 1.2%. Onset early 20s to mid-30s. Females more than males, but males more often as children, mostly with obsessions. Females have more checking and cleaning rituals.

Treating OCD

Very difficult to treat. Treatment may not cure, but it keeps symptoms from ruling the patient's life. The two main treatments are psychotherapy and medication, most effective combined.

TreatmentDetail
AntidepressantsTried first. FDA-approved for OCD: clomipramine, fluoxetine, fluvoxamine, paroxetine, sertraline.
CBT and exposureExposure and response prevention, a component of CBT: gradual exposure to the feared object or obsession, such as dirt, while learning to resist the compulsive ritual.
Deep brain stimulationFDA-approved for adults 18 and older who do not respond to traditional treatment. Implanted electrodes.
Transcranial magnetic stimulationFDA-approved for adults 22 to 68 when traditional treatment fails. Non-invasive magnetic stimulation.

OCD in Children and Adolescents

  • Unwanted thoughts and the behaviors they drive happen frequently, take more than an hour a day, or interfere with activities.
  • The behavior is done to prevent bad things or feel better, but is not connected to an actual danger, or is extreme.
  • One-third of affected adults report onset in childhood.
  • Medications: clomipramine, fluvoxamine, fluoxetine, sertraline.
  • Behavior therapy: exposure and response prevention. CBT for adolescents only, because of cognitive level.
