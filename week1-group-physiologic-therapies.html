Group & Physiologic Therapies

Group Dynamics

Group work: individuals with a common purpose come together and benefit by mutually giving and receiving feedback. A group is two or more people pursuing common goals or interests, so patient and family education counts as leading a group.

Characteristics of a Group

  • Size
  • Defined purpose
  • Degree of similarity: members share the problem the group is built around, even when the similarity isn't visible.
  • Rules: one person speaks at a time; no phone calls.
  • Boundaries
  • Content and process: what is talked about, and what goes on inside the group.

Who Can Lead What

  • A registered nurse: activity, educational, task, and support groups.
  • Advanced degree required: group psychotherapy, aimed at bringing about personality change.

Types of Groups

Psychoeducational groups increase knowledge about a specific psychological or somatic subject. The group types and Yalom's factors are also the categories the peer support paper has to cover.

TypeExample
Medication or health educationSymptom management or disease education, such as a diabetes group on a med-surg floor.
Dual diagnosisBoth a substance use disorder and another mental health disorder, such as major depressive disorder.
Symptom managementManaging anxiety; stress management.
Recreational activityPing pong, the art room, cards. Not recreational therapy, which requires a recreational therapist or someone with an advanced degree.
Interpersonal skillsEnhances knowledge and improves skills so clients can solve their problems.

Therapeutic Community Meeting

  • Essential group on the inpatient unit, usually in the morning.
  • Covers disruptions and disagreements on the unit and reinforces the rules: ten minutes at a time on the phone, because others need it.
  • Where the unit works out how to make things better.

Support / Self-Help Groups

  • Very structured.
  • Provide the opportunity to maintain or enhance personal and social functioning through cooperation and shared understanding of life's challenges.
  • AA, cancer survivors, bereavement, substance abuse, surviving suicide. Can be very empowering.

The therapeutic milieu and its five components are taught in Introduction to Psychiatric Mental Health Nursing

Yalom's Therapeutic Factors

Yalom identified 11 factors that make group therapy work.

FactorWhat It Is
Instillation of hopeLeader and members share optimism about improvement: "you got better, so maybe I can too."
UniversalityClients realize they are not alone: "I'm not the only one who hears voices," "I'm not the only one with thoughts of suicide."
Imparting of informationParticipants receive formal teaching from the leader, or advice from peers.
AltruismMembers gain from giving support to others, which improves self-value; doing it for someone else, not for yourself.
Corrective recapitulationMembers repeat behavior patterns learned in their families, get feedback, and work out how to correct the unhealthy ones.
Development of socializing techniquesNew social skills learned from feedback and modeling; the group is a safe place to try them.
Imitative behaviorMembers copy behavior from leaders or peers and adopt healthier habits.
Interpersonal learningInsight into themselves from others' feedback, with the choice to change. Happens in later phases, in an established group.
Group cohesivenessIn a mature group, each member feels connected to the others, the leader, and the group as a whole, and can accept both positive feedback and constructive criticism.
CatharsisA therapeutic discharge of emotion through experiencing and expressing feelings: "this has really helped me get in touch with my sadness."
Existential resolutionMembers examine what affects everyone (loneliness, mortality, responsibility) and construct meaning.

Advantages & Disadvantages of Group Therapy

Advantages

  • Engages multiple clients in treatment at the same time, which is why group is the main treatment on the units.
  • Participants benefit from the feedback, knowledge, and life experience of others.
  • A safe setting to try out new behaviors and communication skills.
  • Promotes a feeling of belonging.

Disadvantages

  • Time constraints: a unit group runs about 45 minutes; with 15 clients, participation time feels short, especially if someone monopolizes it.
  • Confidentiality cannot be guaranteed: no promise that every member keeps what is said in the group.
  • Disruptive members. Someone acutely psychotic, manic, or intoxicated cannot sit through a group or interact effectively and does not benefit from group treatment; leaders will try to get them out of it.

Group Leadership & Member Roles

Leadership Styles

StyleHow the Leader Behaves
AutocraticExerts control, runs the group, and does not encourage much interaction between members, as in an educational group heavy on information.
DemocraticSupports extensive group interaction in the process of problem-solving: psychotherapy groups, and AA.
Laissez-faireAllows members to behave however they choose and does not control the group's direction; starts it and lets it go where it goes. AA groups are usually democratic or laissez-faire.

Oriented toward the group's function: they strengthen, regulate, and keep it going.

Individual Roles

Meet the need of only one member and hamper rather than enhance group functioning.

RoleWhat They Do
AggressorCriticizes or attacks others' ideas or feelings.
BlockerOppositional: resists progress; argues or disagrees beyond reason.
Recognition seekerCalls attention to themselves; boasts about their achievements.
Play personHorses around, plays on their phone, tells jokes.
DominatorAsserts authority and superiority; manipulates or interrupts others, sometimes with flattery.

Handling Challenging Members

Challenging MemberWhat the Leader Does
Monopolizing memberAddress it as the group goes: everyone has ten minutes, or an equal chance to contribute. Sometimes take the member aside privately after the meeting.
Demoralizing memberListen objectively, then ask in private what is going on with their anger. Be matter-of-fact.
Silent memberDetermine the cause. Provide extra time to think about a response and come back to them.
The member who needs extra timeSlowed psychomotor functioning in severe depression means a longer wait for an answer. Silence is not always resistance; offer to come back to them at the end of group.

Phototherapy (Light Therapy)

ElementDetail
The deviceA fluorescent light box with a strong UV filter, about a 10,000 lux light source.
How it is usedSet it off to the side: do not look directly into it. 30 to 45 minutes a day, two to three times a week, usually in the morning, most often during fall and winter months.
IndicationsDepression occurring during pregnancy, so medications can be avoided; and major depressive disorder with a seasonal pattern, where it is first-line treatment.
Terminology changeSeasonal affective disorder (SAD) was removed in DSM-5 and reclassified as major depressive disorder with seasonal onset / seasonal pattern.
MechanismEfficacy is credited to the influence of light on melatonin; it stimulates the pineal gland, increasing melatonin and serotonin.
How well it worksAs effective as medication for SAD.
Side effectsHeadache and jitteriness; also eye strain, and rarely nausea, sweating, or sedation. Most people have none at all.
Light therapy can trigger mania or hypomania. Before prescribing it for depression, investigate thoroughly to make sure the diagnosis is not actually bipolar disorder. Otherwise it is very safe.

Electroconvulsive Therapy (ECT)

ECT is the most effective treatment for depression, and it is non-invasive.

Indications

  • Severe long-term depression: primary treatment with malnutrition, exhaustion, or dehydration. Rehydrate before ECT.
  • Certain medical conditions: safer than medications in some conditions; avoids polypharmacy in the elderly.
  • Delusional depression: works very well.
  • Psychotic illnesses: the second most common indication for ECT.
  • Refractory depression: has not responded to multiple medication trials, or keeps recurring despite treatment. Not usually first-line; ECT follows failed trials.
  • Catatonia: works especially well in schizophrenia with catatonia.

The Procedure

  • Anesthesia: done under anesthesia with a muscle relaxant.
  • Electrode placement: electricity applied to the temporal regions — unilateral (one side) or bilateral (an electrode on each temple).
  • Course: two to three treatments a week, for a total of 6 to 12 treatments.
  • The stimulus: an electrical current lasting 1 second or less.
  • The seizure: a brief seizure of 30 to 60 seconds, monitored throughout. One extremity is left un-paralyzed so the convulsion can be observed.
  • With or without medication: can be used either way; sometimes it helps boost SSRIs. Improves energy, appetite, sleep, and mood.
  • How it works: not exactly known. Theories: alters neurotransmitter receptors and can increase serotonin; regulates stress hormones; increases cerebral blood flow, possibly neurogenesis.
Cardiac: the heart is heavily stressed at the onset of the seizure and for up to ten minutes after; assess and manage in hypertension, congestive heart failure, cardiac arrhythmias, and other cardiac conditions.

Neurologic: the brain is stressed by increased cerebral oxygen, blood flow, and intracranial pressure, so ECT is contraindicated in brain tumors and subdural hematomas.
After the TreatmentNursing Implication
WakingAbout 15 minutes after the procedure.
Confusion and disorientationVery common, lasting several hours. Reorienting afterward is part of the procedure, not just a reaction to it; tell the family of an outpatient they may need to do the same.
Retrograde amnesiaLoss of the events leading up to and including the treatment itself, usually just the morning before and shortly after. Most patients experience it.

TMS, Vagus Nerve Stimulation & Deep Brain Stimulation

Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation (TMS): Non-Invasive

ElementDetail
What it isMRI-strength magnetic pulses that stimulate focal areas of the cerebral cortex. FDA approved for treatment-resistant depression.
The courseOutpatient, 30 minutes, five days a week for four to six weeks.
What happensPatient is awake and alert. An electromagnet on the scalp sends short pulses through the prefrontal cortex, inducing neurons to fire. Feels like a slight tapping, sometimes with scalp contractions and jaw tightening.
Safety profileNo neurological deficits, no memory problems, no loss of consciousness; seizures rarely. Most reactions mild: scalp tingling and discomfort at the site. Patients can continue their medications.
Adverse reactionsHeadache and lightheadedness.
The one contraindication for TMS is metal in the area of stimulation: brain stimulators, cochlear implants, medication pumps, bullet fragments. Also not used in anyone with a pacemaker or a history of seizures.

Vagus Nerve Stimulation (VNS): Invasive

ElementDetail
OriginOriginally for epilepsy; it decreases seizures and improves mood, so it is FDA approved for partial onset seizures and depression.
The deviceA pulse generator the size of a pacemaker in the chest, wired up and wrapped around the vagus nerve in the neck.
How it firesStimulates the left cervical vagus nerve, pulsing 30 seconds every 5 minutes, 24 hours a day.
MechanismBoosts neurotransmitter levels by activating the limbic system.
Used forInvasive, so generally reserved for treatment-resistant depression.
Side effectsVoice alteration in nearly 60% of patients, plus hoarseness, headache, neck pain, cough, paresthesia, and dyspnea, all decreasing with time. Implanting a device also carries infection risk.

Deep Brain Stimulation (DBS): Most Invasive

  • Very seldom used. Electrodes implanted in the chest, threaded up the neck and into the scalp, with probes placed into the brain.
  • A treatment for Parkinson's disease, now investigated in depression, stimulating regions identified as underactive in depression.
  • Risk is not only chest infection; something is placed inside the brain.

Exercise & Herbal Treatments

Exercise

  • Combats the harmful effects of stress, anxiety, and depression; any exercise is good.
  • Regular exercisers with depression and anxiety report elevated mood and greater happiness.
  • Alters serotonin, dopamine, and norepinephrine, and dampens the HPA axis, thought to be overly active in depression.
  • Has biological, social, and psychological effects.
  • Less expensive than ECT, fewer side effects than medication.

Herbal treatments are one of the seven key terms to know.

HerbUsed For
St. John's WortMild-to-moderate depression. A flower processed into tea or tablets, thought to increase serotonin, norepinephrine, and dopamine.
GinsengStress or fatigue.
Valerian root · ChamomileDepression, insomnia, stress, or anxiety.
SAM-eDepression.
Herbals are not regulated by the FDA. The concentration of active ingredients varies between brands and formulas, and much more research is needed.
