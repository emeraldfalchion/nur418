Key Terms & Learning Outcomes

Introduction to PMH Nursing

Key Terms

TermDefinition
Mental health (WHO)Well-being in which the individual can realize their own potential, cope with the normal stresses of life, work productively, and contribute to the community.
Mental illness (APA)Health conditions involving changes in emotion, thinking, or behavior, associated with distress and/or problems functioning in social, work, or family activities.
Diathesis-stress modelThe most accepted explanation for mental illness. Diathesis = a biological predisposition; stress = any emotional or environmental stress or trauma. Many carry the predisposition. It becomes illness only when a certain stress occurs.
ResilienceThe ability and capacity to secure the resources needed to support personal well-being: optimism, competence, and a sense of mastery over obstacles. Determines the incidence, severity, and prognosis of mental illness.
RecoveryA process of change through which an individual improves their health and wellness, lives a self-directed life, and reaches their full potential. Possible even where cure is not. The recovery model emphasizes hope, self-determination, and quality of life.
ComorbidityTwo co-occurring chronic illnesses: two mental health disorders (depression and anxiety), or a mental and a medical one (depression and diabetes).
Dual diagnosisA co-occurring mental illness and substance use disorder. One of the two must be a substance use disorder. That is what separates it from comorbidity.
Adherence (not compliance)Following the plan of care, and the term that replaced compliance. "Compliance" carries a strongly negative connotation, so the language change is itself a way of breaking down stigma.
DeinstitutionalizationThe mass movement of severely mentally ill people from state hospitals to outpatient care, closing many hospitals. Accelerated by Kennedy's 1963 Community Mental Health Act.
Revolving-door treatmentRepeated ED visits and inpatient admissions. Began in the 1960s: psychotropic drug development plus deinstitutionalization into communities that lacked services, leaving patients without the skills to function independently.
AssaultA threat to touch another person without consent. A verbal threat is enough. No contact has happened yet.
BatteryThe actual harmful touching of another person: giving an injection to a patient who has refused the oral form. Contact is what separates it from assault.
NegligenceFailing to provide correct and adequate treatment.
False imprisonmentRestraining or confining a patient without cause.
Mental Health ParityInsurers must give equal coverage for psychiatric illness as for physical health: deductibles, number of covered visits, and out-of-pocket expense. Also covers substance-related disorders (1996).
Acute care hospitalizationHighly structured, 24-hour locked setting that optimizes safety and addresses crisis intervention. Average stay 3–7 days, and about 3–5 days on an adult unit.
Long-term hospitalizationWhere the client is referred if they need more than 7 days to stabilize.
Partial hospitalization (PHP)Transitional step between inpatient and fully independent home life, for people needing some supervision but not hospitalization. About 6 hours of treatment, multiple days a week.
Day treatmentOne step below PHP: structured activities and group for ongoing, usually chronic mental illness, so family members can work. 2–3 times a week for about a month.

Learning Outcomes

  • The continuum: well-being → emotional problems or concerns → mental illness. People move along it in both directions.
  • What contributes to mental health: biologic, psychological, and sociocultural influences. Characteristics include rational thinking, resiliency, self-esteem, self-awareness, emotional growth, and self-care.
  • Stigma: seeing someone in a negative way because of a characteristic, attribute, or personal trait. It worsens the course and outcomes of mental illness.
  • Counter stigma by talking openly about mental health, educating yourself and others, using person-first language, decriminalizing, pushing back on media portrayals, and advocating for reform.
  • Barriers to care: too few providers, transportation, two-to-three-month waits for outpatient follow-up, low income and cost, the limits of insurance parity, and stigma itself.
  • Admission types: voluntary: the client signs themselves in and consents. Involuntary: admitted against their will on a 72-hour hold, not counting weekends and holidays. Form 202A is filed by the APRN or MD for longer commitment.
  • Admission criteria for an acute unit: danger to self, danger to others, or unable to care for basic needs and/or gross impairment of judgment.
  • Least restrictive means, in order: assess → verbally intervene → reduce stimulation → provide diversion → PRN by mouth → PRN IM → escort to seclusion → restraints last.
  • Chemical restraint is less restrictive than physical or mechanical restraint, and PO is always offered before IM.
  • The therapeutic milieu, five components: containment, support, validation, structure, involvement.
  • Consumers is the term for individuals who have mental illness. The consumer movement demanded involvement in their own treatment decisions. NAMI (1979) is the largest grassroots organization, and advocates person-first language: not "a schizophrenic."
  • Patient rights: to have or refuse visitors, to receive or refuse treatment, and rights regarding psychiatric advance directives. Informed consent means risks, benefits, and alternatives were given and treatment voluntarily accepted.
  • Capacity versus competency: capacity is the ability to make an informed decision. Competency is the legal term for the mental soundness required to decide or act.
  • Confidentiality breaks in exactly two circumstances: a specific threat to kill someone (duty to warn and protect), and suspected child or elder abuse.
The five components of the therapeutic milieu, "Contain Some Very Stubborn Individuals": Containment, Support, Validation, Structure, Involvement.

Neurobiology & Pharmacology

Key Terms

TermDefinition
★ The eight neurotransmittersInhibitory: serotonin, GABA. Excitatory: dopamine, glutamate, epinephrine, norepinephrine. Other: acetylcholine, histamine.
AmygdalaThe emotional center of the brain: emotion, anxiety, fear, and pleasure. Part of the fight-or-flight system, alerting us to danger.
★ NeuroplasticityThe ability of the brain to change with learning by reorganizing neural pathways. Why repetition of a skill or a behavior change matters.
Synaptic pruningThe process that removes unnecessary or damaged neuronal structures, weeding out weaker, seldom-used synapses. Surges prenatally through age two, in the preteen years, and at midlife.
Discontinuation syndromeA sudden decrease or stop of certain psychotropics. The original symptoms rebound, and new physical or psychological symptoms appear. Antidepressants are not addicting, but the body does get used to them.
PsychopharmacologyStart low and titrate up slowly · start only one new medication at a time · use the lowest effective dose · weigh risks against benefits · hold or switch for significant side effects · taper, never stop abruptly.
The eight neurotransmitters, "Silent Guards Inhibit; Dangerous Gangs Excite Neighborhoods": Serotonin and GABA inhibit. Dopamine, Glutamate, Epinephrine and Norepinephrine excite. The two left over are the "other" pair: acetylcholine and histamine don't pick a side.

Learning Outcomes

  • ★ Five structures: thalamus, hypothalamus, cerebellum, brainstem, amygdala.
  • Thalamus: the main relay station of the CNS. Hypothalamus: temperature, appetite, sleep, sex drive, heart rate, blood pressure.
  • Hippocampus: long- and short-term memory. Heavily damaged in chronic alcoholism, leading to Korsakoff syndrome and Wernicke's.
  • Illusion versus hallucination: in an illusion something really is there but is misperceived. In a hallucination nothing is.
  • Serotonin is decreased in depressive and anxiety disorders. Blocking its reuptake treats both very successfully.
  • GABA is the major inhibitory neurotransmitter in the CNS. Increase GABA to decrease anxiety. Decreased GABA: anxiety, mania, and schizophrenia.
  • Dopamine in excess produces auditory hallucinations and plays a role in schizophrenia. Glutamate is decreased in psychosis, the opposite pattern from dopamine.
  • Excess glutamate over-stimulates the brain and causes seizures. Excessive release leads to excitotoxicity and cell death.
  • Acetylcholine is decreased in Alzheimer's and sleep disorders, increased in depression.
  • Norepinephrine is decreased in depression, increased in mania, anxiety, and schizophrenia. Epinephrine works with it in fight-or-flight.
  • Imaging: structural (CT, MRI) shows anatomy. Functional (PET, SPECT, fMRI) shows activity and physiological change.
  • Over-pruning is one biological theory of schizophrenia, timed to the pruning surge in late adolescence and young adulthood.
  • Plasticity in children: young brains are more receptive to environmental input than old ones, so intervention succeeds more readily the younger the child. Rich sensory experiences and protective factors produce fewer maladaptive responses. Decreasing risk factors and increasing protective factors promotes resilience. Experiences a child is not developmentally ready for can have unintended consequences.

Anger, Aggression & Violence

Key Terms

TermDefinition
AngerAn emotional response to frustration of desires, a threat to one's needs, or a challenge, from mild irritation to intense fury. A normal emotion, capable of being under personal control. A problem only when expressed aggressively.
AggressionVerbal or physical attack intended to threaten or injure the victim's security or self-esteem. Virtually always designed to punish, but not always inappropriate, and sometimes necessary for self-protection.
ViolenceIntentional use of force that results in, or could result in, injury to another. Always an objectionable act. That is what separates it from aggression.
RestraintAny manual method, physical or mechanical device, material, or equipment that immobilizes or reduces a patient's ability to move arms, legs, body, or head freely.
SeclusionInvoluntary confinement of a patient alone in a room they are physically prevented from leaving. Less restrictive than restraints and never punitive. Only for violent or self-destructive behavior that jeopardizes immediate physical safety.
★ Critical incident debriefingImmediate and mandatory after a restraint episode, with patient and staff.

Learning Outcomes

  • Four predictors of violence: history of violence, recent acts of violence, using profanity, hyperactivity.
  • Of those four: hyperactivity is the most important predictor of imminent violence. A history of violence is the single best predictor of future violence.
  • Precipitants: anxiety (catch it early), withdrawal and substance use, sleep deprivation, pain, feeling out of control, and poor communication.
  • Warning signs: pacing and restlessness, clenched jaws or fists, changes in speech rate or volume, verbal abuse, glaring or wholly avoided eye contact, and uncharacteristic withdrawal.
  • Aggression occurs most often in the context of limit setting.
  • The four levels: anxiety → loss of rationality (defensive) → hostility, abuse or aggression → tension reduction, the reachable and teachable moment.
  • De-escalation: speak slowly in shortened sentences with a low, calm voice. Offer two options to reduce powerlessness. Give feedback on what you see. Never yell.
  • Nurse safety: stay about one foot further than the patient can reach. Keep an escape route and never let the patient between you and the door. No dangling jewellery. Only one person talks.
  • Restraint hard rules: never prone, never a PRN or standing order, order and face-to-face assessment within one hour, two fingers under the restraint.
  • What silently counts as a restraint: four side rails up (except seizure precautions), sheets tucked so tightly the patient cannot move, holding someone against their will.
  • Seclusion is tried before restraint. Both are a last resort, only when the patient is a danger to self or others and less restrictive interventions have been found ineffective.
  • A restrained patient is never left alone. Remove restraints and do range of motion on one limb at a time, and always place a wedge pillow under the head of anyone restrained in bed.
  • Six contraindications to seclusion and restraint: extremely unstable medical or psychiatric conditions, COPD, spinal injury, seizure disorders, pregnancy, delirium or dementia.
The six contraindications, "Pregnant Patients Should Simply Delay Cuffs": Pregnancy, Psychiatric or medical instability, Spinal injury, Seizure disorders, Delirium or dementia, COPD.

Assessment, Theories & Therapies

Key Terms

TermDefinition
★ The five behavioral therapiesModeling = role play · operant conditioning = tokens and positive reinforcement · systematic desensitization = relaxation plus graded exposure · aversion therapy = negative stimulus · biofeedback = control of involuntary body responses.
Interpersonal therapy (IPT)Targets relationships by improving functioning and communication patterns. Very short-term: 12 to 16 sessions.
Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT)Our thoughts cause our feelings and behaviors, not external things like people or situations. Change the way we think and we change the way we feel, and act better even if the situation does not change. In session: the therapist is active and questioning, homework is assigned and reviewed at the next session, and the goal is increased objectivity about the thoughts governing behavior, never increased subjectivity, which reinforces emotionally driven thinking.
Dialectical behavior therapy (DBT)The integration of opposites: cognitive and behavioral techniques combined with mindfulness. Developed to treat chronically suicidal individuals, many of whom had borderline personality disorder.
The five behavioral therapies, "My Old Son Ate Bugs": Modeling, Operant conditioning, Systematic desensitization, Aversion therapy, Biofeedback.

Learning Outcomes

  • Purpose of the PMH assessment: establish rapport, understand the current problem, review physical status, assess risk and safety, assess psychosocial status, identify mutual goals, formulate the plan of care.
  • Depressed mood obliges a suicide assessment. Ask directly: "Are you depressed?" and then "Do you feel that you might hurt yourself?"
  • Affect versus mood: affect is what you observe. Mood is the emotional state in the person's own words.
  • Affect range, most to least expressive: full/broad, constricted, blunted, flat. Flat resembles the masking seen in Parkinson's.
  • Affect congruency: congruent when mood and affect match. Incongruent when they do not: "I'm doing great," said while crying.
  • Mood terms: euthymic (normal), dysthymic (chronically low), elated or euphoric, labile (up and down), cyclothymic (cycling).
  • Psychosocial interventions are non-pharmacological techniques addressing the societal, familial, and cultural aspects: education, positive self-talk, self-monitoring, limit setting, relaxation, spiritual support, and discharge planning.
  • The three IPT techniques: identification of emotion, expression of emotion, and dealing with emotional baggage carried from past relationships.
  • Aversion therapy example: disulfiram (Antabuse) in alcohol dependence: drinking on it causes nausea, dizziness, and flushing.
  • DBT's four skill sets: emotional regulation, interpersonal effectiveness, distress tolerance, mindfulness.
  • DBT's four modes of treatment: group skills training, individual psychotherapy, telephone contact, and therapist consultation. It takes one to two years before the best results show.

Group & Physiologic Therapies

Key Terms

TermDefinition
GroupTwo or more people pursuing common goals or interests, benefiting by mutually giving and receiving feedback. Patient and family education counts as leading a group.
Phototherapy (light therapy)A fluorescent light box with a strong UV filter, about 10,000 lux, set off to the side. 30 to 45 minutes a day, usually in the morning. First-line for major depressive disorder with a seasonal pattern.
Electroconvulsive therapy (ECT)The most effective treatment for depression, and non-invasive. Done under anesthesia with a muscle relaxant. Two to three treatments a week, 6 to 12 in total.
Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS)Non-invasive MRI-strength magnetic pulses to focal areas of the cerebral cortex. FDA approved for treatment-resistant depression. Outpatient, 30 minutes, five days a week for four to six weeks. The patient stays awake and alert throughout, may have a headache and lightheadedness afterward, has no neurological deficits or memory problems, and can care for children the same day.
Vagus nerve stimulation (VNS)Invasive: a pulse generator in the chest wired around the left cervical vagus nerve, pulsing 30 seconds every 5 minutes. FDA approved for partial onset seizures and depression.
Deep brain stimulation (DBS)The most invasive and very seldom used: electrodes implanted in the chest, threaded up the neck, with probes placed into the brain. A Parkinson's treatment now investigated in depression.
Herbal treatmentsNot regulated by the FDA, and concentrations vary between brands. St. John's Wort for mild-to-moderate depression · ginseng for stress or fatigue · valerian root and chamomile · SAM-e.
ExerciseAlters serotonin, dopamine, and norepinephrine, and dampens the HPA axis. Less expensive than ECT, with fewer side effects than medication.

Learning Outcomes

  • ★ The seven physiological treatments: phototherapy, ECT, VNS, TMS, DBS, herbal treatments, exercise.
  • ★ Task roles: initiator-contributor, information seeker, information giver, coordinator, orienteer, recorder. Building and maintenance roles: encourager, harmonizer, gatekeeper, group observer, follower. Individual roles are the non-productive ones: aggressor, blocker, recognition seeker, play person, dominator.
  • Group characteristics: size, defined purpose, degree of similarity among members, rules, boundaries, and content and process.
  • Who can lead what: an RN may lead activity, educational, task, and support groups. Group psychotherapy requires an advanced degree, because it aims at personality change.
  • Psychoeducational groups teach content: medication or health education, dual diagnosis, symptom management, recreational activity, interpersonal skills. The therapeutic community meeting is the essential inpatient group.
  • Advantages of group therapy: engages several clients at once, members benefit from others' feedback and life experience, a safe setting to try new behaviors, and promotes belonging.
  • Disadvantages: time constraints, confidentiality cannot be guaranteed, and disruptive members. Someone acutely psychotic, manic, or intoxicated does not benefit from group.
  • Leadership styles: autocratic (leader exerts control, little interaction), democratic (extensive interaction in problem-solving), laissez-faire (members set the direction).
  • Rule out bipolar disorder before starting light therapy. It can trigger mania or hypomania.
  • ECT: an electrical current lasting 1 second or less produces a brief seizure of 30 to 60 seconds. One extremity is left un-paralyzed so the convulsion can be observed.
  • ECT is contraindicated in brain tumors and subdural hematoma. The heart is heavily stressed at the onset of the seizure and for up to ten minutes after.
  • After ECT: wakes about 15 minutes later, confusion and disorientation for several hours, and retrograde amnesia for the events leading up to and including the treatment.
  • TMS's one contraindication is metal in the area of stimulation: brain stimulators, cochlear implants, medication pumps, bullet fragments. Also not used with a pacemaker or a history of seizures.
  • VNS side effects: voice alteration in nearly 60%, plus hoarseness, headache, neck pain, cough, paresthesia, and dyspnea, all decreasing with time.

Therapeutic Communication

Key Terms

TermDefinition
ReflectingDirecting ideas, feelings, questions, or content back to the patient. Encourages them to accept their own ideas and feelings, acknowledges their right to hold opinions and make decisions, and encourages them to think of themselves as capable. Patient: "My brother spends all of my money and then wants more." Nurse: "You feel angry when this happens?"
Seeking clarificationHelping patients clarify their own thoughts and maximizing mutual understanding between nurse and patient. "I'm not sure I understood that," or "What would you say is the main point of what you just said?"

Learning Outcomes

  • The therapeutic techniques: listening, maintaining silence, maintaining neutral responses, broad openings and open-ended questions, focusing and refocusing, restating, clarifying and validating, sharing perceptions, reflecting, providing acknowledgment and feedback, giving information, presenting reality, encouraging formulation of a plan of action, providing nonverbal encouragement, summarizing.
  • The barriers: giving advice, which terminates the conversation and fosters dependency by implying the patient cannot decide for themselves · giving approval or disapproval · asking "why," which is heard as accusatory · any judgmental statement · passing responsibility to another provider · confronting the underlying cause.
  • The most therapeutic response focuses on feelings and encourages the widest range of patient responses, making the patient an active participant in the conversation.
  • To begin re-socialization, start with the patient's own interests: what they enjoyed in the past. Transportation, money, and who they would go with are relevant, but they come after.

Depressive Disorders

Key Terms

TermDefinition
LabilityRapid, exaggerated shifts in emotional expression: the mood switches back and forth, typically every one or two hours.
AnhedoniaLoss of interest or pleasure in almost all activities. From an ("without") + hedone ("pleasure"). One of the two symptoms at least one of which must be present for major depressive disorder.
Psychomotor retardationVisible slowing of physical activity: movement or speech. Slow talking, long pauses before speaking, taking a long time to cross a room, slow chewing. Associated with severe depression.
Psychomotor agitationAn increase in activity brought on by mental tension. Restlessness, pacing, tapping fingers or feet, abruptly starting and stopping tasks. Associated with agitated depression.

Learning Outcomes

  • Etiological theories and contributing factors: biological and genetic vulnerability, neurotransmitter dysregulation, cognitive theory, learned helplessness, and stressors that differ across the lifespan.
  • The four depressive disorders, and how to tell them apart: major depressive disorder, disruptive mood dysregulation disorder, premenstrual dysphoric disorder, persistent depressive disorder. Know the symptoms, treatments, and nursing care of each.
  • ★ Learn the medications by classification first, then the drugs inside each. Most of a class shares its side effects. SSRIs and SNRIs group together as serotonin reuptake inhibitors. Know the side effects and what to watch for.
  • ★ Serotonin syndrome: what causes it, its symptoms, and how to treat it. Untreated it can be fatal.
  • ★ Hypertensive crisis: what causes it, its symptoms, its treatment, and how it is prevented.
  • ★ Anticholinergic effects: what they are, what causes them, and how they are treated.
  • Antidepressant principles: 4–6 weeks to symptom improvement. Physiological symptoms improve before psychological ones, which is the window of highest suicide risk. Medication continues 6–9 months past symptom relief. Not addictive but never stopped abruptly. No alcohol.
  • ★ Symptoms of depression to recognize: hopelessness, sadness, worthlessness, shame, anhedonia, anxiety, self-harming behavior, and feeling unable to keep oneself safe.
  • ★ Apply the nursing process and clinical judgment model to the care of individuals with depressive disorders.

Bipolar & Related Disorders

Key Terms

TermDefinition
ManiaAt least 1 week of elevated, expansive, or irritable mood with increased activity or energy, causing marked impairment in functioning or requiring hospitalization. Psychotic features may be present.
HypomaniaAt least 4 consecutive days of the same change, uncharacteristic of the person and observable by others, but not severe enough to cause marked impairment or require hospitalization. No psychotic features. Any psychosis makes the episode manic by definition.
Rapid cyclingFour or more manic episodes for at least 2 weeks in 12 months, with partial or full remission for 2 months at a time or a switch to the opposite pole. Not the same as mood lability, which shifts every one or two hours.

Learning Outcomes

  • ★ Signs and symptoms of bipolar I and bipolar II. Bipolar I: at least one manic episode, the most severe form, highest mortality. Bipolar II: at least one hypomanic plus at least one major depressive episode, never a manic one.
  • Distinguish a manic from a hypomanic episode: 1 week versus at least 4 consecutive days, marked impairment or hospitalization versus neither, psychotic features possible versus none.
  • ★ The common medications used for bipolar disorders: lithium carbonate, the anticonvulsant mood stabilizers, the second-generation antipsychotics, and benzodiazepines for severe agitation.
  • Distinguish early from severe lithium toxicity: therapeutic 0.6–1.4 mEq/L. 1.5–2.0 early toxicity, withhold the dose and draw a level. 2.0–2.5 advanced, hospitalize. Over 2.5 severe, hemodialysis.
  • ★ Side effects of the bipolar medications, including the lamotrigine rash, which can be fatal if the drug is not stopped.
  • ★ Treating an acute manic episode is not the same as maintenance treatment. An acute episode gets something heavier added on top, usually an antipsychotic, which comes back off once the mania is controlled.
  • Nursing care for an individual with a bipolar disorder. Through the first days of acute mania the focus is degree of exhaustion and nutrition. The primary outcome is injury prevention.

Suicide

Key Terms

TermDefinition
Suicidal ideationThinking about, considering, or planning to kill oneself.
Suicidal planThe organized plan and time frame for killing oneself.
Suicide intentAt the time of the injury or death, the person had thoughts of killing themselves or wished to die.
Suicide attemptA non-fatal self-directed potentially injurious behavior with any intent to die.
Suicide / death by suicideThe voluntary and intentional act of taking one's own life. Also called fatal self-directed violence. All three describe the same thing.
Suicidal self-directed violenceBehavior that is self-directed and deliberately results in injury or the potential for injury to oneself, with evidence of suicidal intent.
Non-suicidal self-directed violenceThe same behavior, but with no evidence of suicidal intent.
Interrupted self-directed violence, by otherThe person takes steps to injure themselves but is stopped by another person before the fatal injury.
Interrupted self-directed violence, by selfThe person takes steps to injure themselves but stops themselves before the fatal injury.
Suicide contagionAn increase in suicides when stories about suicide increase, are reported at length or prominently, concern a broadly known person, or carry a dramatic headline. Detailing a method increases the use of that method.
Suicide survivorThe family and friends grieving a loved one's suicide. Not the person who attempted.

Learning Outcomes

  • The unacceptable terms: "committed suicide," "successful" or "failed" attempt, "completed suicide." Say died by suicide and suicide attempt instead.
  • Components of a comprehensive risk assessment, screening, and safety planning: the C-SSRS, SAFE-T, and the Stanley-Brown safety plan.
  • Risk factors, protective factors, and the warning signs. Warning signs include giving away prized possessions or pets, looking for the means, increasing substance use, and withdrawing from friends and family.
  • Key elements of suicide precautions and environmental safety in the hospital: remove sharps, belts and dangerous articles. Search belongings and do a full skin assessment on entry. Levels of observation. Ask the patient to report suicidal thoughts immediately. Reassess intensity each shift. Belongings are returned at discharge, not when precautions are lifted. Other patients move around the unit too.
  • Treatment and management using basic-level interventions, and how to promote recovery in a patient status post attempt. Encourage verbalization of feelings rather than avoiding the attempt.
  • Short-term and long-term management, including pharmacological interventions: low-dose lithium, about 25 mg PO daily for a few weeks, reduces suicidal ideation quickly.
  • ★ Safety is the priority on any question where suicide risk is in play. Ask about it directly and before anything else. Asking does not increase the risk or plant the idea.

Nonsuicidal Self-Injury

Learning Outcomes

  • Define NSSI: deliberate, self-inflicted destruction or injury of body tissue without suicidal intent. That absence of intent is the whole distinction from a suicide attempt.
  • Epidemiology, risk factors, comorbidity, and clinical picture. DSM-5 criteria: at least 5 days in the past year, not necessarily consecutive. Done to relieve negative feelings, resolve an interpersonal difficulty, or induce a positive state. Not socially sanctioned behavior such as piercing or tattooing.
  • Nursing care: establish a therapeutic relationship, care for the wounds, and build healthy coping mechanisms to use instead of self-harm. Assess along four lines: what kind of injury, how often, what triggers it, and what has helped before.
  • Biological treatments and psychological therapies: CBT and DBT, both successful inpatient or outpatient, plus group therapy and psychopharmacology.

Substance Use & Addictive Disorders

Key Terms

TermDefinition
Blood alcohol concentration (BAC)Alcohol intoxication is 80 to 100 mg ethanol per deciliter of blood (mg/dL), also written 0.08 to 0.10 g/dL. The Kentucky DUI threshold is 0.08.
CountertransferenceWhen a healthcare worker unconsciously displaces feelings related to significant figures in their own past onto the patient. Prevented by self-assessment of one's own attitudes about substance use.
Delirium tremens (DTs)The most severe form of alcohol withdrawal: a medical emergency that can result in death. Appears any time within the first 72 hours after the last drink. Hepatitis or pancreatitis increases the likelihood.
Wernicke encephalopathyThe acute, reversible first stage of Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome, from thiamine (B1) deficiency. Confusion, ataxia, double vision, droopy eyelid, nystagmus. Treated with large IV thiamine doses 2–3 times daily for 1–2 weeks.
Korsakoff syndromeThe chronic, debilitating and not typically reversible second stage. Adds severe, irreversible memory impairment, confabulation, hallucinations and repetitious speech. Thiamine replacement for 3–12 months, with a recovery rate of only about 20%.
Dual diagnosisCo-occurring mental illness and a substance or addictive disorder. Distinct from comorbidity, which is any two or more co-occurring chronic disorders.
SBIRTScreening, Brief Intervention, and Referral to Treatment: early intervention and treatment for people with a substance use disorder in general, not alcohol alone, and those at risk. Usable by any healthcare professional in any setting. Goal: reduce and prevent related health consequences, disease, accidents and injuries, and reduce costs and healthcare utilization.
AUDITAlcohol Use Disorders Identification Test, a 10-item WHO tool covering alcohol consumption, drinking behaviors and alcohol-related problems. Used for adults. CRAFFT is the adolescent tool.
CRAFFTSix questions for ages 12–21: Car, Relax, Alone, Forget, Family or friends, Trouble. A score of 2 or more needs further evaluation. 4 or more suggests dependence.
CAGECut down · Annoyed by criticism · Guilty about drinking · Eye-opener, a morning drink. A score of 2 or more is significant. A score of 1 requires further assessment.
CAGE-AIDThe same four questions Adapted to Include Drugs, not alcohol alone.

Learning Outcomes

  • ★ Differentiate the signs and symptoms of the two main withdrawal syndromes, and know how to treat each. Opioid withdrawal: tachycardia, hypertension, hyperthermia, enlarged pupils, lacrimation, rhinorrhea, piloerection, muscle spasms, bone and joint aches, GI cramping and diarrhea, yawning, insomnia. Alcohol or benzodiazepine (sedative-hypnotic) withdrawal: tremors, anxiety, agitation, nausea, rising vital signs, then hallucinosis, then seizures, then delirium tremens. They look very different from each other.
  • ★ Know the opioid withdrawal symptoms on the COWS: resting pulse rate, sweating, restlessness, pupil size, bone or joint aches, runny nose or tearing, GI upset, tremor, yawning, anxiety or irritability, gooseflesh skin.
  • ★ Explain the symptoms, progression and management of Wernicke's encephalopathy and Korsakoff syndrome: both stages, caused by thiamine (B1) deficiency, the first reversible and the second not.
  • ★ Know these eleven medications: acamprosate, diazepam, disulfiram, lorazepam, thiamine, clonidine, naloxone, buprenorphine, buprenorphine & naloxone, methadone, naltrexone.
  • Describe addiction, withdrawal, countertransference and intoxication as they relate to substance use and nursing care.
  • Demonstrate non-stigmatizing, person-first language in care and documentation — "a person with alcohol use disorder," never "an alcoholic." "A person with a substance use disorder," never "an addict."
  • Identify common risk factors: genetic predisposition, neurotransmitter disruption, brain structure and function, immature adolescent brain development, the craving and relapse cycle, tolerance and withdrawal. Plus chronic stressors, socioeconomic factors, anxiety, substance use in family or peers, trauma or abuse, access, and ineffective coping.
  • Describe the neurobiological process and the neurotransmitters involved: the mesolimbic reward pathway: dopamine surge up to 5–10 times normal, reinforcement, craving, repeat use. Dopamine is most central. Serotonin, GABA, glutamate and endorphins are also involved. Alcohol breaks down into acetate, which triggers craving for more acetate, and that effect never resets even with abstinence.
  • Discuss clinical manifestations of use, intoxication, withdrawal, tolerance, overdose and treatment. Opioid overdose is an emergency. The cardinal signs are coma, pinpoint pupils and respiratory depression. Give naloxone and repeat if signs return, because its half-life is shorter than the opioid's.
  • Identify pharmacologic and psychological treatments for withdrawal and recovery. Alcohol withdrawal: diazepam or lorazepam at a CIWA-Ar of 8–10 or more. Recovery: naltrexone, disulfiram and acamprosate, none of which treats withdrawal. Opioids: methadone, buprenorphine, buprenorphine & naloxone, naltrexone, clonidine. Psychological: individual and behavioral therapy, CBT, family therapy, social skills training, support groups.
  • Discuss the use of the CIWA and the COWS. COWS assesses opioid withdrawal. CIWA assesses sedative-hypnotic and alcohol withdrawal, scoring nausea/vomiting, tremors, anxiety, agitation, paroxysmal sweats, headache, tactile, auditory and visual disturbance, and orientation, usually every 4 hours and PRN.
  • Describe the interdisciplinary approach: SBIRT, recovery supports and collaborative care. The care continuum runs detoxification, rehabilitation, halfway houses, partial hospitalization, intensive outpatient programs, outpatient treatment, AA/NA, and relapse prevention.
  • Apply the clinical judgment measurement model to individuals with substance use and addictive disorders: recognize and analyze cues through screening and assessment, prioritize hypotheses, generate solutions, take action, evaluate outcomes. Involving relatives in treatment decreases relapse rates by 20 to 50 percent.

Personality Disorders

Key Terms

ClusterCharacter & Disorders
Cluster AOdd or eccentric. Paranoid · schizoid · schizotypal. Mnemonic: "PaSS on the party."
Cluster BDramatic, emotional, antagonistic and erratic. Borderline · antisocial · histrionic · narcissistic. Mnemonic: "BAHNed from the party."

Learning Outcomes

  • Define personality disorder: an enduring pattern of deviant inner experience and behavior that differs from cultural expectations, is pervasive, inflexible and stable, and leads to distress or impairment, with dysfunction in cognition, impulse control, relationships and affect.
  • Identify the clusters: ten disorders in three clusters: A eccentric, B erratic, C anxious.
  • Identify the differentiating characteristics of each type. Paranoid: longstanding distrust, grudges, vindictiveness. Schizoid: detached loner who does not want company. Schizotypal: magical thinking and ideas of reference they can be made aware of. Borderline: instability of mood, identity and relationships, with self-harm. Antisocial: disregard for others' rights, no remorse, prior conduct disorder. Histrionic: dramatic, attention-seeking, seductive, no insight. Narcissistic: entitlement and grandiosity over a fragile self-worth. Dependent: submissive and clinging, cannot decide alone. Obsessive-compulsive: perfectionism and rigidity that does not disable daily function. Avoidant: inhibited by fear of criticism and rejection.
  • Discuss etiological theories: genetic: traits are inherited but the disorders are not. Neurobiological: neurotransmitters influence temperament. Psychosocial and environmental: learned maladaptive responses, neglect, rejection, divorce, trauma. Plus the diathesis-stress model, a biological predisposition plus a stressor that brings out the disorder.
  • Describe treatment and nursing care: restructuring personality takes years of psychotherapy, so short-term work on problem-solving, social skills and coping is used. Psychopharmacology, individual therapy, DBT, family education and therapy, social skills training groups, group therapy. Medications are rarely indicated long-term. Used briefly to keep patients safe and manage anxiety or psychotic symptoms.
  • Discuss the factors behind variable prognosis: personality disorders are treatable and people keep growing and changing, but prognosis depends on the degree of impairment and the person's motivation. Many do not believe a problem exists, or that they cause it.
  • Describe splitting: a defense mechanism, seen commonly in borderline personality disorder, in which the person cannot integrate and accept both positive and negative feelings, so people, including themselves, and situations are either all good or all bad.

Eating & Feeding Disorders

Key Terms

TermDefinition
Eating disordersEating consistently below or above the caloric need to maintain a healthy weight, occurring without hunger or failing to produce satiety, and resulting in physiologic imbalances or medical complications.
Anorexia nervosa (AN)Life-threatening. Intense fear of weight gain, a severely distorted body image, and restriction of calories relative to requirements with a significantly low BMI.
Bulimia nervosa (BN)Recurrent episodes of uncontrollable binge eating followed by compensatory behavior to avoid weight gain through purging, laxatives, diuretics or excessive exercise.
Binge-eating disorder (BED)Recurrent binge eating with marked distress and impaired control, and no recurrent compensatory behavior. The most common eating disorder.
Binge eatingEating, within a 2-hour period, an amount definitely larger than most people would eat in that time, with a sense of lack of control.
PurgingCompensatory behavior to avoid weight gain: self-induced vomiting, or misuse of laxatives, diuretics or enemas.
SatietyComfortable fullness.
Body mass index (BMI)The gauge used to determine the severity of anorexia nervosa. Mild 17 or more · moderate 16 to 17 · severe 15 to 16 · extreme under 15.
AmenorrheaLoss of menstrual periods in girls and women past puberty.
LanugoDowny growth of body hair on the face and back, seen in anorexia nervosa.
Russell's SignCalluses or scars on the back of the hands and knuckles caused by self-induced vomiting.
Refeeding syndromeA serious and potentially fatal condition that can occur during refeeding, caused by sudden shifts in the electrolytes that help the body metabolize food.
PicaIngestion of substances that have no nutritional value, such as dirt or paint.
Rumination disorderUndigested food returned to the mouth, then rechewed, reswallowed or spit out.
Avoidant/Restrictive Food Intake DisorderFood avoidance causing significant weight loss, nutritional deficiency, dependence on supplements or enteral feeding, and marked interference with functioning. Often tied to the sensory qualities of food.

Learning Outcomes

  • Compare and contrast the physical characteristics of the three eating disorders. Anorexia: significantly low BMI, amenorrhea, lanugo, mottled cool skin, peripheral edema, low blood pressure, pulse and temperature, decreased bone density. Bulimia: typically normal weight, Russell's Sign, parotid swelling, dental enamel erosion and caries. Binge-eating disorder: normal weight, overweight or obese, with hypertension, high cholesterol, heart disease, diabetes and gallbladder disease.
  • Compare and contrast the epidemiology of the three. Anorexia: lifetime prevalence 0.5%, ratio 3:1 female to male, onset in adolescence or young adulthood. Bulimia: 2.3% of women and 0.5% of men, ratio 10:1, onset later adolescence with a mean of 18. Binge-eating disorder: 3.6% of women and 2.1% of men, the most common of the three, all racial and ethnic groups represented fairly equally.
  • Compare and contrast the signs, symptoms and risk factors. All three share dieting, body dissatisfaction, low self-esteem, perfectionism, impulsivity, a history of abuse and comorbid anxiety or depression. What separates them is whether compensatory behavior is present: always in bulimia, only in the binge-purge type of anorexia, never in binge-eating disorder.
  • Distinguish the two types of anorexia nervosa, assigned on the last 3 months of behavior. Restricting type: weight loss through dieting, fasting and excessive exercise, with no regular binge eating or purging. Binge-eating and purging type: regular self-induced vomiting or misuse of laxatives, diuretics or enemas.
  • State the DSM-5 criteria for each disorder. Bulimia and binge-eating disorder both require episodes at least once a week for 3 months. Binge-eating disorder additionally requires three or more of: eating much more rapidly than normal, eating until uncomfortably full, eating large amounts when not hungry, eating alone from embarrassment, and feeling disgusted, depressed or very guilty afterward.
  • Describe the biological factors. Genetic and familial predisposition in both anorexia and bulimia, with genetic correlations to major depressive disorder, anxiety disorders, OCD and schizophrenia. Altered brain serotonin function dysregulates appetite, mood and impulse control, and bulimia specifically involves lower brain serotonin.
  • Describe the cognitive and environmental factors. Cognitively, anorexia is ego-syntonic: the person knows the actions are harmful but believes the benefits outweigh the harm. Both involve poor emotional regulation and low distress tolerance. Environmentally, internalization of a thin body ideal, childhood sexual or physical abuse, trauma, and weight-based teasing or bullying.
  • Describe the criteria for hospitalization. For anorexia: extreme electrolyte imbalance or weight below 75% of ideal body weight, less than 10% body fat, daytime heart rate under 50, systolic blood pressure under 90, temperature under 96 degrees Fahrenheit, or arrhythmias. For bulimia: life-threatening complications or suicide risk, or failed outpatient treatment. Anorexia usually needs hospitalization, bulimia usually does not.
  • Describe the biological treatments. Anorexia has no FDA-approved medication, and fluoxetine helps obsessive-compulsive behavior only after maintenance weight is reached. Fluoxetine is the only FDA-approved medication for bulimia in adults. Binge-eating disorder uses SSRIs and lisdexamfetamine dimesylate. Weight restoration targets 90% of ideal body weight, and refeeding syndrome is the complication to watch for.
  • Describe the psychological therapies. CBT is first-line for bulimia and is most effective combined with fluoxetine. Binge-eating disorder uses individual or group CBT, DBT and support groups. Anorexia has no empirical support for any specific model in adults, but in adolescents there is evidence for insight-oriented therapy, family-based treatment, adolescent-focused therapy and CBT.
  • Describe the three feeding disorders. Pica: ingestion of non-nutritive substances, usually beginning in early childhood, treated by monitoring eating behavior and rewarding appropriate eating. Rumination disorder: food returned to the mouth and rechewed, diagnosed after 1 month of symptoms, treated by repositioning during feeding and improving caregiver interaction. ARFID: food avoidance tied to sensory qualities, treated with behavioral modification, with family therapy not usually necessary.

Schizophrenia & Schizoaffective Disorder

Key Terms

Positive Symptoms: Alterations in PerceptionDefinition
PsychosisExperiencing hallucinations, delusions, or disorganized thoughts, speech or behavior.
ParanoiaIrrational fear ranging from mild to profound, with deep mistrust or suspicion of others.
DelusionsFalse beliefs held despite a lack of evidence, not corrected by reasoning.
PersecutoryBelief that one is being watched, plotted against or ridiculed.
ReferentialBelief that unconnected events are somehow related to you.
GrandioseBelief that one is a powerful or important person.
ErotomanicBelief that another person desires you romantically.
NihilisticThe delusion of nonexistence: the mind, body or world no longer exists.
SomaticBelief that the body is changing in unusual ways.
ControlBelief that an external force controls your thoughts, feelings, impulses or behavior.
HallucinationsPerceiving a sensory experience for which no external source exists. Can involve all five senses.
AuditoryHearing voices or sounds. The most common type.
CommandAuditory hallucinations that instruct the patient to act in a specific way, from innocuous to dangerous. Suicidal or homicidal commands require immediate safety measures.
Visual · olfactory · gustatory · tactileSeeing people or things · smelling odors · experiencing tastes · feeling body sensations.
Positive Symptoms: Speech & ThoughtDefinition
CircumstantialityExtremely detailed, lengthy conversation that eventually reaches the point.
TangentialityWandering off topic and never reaching the point.
Looseness of associationAbsence of the normal connectedness of thoughts, ideas and topics.
Flight of ideasTopic changes repeatedly and rapidly with only superficial connections.
EcholaliaPathological repetition of another's words.
Clang associationChoosing words by sound rather than meaning, often a rhyme.
Pressured speechUrgent or intense speech, with reluctance to allow comments from others.
Word saladA string of totally unconnected words, meaningless to a listener.
NeologismsMade-up words with meaning for the patient only.
Thought blockingReduction or stoppage of thought, losing the thread mid-sentence.
Thought insertionBelief that someone else has inserted thoughts into the patient's brain.
Thought deletionBelief that thoughts have been taken or are missing.
Positive Symptoms: Alterations in BehaviorDefinition
CatatoniaPronounced increase or decrease in the rate and amount of movement. Excessive motor activity is purposeless.
EchopraxiaInvoluntary imitation of another's movements and gestures.
Motor retardationPronounced slowing of movement.
Motor agitationExcited behavior such as running or pacing rapidly, often in response to stimuli.
NegativismTendency to resist or oppose the requests or wishes of others.
Stereotyped behaviorsRepetitive, purposeless movements peculiar to the person.
Gesturing or posturingPosture held in a fixed position.
Impaired impulse controlReduced ability to resist impulses.
Boundary impairmentImpaired ability to sense where one's body or influence ends and another's begins.
Negative, Cognitive & Affective SymptomsDefinition
Affective bluntingReduced or constricted affect.
AlogiaReduction in speech, or poverty of speech.
AnhedoniaReduced ability or inability to experience pleasure or joy.
ApathyDecreased interest in activities or beliefs that would otherwise matter.
AsocialityDecreased desire for social interaction, or discomfort during it.
AvolitionReduced or lost motivation or goal-directed behavior.
AnosognosiaInability to realize one is ill, caused by the illness itself. Leads to resistance to or cessation of treatment, often combined with paranoia.
Concrete thinkingImpaired ability to think abstractly, interpreting things literally.
Impaired memoryImpacts short-term memory and the ability to learn.
Impaired information processingDelayed responses, misperceptions or difficulty understanding others.
Impaired executive functioningDifficulty reasoning, setting priorities, comparing options and planning.
Affective symptomsAltered experience and expression of emotions. Mood may be unstable, erratic, labile or incongruent.
Medication EffectsDefinition
Extrapyramidal side effects (EPS)Serious side effects affecting motor control and coordination. The first three begin within a few weeks of starting or increasing a medication.
Acute dystonic reactionsSudden, sustained contraction of muscle groups, usually head and neck. Airway involvement is an emergency.
AkathisiaMotor restlessness: pacing, rocking, inability to stay still. Easily mistaken for anxiety or agitation.
PseudoparkinsonismTemporary symptoms resembling Parkinson's disease: stiff stooped posture, shuffling gait, bradykinesia, pill-rolling, resting tremor.
Tardive dyskinesia (TD)Involuntary rhythmic movements with long-term antipsychotic treatment, usually oral and facial. Assess with the AIMS at least every 3 months.
Anticholinergic side effectsDry mouth, blurred vision, dry eyes, constipation, urinary retention, drowsiness, confusion, tachycardia, skin flushing. Can progress to toxicity.
Neuroleptic malignant syndrome (NMS)Rare, serious and potentially fatal, usually early in therapy. Severe muscle rigidity, altered mental status, temperature over 103 degrees Fahrenheit, hypertension, tachycardia, tachypnea, diaphoresis, incontinence. 10% fatality rate.
Metabolic syndromeWeight gain, dyslipidemia, increased blood glucose and insulin resistance. Of special concern with atypical antipsychotics.

Learning Outcomes

  • ★ Know which symptoms each classification treats. Typical antipsychotics primarily treat the positive symptoms and have little effect on the negative symptoms. Atypical antipsychotics treat both positive and negative symptoms.
  • ★ Know the medications that fall under each classification, and the side effects and adverse reactions typical of that classification. Typical: chlorpromazine, haloperidol, and haloperidol decanoate as a long-acting injection. Atypical: clozapine, olanzapine, quetiapine, risperidone, aripiprazole, with risperidone and paliperidone palmitate as long-acting injections. Anticholinergics used to treat side effects: benztropine, trihexyphenidyl.
  • ★ Know how to treat or manage the medication side effects and adverse reactions. Acute dystonia: monitor and act emergently if the airway is involved. Akathisia: reduce the dose or change medication, add benztropine, and propranolol, lorazepam or diazepam short-term only. Pseudoparkinsonism: identify and slowly discontinue the drug, reduce the dose, or add benztropine or trihexyphenidyl. Tardive dyskinesia: discontinue or reduce, switch to an atypical by tapering one down while tapering the other up, and valbenazine or deutetrabenazine. NMS: stop all antipsychotics immediately, ICU, dantrolene sodium and bromocriptine mesylate, lorazepam for agitation, cooling and hydration.
  • ★ Agranulocytosis is a side effect of taking clozapine. A reduction in circulating granulocytes that limits the ability to fight infection. Life-threatening, greatest risk in the first months, monitor WBC weekly for the first 18 weeks, and reversible if treated early.
  • Discuss the proposed etiologies of schizophrenia: genetics, environmental factors, brain chemistry and substance use, understood through the vulnerability-stress model. Prevalence is 1% to 1.1% worldwide, about 3.5 million in the United States, more frequent in males and in urban areas, with peak onset between 15 and 35 and 50% before age 25.
  • Differentiate the positive symptoms, which exist but should not be there: alterations in perception (paranoia, delusions, hallucinations), speech, thought and behavior.
  • Differentiate the negative symptoms, which should be there but are not: affective blunting, alogia, anhedonia, apathy, asociality and avolition. They are a serious problem because they amount to the absence of essential human qualities.
  • Differentiate the cognitive and affective symptoms. Cognitive: anosognosia, concrete thinking, impaired memory, impaired information processing and impaired executive functioning. Affective: an altered experience and expression of emotion, with mood that is unstable, erratic, labile or incongruent.
  • State the DSM-5 criteria for schizophrenia: two or more characteristic symptoms for a significant portion of a 1-month period, at least one being delusions, hallucinations or disorganized speech, with functioning markedly below the previous level and continuous signs for at least 6 months.
  • Name the phases: prodromal, with mild changes appearing 1 to 12 months before the first full episode · acute, which can last several months even with treatment · stabilization, moving toward the previous level of functioning · maintenance or residual, where positive symptoms are largely absent but negative and cognitive symptoms continue.
  • Discuss schizoaffective disorder symptoms and treatments. Symptoms of schizophrenia plus a mood disorder, with lifetime prevalence 0.3%. Bipolar type has manic and depressive episodes, depressive type has depression only. The distinguishing feature is that hallucinations and delusions persist for two or more weeks in the absence of a major mood episode. Many interventions are borrowed from schizophrenia and bipolar treatment.
  • Discuss care considerations. Priority problems are led by safety: risk for violence toward others, risk for suicide or self-harm, disturbed thought processes and sensory perception, risk for non-adherence. Outcomes are specific and measurable with a timeframe, developed with the patient. Interventions: assess with the C-SSRS each shift, assess orientation, teach one coping strategy each shift, administer medications and PRNs for agitation, and educate patient and family. Evaluate against safety, physical care, medication stabilization, education and psychosocial support.
  • Explain why non-adherence matters. 80% to 90% relapse during the course of the illness, each relapse takes longer to recover from, and stopping medication almost certainly leads to relapse, with 80% relapsing within 2 years. Combining medication and psychotherapy reduces the severity and frequency of relapses.
  • Intervene with delusions and hallucinations. Never debate, argue or try to disprove a delusion, which intensifies it and destroys trust. Focus on the underlying fear and on helping the patient feel safe, validate what is real and present reality. For hallucinations, ask directly about type and content, do not respond as if they are real and do not deny the experience, and engage the patient in reality-based activities.
