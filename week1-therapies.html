Assessment, Theories & Therapies

The Psychiatric Mental Health Nursing Assessment

Do a complete assessment on every patient, in any practice setting, and understand the role the mind-body connection plays in mental health disorders.

Purpose of the Assessment

  • Establish rapport: the foundation of everything that follows.
  • Understand the current problem, including the chief complaint.
  • Review physical status: obtain baseline vital signs.
  • Assess risk and safety: risk factors, and anything affecting the safety of the patient and others on the unit.
  • Assess psychosocial status: the dimensions of the person's life.
  • Identify mutual goals: set treatment goals with the client.
  • Formulate a plan of care: document the data in a retrievable format.

Gathering the Data

  • Review of systems: vital signs and all systems.
  • Labs: bring abnormal results to the NP, PA, or MD.
  • Mental status: current cognitive processes, including behavior, speech, appearance, and mood.
  • Validation: previous records on the unit, police reports, emergency department records.
  • Rating scales: the PHQ-9 and the suicide risk screening.
  • Psychosocial assessment: occupation, sexuality, hobbies, spirituality, coping, relationships, lifestyle.

Analyzing & Recognizing Cues

What You Assess, and What You Are Looking For

  • Affect: what you see.
  • Thought processes and content: circumstantial or loose? Delusional? Any hallucinations? Any suicidal thoughts?
  • Judgment and insight: assessed as its own area.
  • Mood and feelings: in the person's own words.
  • Physical behavior and activity: eye contact, posture, agitation, psychomotor retardation.
  • Movement: tics, tremors, akathisia.
  • Communication: speech rate (rapid, slow, pressured), volume, and fluency.
If a person is depressed, they must be assessed for suicidal tendencies. Be aware of symptoms that can mask depression. If a patient seems depressed or down, do not be afraid to ask directly: "Are you depressed?" and, if yes, "Do you feel that you might hurt yourself?" Always weigh the physical evidence and the mood of the patient when planning care.

Affect

Affect is the observed responsiveness of a person's emotional state: the physical manifestations and observable behavior. Gauged two ways: range and congruency.

Range

  • Full / broad: normal expression of feeling, sad or happy; what most people without mental illness show.
  • Constricted: a little less expression than normal.
  • Blunted: limited expression, but some is still there; may smile a little at times.
  • Flat: no emotion on the face whatsoever, like the masking seen in Parkinson's. A blank canvas.

Congruency

  • Congruent: mood and affect match.
  • Incongruent: "I'm extremely happy today," said flatly; crying while saying "I'm doing great."
  • Sad also describes affect: eye contact downcast, stooped posture, and the facial expression.

Mood

Document the patient's own description of their mood; a psychiatric term can be added alongside it.

TermMeaning
EuthymicNormal mood.
Elated / euphoricAbnormally elevated mood.
DysthymicChronically low mood.
LabileMood goes up and down.
CyclothymicCycling mood.
Everyday descriptorsCalm, anxious, agitated, overwhelmed, helpless, and hopeless, which is very important to document.

Screening Questions for Mood

  • "How are you feeling?"
  • "Have you been discouraged / depressed / low / blue lately?"
  • "Have you been energized / elated / high / out of control lately?"

Psychosocial Interventions

Psychosocial interventions are non-pharmacological therapeutic techniques that address the psychological aspects (societal, familial, and cultural) through individual or group therapy, and promote mental wellness.

InterventionWhat It Involves
Group or individual therapyDelivered one-to-one or in a group.
Music therapyStrongly evidence-based, and not just psychiatric; now used in NICUs and other ICUs.
EducationOn the illness, its symptoms, or its treatment.
Positive self-talkCognitive behavioral technique to modify negative thought patterns; improves self-esteem, resilience, and emotional regulation.
Self-monitoringJournaling mood, feelings, or sleep; strong because the client can keep doing it after discharge.
Enhancing communication skillsBuilding healthy relationships, navigating social situations, expressing needs and emotions.
Healthy relationshipsReduce isolation; provide emotional and practical support.
Play therapyFor younger children.
Family consultations and sessionsHelp relatives understand what is going on, help parents develop more effective parenting skills, and teach suicide risk and how to monitor for it. Also used where a client has a guardian, or a spouse.
Limit settingFor sexual preoccupation or agitation. Be matter-of-fact: "What you're saying is inappropriate, and I will come back when we can interact better." Plan your response in advance.
Increasing coping skills and self-esteemRecreational activities and therapies: a craft can become a coping skill and boost self-esteem. Group exercise: list three positive things about yourself.
Stress reduction and relaxationTaught as strategies the client can use.
Spiritual supportNot necessarily about religion, but a sense of connection to something bigger than ourselves, including nature or art. Cognitive, experiential, and behavioral; gives a person strength.
Psychological safetyEveryone contributes in groups; no answer is wrong and there are no wrong questions.
Unconditional positive regardGiven to the client regardless.
Recognition of relapse symptomsNurses watch for them, and clients are taught to recognize their own.
Discharge planningCounts as a psychosocial intervention in its own right.

Psychoanalytic Theory & Therapy

Freud's theory, rarely used now in the acute setting. Freud believed mental disorders resulted from unresolved issues from childhood.

Three Levels of Awareness

  • Consciousness: everything one is aware of at a given time.
  • Preconsciousness: easily retrievable.
  • Unconscious: memories and emotions retrievable only with the help of a trained therapist. This is where a lot of clients' traumatic memories live.

Personality Structures

  • Id: present at birth; unconscious, impulsive, illogical.
  • Ego: begins around age 2–3; the problem-solver and reality-tester.
  • Superego: the moral part of personality.
  • Freud thought all three should work together, but that the ego is in charge.
ToolWhat It Is
Free associationThe patient shares whatever is on their mind.
Dream analysisAnalyzing dream content for specific meanings; dreaming of falling may mean being unable to control situations.
Defense mechanism recognitionDefense mechanisms decrease anxiety and internal stress; help patients recognize and change the overuse of mechanisms like denial and rationalization. More in Chapter 15.

Purpose: uncovering unconscious conflicts. Not seen inpatient; it runs two to three times a week, for several years. What carries forward: childhood experience shapes personality development, and traumatic memories are unconscious.

Behavioral Theories & the Five Behavioral Therapies

Personality consists of learned behavior, influenced by conditioning: pairing a behavior with a condition that reinforces or decreases it (Pavlov's dog and the bell). Three textbook theories gave rise to behavioral therapy: classical conditioning, behavioral theory, and operant conditioning theory.

TherapyWhat It Does
ModelingDemonstrating behaviors more effective than the current ones; a lot like role-playing.
Operant conditioningUsing positive reinforcement to increase the behaviors you want to see: tokens earned for going to groups, completing ADLs, and asking for meds, rising through levels that unlock privileges.
Systematic desensitizationEvidence-based; combines relaxation techniques with gradual exposure to overcome a phobia. Can be virtual, in vivo (real world), or imaginal.
Aversion therapyThe opposite of operant conditioning: a negative stimulus to decrease unwanted behavior. Disulfiram (Antabuse) in alcohol dependence: drinking on it causes nausea, dizziness, and flushing, so drinking decreases.
BiofeedbackThe patient learns to control normally involuntary bodily responses (blood pressure, brain waves, heart rate) using electrical sensors that feed back information about the body. Used for stress, pain, anxiety, headaches, and ADHD.
Modeling = role playing · operant conditioning = tokens / positive reinforcement · systematic desensitization = exposure · aversion therapy = negative stimulus decreases behavior · biofeedback = control of body.

Talk Therapies & Interpersonal Therapy

Medications and talk therapy are the two main treatments for mental illness.

Interpersonal therapy targets relationships by improving functioning and communication patterns. Very short-term: 12 to 16 sessions.

TechniqueWhat Happens
Identification of emotionIdentify what the emotion is and where it is coming from.
Expression of emotionExpress it in a healthy way: calmly telling a partner how you feel, instead of acting out of anger and sarcasm.
Dealing with emotional baggageUnresolved issues from past relationships are carried into present ones; examining them shows how they affect current mood and behavior.

Cognitive Therapy & CBT

Cognitive therapy is the umbrella term for therapies focused on thoughts (cognitions), with a dynamic interplay between the individual and the environment.

The core CBT idea: our thoughts cause our feelings and behaviors, not external things like people or situations. So if we change the way we think, we can change the way we feel, and act better even if the situation does not change.

Cognitive Distortions: "Stinkin' Thinkin'"

Part of CBT: taking the irrational internal commentary we run all day and making it more rational and more positive. Developed at Hazelden, then taken further by David Burns in The Feeling Good Handbook; the set is his "Top 10 Stinkin' Thinkin' Cognitive Distortions."

DistortionWhat It Sounds Like
1. All-or-nothing thinkingSeeing things in black or white.
2. OvergeneralizationA single negative event becomes always or never: "I'm never going to get a promotion."
3. Mental filterDwelling excessively on one negative and ignoring all the positive feedback.
4. Discounting the positiveRejecting positive experiences: "that doesn't count," "that was just a fluke."
5. Jumping to conclusionsInterpreting things negatively with no facts: mind reading and fortune telling.
6. MagnificationBlowing something out of proportion.
7. Emotional reasoningTreating a feeling as evidence of fact.
8. Should statements"I should have done this, I should have done that."
9. LabelingAll-or-nothing thinking taken to an extreme: instead of "I made a mistake," you say "I'm a loser."
10. Personalization and blameHolding yourself personally responsible for something that was not even under your control.

Rational Emotive Therapy (RET)

RET explores irrational thinking and beliefs: identifying activating situations and the negative emotions that lead to irrational beliefs, then teaching the client to delete the "must," "should," "ought," and "have to."

The ABCs of RET

  • Activating event: "I receive an invitation to a party."
  • Beliefs: "I hate parties, I don't like hanging out with people."
  • Consequences, emotional: "I feel miserable."
  • The therapy works on B, because changing beliefs changes the emotional consequences.
  • Desired outcome: the client controls their behavior and thinking, and changed thinking produces changed behavior.

Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT)

DBT involves the integration of opposites. Originally developed to treat chronically suicidal individuals, many of whom had borderline personality disorder. It also helps with other self-destructive or self-injurious behaviors such as eating disorders and substance abuse.

FeatureDetail
What it combinesCognitive and behavioral techniques combined with mindfulness.
SettingBoth inpatient and outpatient, but more often outpatient, because it takes one to two years before you see the best results.
Core interventionsProblem-solving · exposure technique · skill training · contingency management · cognitive modification.
The four primary modes of treatmentGroup skills training · individual psychotherapy · telephone contact · therapist consultation and team meetings.
The teamSeveral people, including an MD psychiatrist, not just a therapist or social worker. You have one primary therapist you check in with regularly.

The Four Skill Sets

Skill SetWhat Is Learned
Emotional regulationManaging and labeling intense moods and emotions.
Interpersonal effectivenessProblem-solving skills and assertiveness, including being able to say no, which many people find very hard.
Distress toleranceAlso appears as self-management: "learning to control, manage, or change your behaviors, thoughts, and emotions."
MindfulnessBeing present in the moment; meditation skills.
Mindfulness: indulging a wandering mind reinforces the emotions that make us suffer, and most negative thoughts are about the past or the future. The past is over; the future is unreal until it happens; the present moment is the only one you can experience. Examples of the wandering mind: anger, craving, depression, and self-pity.

Systematic desensitization.

Our thoughts, not external people or situations.

Activating event, Beliefs, and emotional Consequences.

Labeling.

Mental filter.
What population was DBT originally developed to treat?
Chronically suicidal individuals, many with borderline personality disorder.

Emotional regulation, interpersonal effectiveness, distress tolerance, and mindfulness.
