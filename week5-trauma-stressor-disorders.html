Trauma & Stressor Disorders

The Trauma-Related Disorders

Disorders in This Group

  • Attachment disorders: reactive attachment disorder and disinhibited social engagement disorder.
  • Adjustment disorder.
  • Post-traumatic stress disorder in children and in adults.
  • Acute stress disorder.
  • Depersonalization/derealization disorder, related to early interpersonal trauma.

What Counts as a Traumatic Event

  • Military combat or prisoner of war exposure.
  • Crime-related: bombing, assault, mugging.
  • Natural disasters: floods, tornados, earthquakes.
  • Mass violence.
  • Serious accident: automobile, airplane, train.
  • Interpersonal trauma: physical, sexual or emotional abuse, and emotional or physical neglect.
  • Sudden and traumatic loss at any stage of life.

The person who goes on to develop a trauma disorder may have been harmed themselves, may have had the harm happen to a loved one, or may have witnessed a harmful event happening to someone else.

Trauma in Children

Change the question. Instead of asking what is wrong with this child, ask what happened to this child. Behavioral problems carry a meaning that sits behind them.

Witnessing or being part of violence is a well-documented risk factor for depression, anxiety, PTSD, impulse control disorders, substance abuse, academic problems and low self-esteem. The pattern is cyclical: children who are abused and go untreated are at risk of abusing others and develop dysfunctional patterns in close relationships.

Child Abuse and Neglect

  • Neglect is the most prevalent form of child abuse in the United States.
  • 74% of children who died from abuse and neglect were younger than 3 years old.
  • Parents are responsible for nearly 80% of child fatalities.
  • Boys suffer a higher child fatality rate than girls.
  • Trauma changes the brain. Severe neglect in the first three years of life produces visible change in the temporal lobes.

Phase Model of Treatment

Psychological, biological and family goals inside a staged protocol.

  1. Safety and stabilization. Create a safe, predictable environment, stop self-harm and self-destructive behavior, and teach about trauma and its effects.
  2. Reduce arousal and regulate emotion through symptom reduction and memory work. Help the child find comfort from other people, integrate suppressed emotions, accept ambivalence, overcome avoidance, improve attention and begin transforming memories.
  3. Catch up developmental and social skills. Problem-solving, self-awareness, social skills training, coping skills, socially appropriate goals and a value system.

The Window of Tolerance

A traumatized child's sympathetic and parasympathetic arousal are at odds, and the child has a hard time shifting emotional and physiological states to adapt to different environments. They alternate between two poles. Interventions help the child self-regulate and stay in balance, inside the window.

Hyperarousal

Intense fear, aggression, anxiety.

Hypoarousal

Withdrawal, numbness, isolation.

Attachment Disorders

Attachment is a profound, reciprocal physical and emotional relationship between a child and a primary caregiver. It endures, and it sets the stage for all future intimate and trusting relationships. Where healthy emotional bonds are not formed, an attachment disorder follows.

Parental Risk Factors

Anything that prevents meeting the child's social, emotional or physical needs in a sensitive, responsive way.

  • Mental illness or substance abuse.
  • Financial or interpersonal stressors.
  • Illness.
  • A caregiver who wants to be present but cannot be, such as a parent who becomes severely depressed and withdrawn after a loss.

Child Risk Factors

  • Difficult temperament, or slow to warm and very shy.
  • Cannot communicate needs clearly, or is hard to soothe.
  • Illness.
  • Irregular feeding schedule or sleep-wake cycle.

Reactive Attachment Disorder

DSM-5 CriteriaWhat It Requires
A. Inhibited, withdrawn behaviorA consistent pattern of inhibited, emotionally withdrawn behavior toward adult caregivers. The child rarely or minimally seeks comfort when distressed, and rarely or minimally responds to comfort when it is given.
B. Social or emotional disturbancePersistent, with at least two of: minimal social and emotional responsiveness to others, limited positive affect, or episodes of unexplained irritability, sadness or fearfulness that appear even during nonthreatening interactions with caregivers.
C. Extremes of insufficient careAt least one of: social neglect or deprivation, a persistent lack of basic emotional needs for comfort, stimulation and affection being met. Repeated changes of primary caregivers that limit stable attachment, such as frequent foster care changes. Rearing in an unusual setting such as an institution or orphanage.
Behaviors you may seeWithdrawal, fear, sadness or irritability that is not readily explained, and a sad and listless appearance. Failure to smile. Watching others closely without engaging. Not asking for support. Not reaching out when picked up. No interest in peekaboo or other interactive games.

Disinhibited Social Engagement Disorder

DSM-5 CriteriaWhat It Requires
TimingOccurs in the first 2 years of life.
Behavior toward strangersThe child approaches and interacts with unfamiliar adults, with two of: reduced reservation about approaching an unfamiliar adult, being overly familiar and violating social or cultural boundaries, not checking back with the caregiver, or willingness to go with an unfamiliar person without reservation.
Pathogenic careEvidence of persistent pathogenic care, meaning severe social neglect, shown by one or more of: the caregiver neglects basic physical and emotional needs, or repeated changes of caregiver such as frequent foster or institutional placements, so stable attachment cannot occur.
Rule outThe behavior cannot be explained by developmental delay.
The discrimination. Reactive attachment disorder forms no attachment and withdraws. Disinhibited social engagement disorder is the opposite, indiscriminately seeking interaction with any adult. A child may sit on a stranger's lap on a playground and never look back at the caregiver.

Treatment of RAD and DSED

There is no standard treatment, and it must always include the primary familial caregivers. Early intervention has better outcomes.

What the Child Needs

  • Positive interactions with caregivers and, if inpatient, with staff.
  • Attachment through the five senses. Hold, hug, touch, feed and talk to the child. Use story-telling. Encourage meals with other children and with familial caregivers.
  • A safe and stable living situation after discharge, and consistent caregivers.
  • A positive, stimulating, interactive environment, with nurturing and responsive care.
  • Medical, safety and housing needs addressed.
  • Caregiver education about the condition, plus psychotherapy, parent skills training and family therapy.

Adjustment Disorder

Occurs in children, adolescents and adults, and is time-limited and milder than PTSD or acute stress disorder.

FeatureDetail
OnsetAn emotional or behavioral reaction within 3 months of exposure to a stressor. Examples: loss or death of a loved one, retirement, parents divorcing, illness in a family member, a new sibling, a relationship breakup.
DurationSymptoms end by 6 months.
SeverityDistress affects the ability to function, and the reaction is out of proportion to the severity of the stressor.
SymptomsAnxiety, depression or mixed. In children, regressive behaviors and fearful or acting-out behavior.
Nursing careSupport, understanding and encouragement. Active listening and therapeutic communication skills. Building coping skills is the key intervention, and it is what prevents further episodes.

Acute Stress Disorder

The Timeframe Is the Diagnosis

Acute stress disorder is diagnosed 3 days to 1 month after the traumatic event. If symptoms continue beyond 1 month, the diagnosis changes to PTSD. This is the most important distinction between the two.

The person continually re-experiences the event, avoids situations or people that are reminders, and has increased anxiety and excitation such as hypervigilance, which negatively affects their lifestyle. The diagnostic emphasis on dissociative symptoms is the other distinction from PTSD.

DSM-5 Criteria

  • Exposure directly or indirectly to a traumatic event.
  • 9 or more symptoms from any of the five categories below.
  • Symptoms cause significant distress or significantly impair social or occupational functioning.
  • Symptoms are not attributable to a substance or another medical disorder.
Symptom CategorySymptoms
IntrusionRecurrent, involuntary, intrusive distressing memories of the event. Recurrent distressing dreams. Dissociative reactions such as flashbacks, in which the patient feels as though the event is recurring. Intense psychological or physiologic distress when reminded of the event, by a similar location or similar sounds.
DissociativeAn altered sense of reality, feeling in a daze, time slowing, altered perceptions. Inability to remember an important part of the traumatic event.
Negative moodPersistent inability to experience positive emotions such as happiness, satisfaction or loving feelings.
AvoidanceEfforts to avoid distressing memories, thoughts or feelings associated with the event, and to avoid external reminders: people, places, conversations, activities, objects, situations.
ArousalSleep disturbance. Irritability or angry outbursts. Hypervigilance. Difficulty concentrating. Exaggerated startle response.

Assessment needs a non-rushed, reassuring approach. Recently traumatized people have more difficulty sharing their symptoms and are more likely to experience derealization, which makes them feel less secure in the environment.

Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder

Clinical Picture and Required Exposure

Persistent re-experiencing of a highly traumatic event, with responses of intense fear, helplessness or horror. It can begin a month after exposure, but a symptom delay of months or years is not uncommon. Flashbacks and hypervigilance can produce physiological reactions: a sense of terror causing a stomach ache, heart palpitations or muscle tension. Trust is a common issue, and interpersonal, social and occupational relationships are difficult.

Qualifying Trauma Exposure

  • Directly experiencing a traumatic event.
  • Witnessing, in person, a traumatic event that happened to someone else.
  • Learning about a traumatic event that happened to a close friend or family member. Cases of death must be violent or unexpected.

Exposure through electronic media, television, movies or photographs does not count.

DSM-5 Criteria

  • Symptoms for longer than 1 month.
  • Severe enough to interfere with daily life, such as relationships or work.
  • Unrelated to medication, substance use or another illness.
  • The required number of symptoms in each of the four groups below.

The Four Core Symptom Groups

GroupHow ManySymptoms
Re-experiencingAt least 1Flashbacks, bad dreams, frightening thoughts. Recurrent intrusive recollections of the event. Flashbacks are dissociative experiences during which the event is relived, sometimes with vivid sensory input.
AvoidanceAt least 1Staying away from places, events or objects that are reminders. Avoiding thoughts or feelings related to the event, and avoiding talking about it. Often accompanied by detachment, emptiness and numbing.
Arousal and reactivityAt least 2Being easily startled. Feeling tense or on edge. Difficulty sleeping. Angry outbursts. Also irritability, difficulty concentrating and hypervigilance.
Cognition and moodAt least 2Trouble remembering key features of the traumatic event. Negative thoughts about oneself or the world. Distorted feelings such as guilt or blame. Loss of interest in enjoyable activities. Also chronic depression and negativity.

Risk Factors, Epidemiology and Comorbidity

Not everyone who lives through a dangerous event develops PTSD. Some factors are present before the trauma, others become important during and after it.

CategoryDetail
Risk factorsPrevious traumatic experiences, particularly during childhood. Getting hurt, or seeing people hurt or killed. Feeling horror, helplessness or extreme fear. Little or no social support after the event. Extra stress after the event, such as loss of a loved one, injury, or loss of a job or home. A personal or family history of mental illness or substance use.
EpidemiologyAdults: lifetime prevalence 6.8%. Adolescents 13 to 18: lifetime prevalence 5%. In both groups, females are twice as likely as males to develop PTSD.
Comorbidity80% have one or more other mental health illnesses, and 20% have none. The most common are major depressive disorder, anxiety disorders, sleep disorders, dissociative disorders and substance use disorders.

PTSD in Children and Adolescents

Children are exposed to traumatic events without the experience, strength, coping skills or ability to adequately defend themselves that adults have. Not all children who experience trauma go on to develop PTSD.

Preschool Children

  • A reduction in play, and play that includes aspects of the traumatic event.
  • Social withdrawal.
  • Negative emotions: fear, guilt, anger, horror, sadness, shame or confusion.
  • Children may blame themselves for the traumatic event.

Other Symptoms in Children

  • Reliving the event in thought or play, nightmares and sleep problems, and becoming very upset at reminders.
  • Lack of positive emotions. Hypervigilance and being easily startled.
  • Acting helpless, hopeless or withdrawn. Denying the event happened, or feeling numb.
  • Irritability, aggressive or self-destructive behaviors. Problems concentrating.
  • Detachment or estrangement from others, and diminished interest in significant activities.
  • Somatic symptoms: headaches, stomachaches or pain, plus memory problems.

Treating Acute Stress Disorder and PTSD

Psychotherapy is the main treatment for acute stress disorder, and trauma-focused psychotherapy is the primary treatment for PTSD. For PTSD the options are prolonged exposure, cognitive processing therapy and EMDR, often combined with anxiety management and stress reduction aimed at relieving symptoms.

Trauma-Focused CBT

Delivered by a trained, licensed mental health professional such as a psychologist or psychiatrist. It has four components.

  1. Learning how the body responds to trauma and stress.
  2. Symptom management skills.
  3. Identifying and reframing problematic thinking patterns.
  4. Exposure therapy, safely exposing the patient to the sources of fear and avoidance.

Patients avoid what they associate with the traumatic event, so they never learn that they can manage their fear when they meet the stimulus. Exposure therapy slowly encourages them to enter the situation and stay in it until they learn to cope.

Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing

EMDR

  • Evidence-based for children and adults, and recommended as a first-line treatment for traumatized children.
  • Retrains the patient to associate the traumatic event with positive coping. The therapist repeatedly engages the patient's gaze while the patient recalls the event and reframes it with positive thoughts. Other stimulation may be audio tones or tapping.
  • At the time of a trauma, strong emotions interfere with processing and the moment becomes frozen in time, so recalling it feels like reliving it. EMDR appears to unfreeze the memory so it can be resolved. The memory remains, but it becomes less upsetting and can be examined from a more detached perspective.
  • The exact mechanism is not known. Theories are still evolving, and it may work through neurological and physiological change.

Pharmacotherapy

There is minimal evidence supporting medications to treat acute stress disorder. In PTSD, antidepressants help with depression and anxiety symptoms, sleep problems and concentration.

Treating Children and Adolescents

Early diagnosis and treatment are very important. Treatment depends on the child's symptoms, age, general health and severity, and should be based on a comprehensive evaluation. The goal is to enhance normal growth and development and improve quality of life.

Psychotherapy

  • Cognitive behavioral therapy, to learn skills to manage anxiety and master the situations that contributed to the PTSD.
  • Play therapy.
  • EMDR.

Medication and Recovery

  • There are currently no FDA-approved medications for children with PTSD.
  • SSRIs may improve social and school functioning, and may decrease avoidance, numbing and dissociation.
  • Recovery is highly variable, and depends on the child's internal strengths, coping skills and resiliency plus their support systems. Parents play a vital supportive role, so involve them.

Applying the Nursing Process

There is no test that diagnoses acute stress disorder. A provider makes the diagnosis after a thorough psychological assessment using the DSM-5 criteria. The nursing assessment for the two disorders is very similar.

StepWhat It Involves
AssessmentInterviewing, screening, testing, observing and interacting, plus history from multiple sources. It is ongoing throughout treatment, and what is collected depends on the setting, symptom severity, presenting problem and available resources.
Priority problemsAnxiety. Impaired coping. Social isolation. Hopelessness.
OutcomesSet alongside the patient, short and long term, and realistic. Reduced response to trauma, decreased anxiety, improved adaptation, and the ability to manage anxiety in healthy ways.
ImplementationEstablish trust, safety and a therapeutic relationship. Help the person problem-solve, connect them to family, friends and community supports, educate about the disorder and its treatment, and monitor response and adherence. Use art and play therapy with children, and teach relaxation techniques, self-care and recognition of their own symptoms.
EvaluationSafety has been maintained. Anxiety has been reduced. Stress is handled adaptively. Emotions and behavior are appropriate for the situation.

Screening and Assessment in Adults

Two instruments are used: the Primary Care PTSD Screen for DSM-5 and the PTSD Checklist. A positive screen indicates a more comprehensive assessment, adding the time of onset, frequency, course, severity, level of distress and degree of functional impairment on family, work and personal life. Also assess suicidal or violent ideation, irritability and agitation, social supports, sleep, social withdrawal, life stressors, current medications and past medical and psychiatric history, and complete a mental status exam.

Assessment in Children and Adolescents

A mental status assessment shows the child's state at the time and identifies problems with thinking, feeling and behaving. History comes from parents or caregivers.

Broad Categories to Assess

Safety, general appearance, socialization, activity level, speech, coordination and motor function, affect, manner of relating, intellectual function, thought processes and content, and characteristics of play.

Observation and Interaction

  • A semi-structured interview about home life, parents and siblings, school, teachers and peers, in which the child describes their own problems. The initial interview is key to observing interactions with caregiver and siblings and to building trust.
  • Games, drawings and puppets are used with younger children, who often do not respond to a direct approach.
  • Assess for nightmares and night terrors, intrusive thoughts and memories, flashbacks, numbing, avoidance, self-injurious behavior, somatic symptoms, amnesia and difficulty concentrating. The child may re-enact the trauma in play.

Trauma-Informed Care and Inpatient Management

A trauma-informed approach acknowledges that healthcare organizations and care teams need a complete picture of a patient's life situation, past and present, to deliver care with a healing orientation. Trauma-informed care is the treatment framework built on it: recognizing and responding to the effects of all types of trauma, recognizing its signs and symptoms, and actively avoiding re-traumatization. Adopting it can improve patient engagement, treatment adherence, health outcomes and provider and staff wellness.

The shift. Trauma-informed care moves the focus from "What's wrong with you?" to "What happened to you?"

Re-traumatization is often built into routine. 15-minute night checks done with a flashlight cast a shadow into the room, which can re-traumatize a patient whose trauma memory holds that same image. There are alternative ways to do the check.

Why It Matters Outside Behavioral Health

Patients with PTSD are not only admitted to behavioral health units. They are seen on med-surg units, in ICUs and in the emergency department. PTSD is a common comorbidity among hospitalized patients, who have more contact with healthcare systems even for non-mental-health problems and a higher burden of medical comorbidities: diabetes, liver disease, gastritis, arthritis and coronary artery disease. Typical hospital routines may worsen anxiety and anger.

ElementWhat the Team Does
Trauma-informed care modelUse it as the conceptual model for the admission.
Provider recognitionCarefully review past medical history, since some patients already carry the diagnosis. Comorbid mood, anxiety or substance use disorders raise the risk and are a clue. When PTSD is suspected, screening is the next step.
CollaborationBetween providers, psychiatrists or psychologists, and pharmacists.
Individualizing careAssess the individual's needs and use active listening to identify triggers. Broaden safe opportunities for control, allow the patient choices and keep them informed. Offer complementary and integrative therapies such as aromatherapy and deep breathing.

Depersonalization/Derealization Disorder

Many people have a passing experience of depersonalization or derealization at some point. It becomes the disorder when the feelings keep occurring or never fully go away and make it hard to function personally, socially or occupationally. Episodes may last hours, days, weeks or months, and the disorder impacts relationships, work and daily activities. It is more common in people who have experienced trauma such as violence, abuse or other extreme stress.

Depersonalization

An extremely uncomfortable feeling of being an observer of one's own body or mental processes.

  • Feelings of unreality, detachment or unfamiliarity with parts of the self or the whole self.
  • Detachment from feelings, thoughts, body parts or sensations.

Derealization

The focus is on the outside world. The recurring feeling that one's surroundings are unreal or distant.

  • Feeling as though walking around in a fog, a bubble or a dream, or that an invisible veil stands between them and the rest of the world.
  • Visual distortions: blurriness, a widened or narrowed visual field, altered size of objects.
  • Auditory distortions: muting or heightening of sound.
CategoryDetail
CauseNot well understood. Possibly genetic and environmental factors. High levels of stress and fear may lead to episodes.
Risk factorsCertain personality traits, particularly traits that make a person avoid stressful situations or find it hard to describe emotional experience. Serious trauma as a child or adult, such as experiencing or witnessing violence or abuse. Severe stress from relationships, finances or work. Depression and anxiety, especially serious or long-lasting depression, and panic attacks. Substance misuse.
ComorbidityMajor depressive disorder. Anxiety disorders. The avoidant, borderline and obsessive-compulsive personality disorders.
TreatmentEpisodes are often short-lived and resolve on their own. Psychotherapy is the main treatment, either CBT or psychodynamic therapy. Self-hypnosis and repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation have also been used with success.
PharmacotherapyNo medication has been proven effective for this disorder. Medications may be used to treat comorbid disorders and symptoms such as depression or anxiety.

The goals of talk therapy are to help the person understand why the depersonalization or derealization is occurring, learn techniques that take their mind off the symptoms and reconnect them to the world and their feelings, and build coping strategies for times of extreme stress.
