Caring for Persons with Abuse

Types of Abuse and Key Concepts

Legal definitions of family or domestic violence vary from state to state, and 38 states place domestic violence definitions within criminal codes.

Type of AbuseDefinition and Examples
Physical abuseThe infliction of physical pain or bodily harm. Slapping, punching, choking, pushing, restraining, biting, throwing and burning.
Sexual abuseAny form of sexual contact or exposure without consent, or in circumstances in which the victim is incapable of giving consent. Also called sexual assault or rape.
Emotional abuseUndermining a person's self-worth. Constant criticism, humiliation, diminishing their abilities, name calling, intimidating, isolating and damaging relationships with others.
NeglectThe failure to provide for physical, emotional, educational and medical needs.
Economic abuseControlling a person's access to economic resources, making them financially dependent. May include forbidding school attendance or employment.
Key ConceptWhat It Means
Crisis situationAny situation that puts stress on a family with a violent member. A person with effective impulse control, problem-solving skills and a healthy support system is less likely to resort to violence. Stressful events tax coping skills, and social isolation from frequent moves or an inability to make friends adds to ineffective coping.
PerpetratorAny member of a household who is violent toward another member, including a parent, a sibling or an extended family member. Perpetrators consider their own needs more important than anyone else's, perceive themselves as having poor social skills, and lack supportive relationships outside the partnership.
Vulnerable personThe family member upon whom abuse is perpetrated.
SurvivorPreferred over victim. It recognizes the recovery and healing process that follows victimization and does not carry the connotation of passivity that victim does.

Intimate Partner Violence

Abuse within an intimate partner relationship where one partner asserts power and control over the other. It includes physical violence, rape, stalking and psychological aggression by a current or former partner, who may be a spouse, boyfriend, girlfriend, dating partner or ongoing sexual partner.

Epidemiology

  • 25% of women and 10% of men have experienced intimate partner violence during their lifetime.
  • 10% of women and 2% of men report having been stalked by an intimate partner.
  • Females between 18 and 34 have the highest rates.
  • It occurs in 50% of married couples.
Risk FactorWhat It Looks Like
Male perspective dominanceA belief in male dominance and the need to be in charge. Physically acting out makes the abuser feel more in control, masculine and powerful.
Pathological jealousyRefusing to let a partner work outside the home, or demanding they work in the same place to monitor them. Forbidding personal friends or outside activities. Monitoring an odometer and keeping stopwatches. Abusers still accuse their partners of infidelity, and controlling the finances limits freedom further.
Substance abuseAlcohol and other drugs weaken inhibitions and lead to disregard of social rules prohibiting violence. Even when drug and alcohol use is reduced or eliminated, family violence usually still occurs.
PregnancyThe partner may resent the added responsibility of a baby, or resent the relationship the baby will have with its mother.

Leaving Is the Most Dangerous Moment

Victims are at greatest risk for violence when they threaten to leave, or actually leave, the relationship. Violence also escalates when the person makes any move toward independence, such as visiting friends without permission, getting a job, or going back to school.

The Cycle of Violence

Walker describes a pattern of behavior perpetrators use to control their partners. It runs in three stages.

Tension-Building Stage

Begins with minor incidents: pushing, shoving, verbal abuse. The victim ignores or accepts the behavior for fear of escalation, and the abuser rationalizes that the behavior is acceptable. Both may try to reduce the tension, the abuser with alcohol or drugs, the victim by minimizing the importance of the incident.

Acute-Battering Stage

Occurs when the tension peaks. Usually triggered by an external event or by the abuser's emotional state. Some experts believe the victim may provoke the incident to remove the tension and fear and move on to the honeymoon phase.

Honeymoon Stage

A period of calm. The abuser shows kindness and loving behavior, at least initially feels remorseful and apologetic, and may bring presents and promise it will never happen again. The victim feels needed and loved and hopes for change.

Tension buildsViolence peaksCalm returns, then the cycle repeats

What Repetition Does

Legal proceedings or plans to leave that were started are often abandoned during the honeymoon stage. Without intervention the cycle repeats, and with each repeat:

  • The calm stages get briefer, and the periods of anger and fear grow more intense.
  • The victim's self-esteem erodes, and the victim either believes the violence was deserved or accepts the blame for it.
  • This leads to depression, hopelessness, immobilization and self-deprecation.

Older Adult Abuse

Intentional actions that cause harm, or create a risk of harm, to a vulnerable person. Mistreatment includes the failure to provide for the older adult's basic needs or to protect them from harm. Family members, a custodian or care facility personnel may inflict it, and financial abuse is an additional problem in this population: caretakers may steal cash or credit cards or coerce the older person to transfer property or accounts. All 50 states have enacted laws to prosecute older adult abuse.

Epidemiology

  • 1 in 10 adults older than 60 who live at home are victims of abuse.
  • For every case reported, five go unreported, because of isolation, dependency and fear of retaliation.
  • The older adult may be caring for themselves, creating potential for self-neglect.
  • It occurs in both institutional and family settings, and family members are the perpetrators in most incidents.
Risk FactorDetail
Poor mental or physical healthEither makes the older adult vulnerable.
Disruptive disordersSuch as Alzheimer's disease.
DependencyThe dependency needs of older adults are usually what put them at risk.
Families with a history of violenceCaring for an older adult is stressful even in the best cases, and where violence is already a family coping strategy the potential for abuse is high. Parents who abused their own children are more likely to become a target for abuse by those children.
The most typical victimFemale. Over 75. White. Living with a relative. Physically and/or mentally impaired, often with Alzheimer's disease.

Assessment and the Interview

Screen all patients. Victims of violence turn up in every healthcare setting, so knowing how to screen matters everywhere. Symptoms may be vague: chronic pain, insomnia, hyperventilation and gynecological problems.

The Interview Process and Setting

Interview the victim alone. Sit near the person and establish trust before focusing on details, because trust is what makes disclosure possible. Use open-ended questions, which are less threatening and elicit more information than closed yes-or-no questions: "Tell me about what happened to you." "Who takes care of you?" "What happens when you do something wrong?" Every assessment should elicit a history of sexual abuse, family violence and drug use.

Box 28.2 Interview Guidelines: Do

  • Conduct the interview in private.
  • Be direct, honest and professional.
  • Use language the patient understands, and ask them to clarify words you do not understand.
  • Be understanding and attentive, and let the person tell the story without interruption.
  • Inform the patient if you must refer to Children's or Adult Protective Services, and explain the process.
  • Assess safety and help reduce danger at discharge.

Box 28.2 Interview Guidelines: Do Not

  • Try to "prove" abuse by accusations or demands.
  • Display horror, anger, shock or disapproval of the perpetrator or the situation.
  • Place blame or make judgments.
  • Allow the patient to feel "at fault" or "in trouble."
  • Probe or press for answers the patient is not willing to give.
  • Conduct the interview with a group of interviewers.
  • Use words like "abuse" or "violence." Ask about conflict resolution instead.

Assessing for Each Type of Abuse

TypeWhat to Look For
PhysicalCovert signs are a series of minor complaints: headaches, back trouble, dizziness and accidents, especially falls. Overt signs are bruises, scars, burns and wounds in various stages of healing, on the head, face, chest, arms, abdomen, back, buttocks and genitalia (Box 28.3).
SexualSexualized behavior is one of the most common symptoms in children. Younger children may show precocious sexual knowledge, draw sexually explicit material, demonstrate sexual aggression, or act out sexual interactions in play. In older children, sexual promiscuity is one of the most common symptoms. Victims may also have PTSD symptoms such as nightmares, somatic complaints and guilt.
EmotionalMay exist on its own or alongside physical or sexual abuse, and is less obvious and harder to assess. Indicators: low self-esteem, feelings of inadequacy, anxiety, withdrawal, learning difficulties and poor impulse control.
NeglectNeglected children and older adults often appear undernourished, dirty and poorly clothed, with inadequate medical or dental care such as lack of immunizations and untreated conditions.
EconomicNeeds unmet when adequate funds are available. Unpaid bills left by the person in charge of the finances, leading to disconnected utilities, and extreme dependency.

When the Story Does Not Fit the Injury

Suspect abuse when the explanation does not match the injury seen, or when the patient minimizes the seriousness of the injury. Ask directly and non-threateningly whether someone close to them caused it, and observe nonverbal responses such as hesitation or lack of eye contact. Then ask specific questions: when was the last time it happened, how often does it happen, in what ways are you hurt. Inconsistent or vague explanations warrant further investigation.

In adults sexually abused as children, depression is the most commonly reported consequence, along with anxiety, risk for suicide, aggression, chronic low self-esteem, chronic pain, obesity, substance misuse, self-mutilation and PTSD.

AreaWhat to Assess

Confusion, shame, despair and powerlessness cause victims to withdraw, which increases isolation.
Support systemsThe person usually relies on the perpetrator for basic needs. That dependence, plus the isolation the perpetrator imposes, limits access to support. Assess interpersonal, intrapersonal and community-based resources.
Suicide and homicide potentialAssess both.
Drug and alcohol useA person experiencing violence may self-medicate to escape an intolerable situation. Assess for chronic alcohol or drug use and provide treatment referrals.

Priority Problems and Outcomes

Priority Problems

Safety is the number one concern.

  • Risk of violence and risk for suicide.
  • Anxiety, fear, hopelessness and powerlessness.
  • Chronic and situational low self-esteem, from negative messages and disrespectful treatment.
  • Individual impaired coping, producing deficits in managing day-to-day responsibilities.
  • Impaired family coping and impaired family process, the inability to achieve family functions and tasks and to handle tensions and stress.
  • Caregiver stress, which itself guides outcomes and interventions.

Outcomes

Abuse cessation is the top priority.

  • Physical abuse has ceased.
  • Emotional abuse has ceased.
  • Sexual abuse has ceased.
  • Financial exploitation has ceased.

Outcomes must be patient-centered, developed with the survivor and their primary support person, and continually reassessed and revised as new information arrives.

Planning, Interventions and Legal Duty

The nurse is often the first point of contact, and is ideally positioned to contribute to prevention, detection and intervention. The Joint Commission requires staff education in family violence and abuse and standards of care to guide practice. Unless a child has been removed from the home, most interventions after emergency care take place in the community. Plans put patient safety first, are discussed with the patient, and consider the needs of the abuser where that person is motivated to learn alternatives to violence.

Nurses Are Legally Mandated Reporters

Nurses are legally mandated to report suspected or actual cases of child and vulnerable adult abuse. The appropriate agency may be the state or a law enforcement agency, juvenile court or a county health department. Each state has its own guidelines, including whether the report must be oral, written or both, and the timeframe, such as immediately, within 24 hours, or within 48 hours.

Never Use a Family Member as an Interpreter

If the person does not speak English, provide a trained interpreter. Using a family member breaks confidentiality and exposes the person to future retaliation. With a language barrier, speak slowly and clearly without jargon and allow time for the person to respond.

Culture is central to how people organize their experience. Even the most acculturated person tends to revert to their cultural past when organizing coping strategies after a stressful event.

Counseling and the Safety Plan

Building a Safety Plan

A safety plan is a plan for rapid escape when abuse occurs. Counseling includes crisis intervention, and it is powerful to emphasize that people have a right to live without fear of violence. Telling an abused person that no one deserves to be hit can be a very powerful statement.

  • Ask the patient to identify signs of escalation of violence, and to pick one particular sign that means it is time to leave.
  • If children are present, agree on a code word that, when spoken by the parent, means it is time to go.
  • Plan where they will go and what transportation will get them out. Planning ahead may make it possible to leave before the violence occurs.
  • Shelters and safe houses are part of the plan.

Community Support and Teaching

Referrals and Teaching

  • Provide referral numbers, and stand by the patient as they make the calls. Refer to parenting resources, emergency financial assistance, legal counseling and vocational counseling.
  • Community mental health centers serve both victims and perpetrators, and nurses there coordinate community, medical, criminal justice and social services.
  • In families at risk, teaching helps them recognize the behaviors and situations that may trigger violence, so they can anticipate and prepare before a crisis occurs.

Prevention, Evaluation and Treatment Modalities

LevelDefinitionExamples
PrimaryMeasures taken to prevent abuse occurring.Reducing stress and the influence of risk factors. Increasing social supports, coping skills and self-esteem.
SecondaryEarly intervention in abusive situations.Screening programs for those at risk. Medical treatment for injuries. Coordination of community services.
TertiaryOccurs in mental health settings, where the nurse facilitates healing and rehabilitation.Counseling individuals and families. Support for groups of survivors. Helping survivors achieve their optimal level of safety, health and well-being. Legal advocacy programs. Complementary therapies such as mindfulness-based stress reduction.

Evaluate the big picture. Failures of intervention are often due to social, economic and political problems, so nurses can also direct interventions at the social environment.

Treatment Modalities

Nurses educated at the master's or doctoral level and certified as psychiatric mental health nurse practitioners are qualified to conduct specialized therapy. It is most effective after a crisis has occurred, when the situation is less chaotic. Individual, family and group therapy all exist both for those who are abused and for those who abuse. Groups often use CBT to help the perpetrator see abusive actions as behavioral patterns they can change.

Couples Therapy Can Increase the Danger

Couples therapy can put the abused partner at increased risk of harm or even death. Conjoint therapy should take place only if the perpetrator has had individual therapy and demonstrated change as a result, and only if both parties agree to participate.

Sexual Assault and Sexual Violence

Definitions

Sexual assault and sexual violence run from unwanted sexual advances and harassment through to rape.

Completed Rape

"Penetration, no matter how slight, of the vagina or anus with any body part or object, or oral penetration by a sex organ of another person, without the consent of the victim."

Attempted Rape

Threats of rape, or the intention to rape, that is unsuccessful.

TypeDetail
Stranger rapeThe perpetrator is unknown to the victim.
Spousal or marital rapePenetration of one's partner, spouse or significant other without consent. Historically, sexual intercourse was considered a marital right.
Statutory rapeAssumes minors do not have the capacity to consent, even if they agree to it or initiate it. In most states the age of consent is between 16 and 18 years old.
Date or acquaintance rapeA non-domestic relationship where the perpetrator is known to the victim. Common, with as many as 1 in 4 college women reporting such an occurrence.
Drug-facilitated sexual assaultDrug or alcohol intoxication resulting in the inability to give consent. Victims lose the ability to fight off their attackers, may develop amnesia and become unreliable witnesses. Most perpetrators are known to the victims.
Incest and human sex traffickingBoth are counted among the forms of sexual violence.

Epidemiology

Who Is Affected

  • Rape is the third most common violent crime in the United States, behind aggravated assault and robbery.
  • 19.3% of women and 1.7% of men are victims at some point in their lives.
  • The perpetrator is usually male, regardless of the victim's gender.
  • 50% of women who are raped are victimized by acquaintances.
  • 79% of female victims of completed rape are victimized by age 25.
  • Males are more likely to suffer physical trauma, to be victimized by multiple perpetrators, and to be raped in locked institutions.

Clinical Picture

There is no typical patient presentation, and emotional responses vary. Victims are traumatized both physically and emotionally. Long-term effects may include major depressive disorder, anxiety, fear, suicide, difficulties in daily functioning, low self-esteem, sexual dysfunction and somatic complaints.

Acute Stress Disorder and PTSD After Assault

  • Acute stress disorder: symptoms less than 1 month. Intrusive memories, dreams and flashbacks, negative thoughts, moods and feelings, avoidance of reminders, arousal with hyper-alertness, anxiety and angry outbursts, and dissociation that may leave the person unable to remember parts of the event.
  • PTSD: symptoms greater than 1 month. Depersonalization, detachment from one's own mental processes or body, described as living in a dream. Derealization, feeling that the world is dreamlike, distant or distorted.

Acute stress disorder, PTSD and depersonalization/derealization are taught in full in Trauma & Stressor Disorders

Victims of incest may experience a negative self-image, depression, eating disorders, personality disorders, self-destructive behaviors and substance misuse. In psychiatric patients, a history of sexual abuse is associated with depression, anxiety disorders, substance use disorders, suicide attempts, self-mutilation, compulsive sexual behavior and psychosis-like symptoms.

The Sexual Assault Nurse Examiner

SANE and SART

  • A SANE is a registered nurse with specialized training in caring for sexual assault patients, who has demonstrated competency in medical and legal evaluation and is capable of being an expert witness in court. The SANE uses the nursing process with a patient-centered approach.
  • The SANE is a member of the Sexual Assault Response Team, a multidisciplinary team that may include nurses, physicians, attorneys, social workers, advocates, other mental health professionals and forensic laboratory professionals.
  • There are shortages. If no SANE or specialty trained clinician is available, the nurse must provide both the medical and the legal aspects of care.
  • Nurses are instrumental in preserving evidence, which can lead to the prosecution of a crime or the exoneration of an innocent person of interest.

Nursing Care and Recovery After Sexual Assault

Initial evaluations may be extremely stressful to someone already traumatized. A police interview, repeated questioning and the physical examination all add to the trauma. A careful, patient-centered assessment is a form of advocacy that supports the patient through the process. Gather objective data from the patient and from the family or friends who accompany them.

Consent Is Required, and May Be Declined in Part

The patient has the right to decline all or part of the legal or medical examination. Informed consent must be provided and consent forms signed before taking photographs, performing a physical examination, or proceeding with any other procedure needed to collect evidence and provide treatment.

StepWhat It Involves

Self-assessmentNurses' attitudes influence the physical and psychological care a survivor receives. Knowing the myths and facts surrounding sexual assault raises awareness of your own beliefs. Examining your feelings about abortion also matters, because a patient may choose one if a pregnancy results from the rape. Do this before you meet the patient.

Planning, Intervention and Case Management

Planning and Timely Intervention

  • With no serious physical or psychological injury, treatment is provided and the patient is released. Because the ramifications last well beyond the acute phase, the plan includes follow-up care and community supports and how to access them.
  • Timely intervention is necessary and reduces the aftermath. Rape can constitute an acute situational crisis. To return to their previous level of functioning, survivors must fully mourn their losses and experience their anger.
  • Social support is tremendously beneficial. A survivor who can confide in one or two friends or family members right after the assault has fewer somatic manifestations of stress. Family and friends often need support as much as the survivor does.

When the Survivor Does Not Come In

  • A victim may be too traumatized, ashamed or afraid to go to the hospital, and cultural definitions of what constitutes rape may affect the decision to seek treatment.
  • RAINN, the Rape, Abuse and Incest National Network, runs a 24-hour telephone and chat line staffed by volunteers trained in rape crisis support. Counselors listen empathetically, encourage the survivor to go to the emergency department, and focus on immediate steps.

Discharge and Follow-Up

  • Give referral information in printed instructions, covering physical concerns, emotional reactions, legal matters, victim compensation and online resources. High anxiety limits what the patient retains verbally, and written material can be re-read.
  • Reassess the emotional state by telephone or personal contact within 24 to 48 hours of discharge, and discuss this with the patient beforehand.
  • Survivors may avoid mental health providers because of stigma, so an outpatient nurse should assess for stress-related symptoms or depression. Reporting symptoms and seeking treatment are adaptive coping behaviors and should be reinforced.

What Recovery Looks Like

A survivor is in recovery when relatively free of any signs or symptoms of acute stress disorder and PTSD: sleeping well with few nightmares, eating as they did before the rape, able to remain calm and relaxed, supported by family and friends, and with absent or only mild somatic reactions. They may also return to pre-rape sexual functioning and interest.

The measure of recovery. In general, the closer the survivor's lifestyle is to the pattern present before the rape, the more complete the recovery has been.

Define economic abuse.
Controlling a person's access to economic resources so they become financially dependent. It may include forbidding school attendance or employment.
What is a crisis situation in the context of family violence?
Any situation that puts stress on a family with a violent member. A person with effective impulse control, problem-solving skills and a healthy support system is less likely to resort to violence.
Why is "survivor" preferred over "victim"?
It recognizes the recovery and healing process that follows victimization, and it does not carry the connotation of passivity that "victim" does.
Name the three stages of the cycle of violence, in order.
The tension-building stage, the acute-battering stage, and the honeymoon stage.
What happens during the honeymoon stage?
The abuser is kind and loving, feels remorseful and apologetic, brings presents and promises it will never happen again. The victim feels needed and loved, and legal proceedings or plans to leave are often abandoned.
When is a victim of intimate partner violence at greatest risk for violence?
When they threaten to leave or actually leave the relationship. Violence also escalates with any move toward independence, such as getting a job or going back to school.
What goes into a safety plan?
A plan for rapid escape. The patient identifies signs of escalation and picks one that means it is time to leave, agrees a code word with the children, and plans where to go and what transportation will get them out.
Describe the most typical victim of older adult abuse.
Female, over 75, white, living with a relative, and physically and/or mentally impaired, often with Alzheimer's disease.
Compare primary, secondary and tertiary prevention of abuse.
Primary prevents abuse from occurring, by reducing stress and increasing supports, coping skills and self-esteem. Secondary is early intervention, by screening at-risk individuals and treating injuries. Tertiary occurs in mental health settings, facilitating healing and rehabilitation.
What is the nurse's legal duty when abuse is suspected?
Nurses are legally mandated to report suspected or actual cases of child and vulnerable adult abuse. Each state sets its own guidelines for whether the report is oral, written or both, and for the timeframe.
Define completed rape and attempted rape.
Completed rape is penetration, no matter how slight, of the vagina or anus with any body part or object, or oral penetration by a sex organ of another person, without the consent of the victim. Attempted rape is a threat or intention to rape that is unsuccessful.
What is a SANE, and which team does a SANE belong to?
A registered nurse with specialized training in caring for sexual assault patients, competent in medical and legal evaluation and able to be an expert witness in court. The SANE is a member of the Sexual Assault Response Team.
