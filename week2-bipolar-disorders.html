Bipolar & Related Disorders

Epidemiology & Risk Factors

FactorWhat Is Known
Commonly misdiagnosedUp to 21% of patients diagnosed with major depression have an undiagnosed bipolar disorder. First episodes are depressive, and more time is spent in the depressive phase than in mania or hypomania.
Bipolar IMore common in males; average age of onset 18.
Bipolar IIMore common in females; usually starts around age 20.

The Three Bipolar Disorders

The DSM-5 lists seven bipolar and related disorders — bipolar I, bipolar II, cyclothymic disorder, substance- or medication-induced, due to another medical condition, other specified, and unspecified. The three below are the focus. They differ in degree.

Cyclothymic Disorder

A mild mix of both poles. Fluctuating hypomanic and mild-to-moderate depressive symptoms, but no full episode of either.

Bipolar II

At least one hypomanic episode plus at least one major depressive episode. Never a manic episode.

Bipolar I

The most severe form, with the highest mortality rate of the three. At least one manic episode.

Specifiers

SpecifierWhat It Means
With mixed featuresSymptoms of both depression and mania at the same time. Rare; charted as, for example, "bipolar I disorder with mixed features."
With rapid cyclingFour or more manic episodes for at least 2 weeks in 12 months, with partial or full remission for 2 months at a time or a switch to the opposite episode. High risk of recurrence, resistant to drug therapy, greater severity of illness, and depressive symptoms predominate.
Rapid cycling is not mood lability. Rapid cycling is measured in episodes across a year; mood lability shifts every one or two hours and is more what you would see in borderline personality disorder.

The Manic Episode & Bipolar I

Mania is a medical emergency. It requires pharmacotherapy, and hospitalization is often necessary.

Diagnostic Criteria for Bipolar I

  • A. Abnormally and persistently elevated, expansive, or irritable mood and increased goal-directed activity or energy, for at least 1 week.
  • B. Three or more of the seven symptoms below — four if the mood is only irritable.
  • C. Symptoms cause marked impairment in social or occupational functioning, or require hospitalization to prevent harm to self or others, or there are psychotic features.
  • D. Not caused by substance use, medication, withdrawal, or a medical condition.
DIG FAST — the clinical features of mania and hypomania: Distractibility · Indiscretion · Grandiosity · Flight of ideas · Activity increase · Sleep deficit · Talkativeness.
FeatureWhat It Looks Like
DistractibilityAttention too easily drawn to unimportant external stimuli; multiple overlapping projects, often at unusual hours.
IndiscretionExcessive involvement in pleasurable activities with high potential for painful consequences: spending sprees, hypersexuality, gambling, profuse generosity. Also covers impaired judgment and impulse control, and the legal and social problems that follow.
GrandiosityInflated self-esteem, often to the point of delusion — a special relationship with a famous person, a special invention such as a cure for cancer, or religious grandiosity.
Flight of ideasRacing thoughts, or the subjective experience that thoughts are racing.
Activity increaseIncrease in goal-directed activity, or psychomotor agitation — purposeless, non-goal-directed activity and pacing.
Sleep deficitDecreased need for sleep — feeling rested after only three hours; or needing sleep but unable to get there because the mind is racing.
TalkativenessPressured speech: rapid, loud, very difficult to interrupt. They will spontaneously approach and talk to strangers, which makes assessment hard.

What Bipolar I Looks Like Over Time

  • At least one manic episode, usually alternating with major depressive and/or hypomanic episodes.
  • Classically there are psychotic features — delusions and hallucinations. They may be absent over a lifetime, but that would be very unusual. Psychotic features place the diagnosis in bipolar I: a hypomanic episode with psychosis is by definition a manic episode.
  • The more manic episodes occur, the more intense they become, and the highs mirror the lows in depth and intensity — which is why medication adherence matters so much.

Behaviors of Mania

  • Mood lability: extreme mood swings, with irritability or sudden outbursts of misplaced rage.
  • Demanding and manipulative — set limits in a firm but neutral manner.
  • Low frustration tolerance; feels misunderstood; a great deal of pacing.
  • Dramatic mannerisms; jokes and puns; flamboyant or sexually suggestive dress.
  • May not slow down enough to eat or drink — watch nutrition and hydration.

Hypomania, Bipolar II & Cyclothymia

Manic Episode

  • At least 1 week of elevated, expansive, or irritable mood with increased activity or energy.
  • Marked impairment in social or occupational functioning, or hospitalization required.
  • Psychotic features may be present.

Hypomanic Episode

  • At least 4 consecutive days, present most of the day, nearly every day.
  • An unequivocal change in functioning that is uncharacteristic of the person and observable by others.
  • Not severe enough to cause marked impairment or to require hospitalization.
  • No psychotic features — if there are any, the episode is by definition manic.

Bipolar II

  • Presence or history of at least one major depressive episode and at least one hypomanic episode, and never a manic episode.
  • Impairment in functioning in at least one area. No psychosis with hypomania, but psychosis may occur during the depressive phase.
  • Patients present during the depressive phase — they tend to like the hypomanic phase because they get more done, so they do not complain about it. Getting a history of ups and downs, plus collateral from family and friends, is what separates this from a depressive disorder.
  • Impulsivity is a strong symptom in bipolar disorders and leads to suicide attempts.

Cyclothymic Disorder

  • Present for at least 2 years in adults, 1 year in children and adolescents.
  • Multiple hypomanic symptoms but not a hypomanic episode; depressive symptoms but no major depressive, manic, or mixed episode.
  • Symptoms present more than half the time, and never absent for more than 2 months at a time.
  • Not as deep or as high as bipolar I or II, but more persistent. Rapid cycling is possible.
  • 50% risk of subsequently developing bipolar I or bipolar II.

Assessment & Nursing Care

AreaWhat You Assess and Document
MoodMania — expansive, elevated, euphoric, or unrestrained; hypomania; depression; lability, where the mood switches back and forth.
BehaviorAgitated, manipulative, restless, pacing.
Thought processesLoose associations — thoughts only loosely connected. Flight of ideas — continuous accelerated speech with abrupt changes from topic to topic. Circumstantial — talks off subject but eventually returns to the point. Tangential — like circumstantial, but never gets back to the point.
Thought contentGrandiose, paranoid, and persecutory delusions.
Speech patternsPressured, circumstantial, and clang associations — rhyming.
Cognitive functioningDeficits possible, as in schizophrenia and severe mood disorders.

Assessment Guidelines

  • Danger to self or others, and the need for protection from uninhibited behaviors. Poor impulse control leads to giving away belongings or money and to gambling debts — social work or guardianship may be needed to avoid bankruptcy.
  • Need for hospitalization — are they unstable?
  • Medical status and coexisting conditions. In mania they do not stop to eat, drink, or sleep, so a thorough medical examination is required.
  • The patient's and family's understanding of the disorder, the signs of relapse, and available support groups and resources.

Nursing Care for Acute Mania

The primary outcome in acute mania is injury prevention. Through the first few days the focus is the patient’s degree of exhaustion and nutrition — they have been going for days on little sleep, food, or fluid, and that outranks demanding or impulsive behavior. The patient will be well hydrated, maintain stable cardiac status, maintain or obtain tissue integrity, get sufficient sleep and rest, demonstrate thought self-control with the aid of staff or medication, and make no attempt at self-harm.

ActionHow It Is Done
Manage medicationsGive them on time, and use PRN medication when agitation is starting.
Decrease physical activityNo gym, treadmill, or exercise bike. Walking with a staff member is the right activity — it allows supervision and redirection with minimal energy expenditure. Puzzles are too frustrating, and games like bingo or volleyball are overstimulating or too strenuous.
Increase food and fluid intakeFinger foods and high-protein foods they can eat on the go, in larger amounts and more frequently, with fluids always on hand — they will not slow down to sit and eat.
Ensure sleepAt least 4 to 6 hours per night; patients in mania may go days without sleeping.
Intervene for self-care and set limitsPrompt and supply what is needed for showering and grooming. Foot care — constant pacing, often without shoes, produces blisters and calluses. Seclusion and restraint are sometimes required to set limits on behavior.

Patient & Family Teaching

  • Bipolar disorder is chronic and episodic and there is no cure, but recovery is possible. Treatment requires one or more mood-stabilizing agents taken for a long time.
  • Teach the expected side effects and toxic effects of the medication, and whom to call and where to go for an adverse reaction. Emergency phone numbers are kept somewhere easily accessed.
  • Teach the signs and symptoms of relapse, which may come "out of the blue," and the family's role in preventing a full relapse.
  • Alcohol, drugs of abuse, caffeine — particularly energy drinks — and OTC medications can cause a relapse.
  • Good sleep hygiene is critical to stability. Lack of sleep is frequently the earliest symptom of a manic episode, and mania can sometimes be averted by using sleep medication early.
  • Coping strategies lower stress, enhance a sense of personal control, and increase community functioning.
  • Group and individual therapy build insight and relapse-prevention skills, provide social support, improve medication adherence, and reduce hospitalizations.

Medications for Mania & Mood Stabilization

The mood stabilizers and antipsychotics below are also gathered by class, with the other Week 2 drugs, in Medications

Treating an acute manic episode is not the same as maintenance treatment for bipolar disorder. An acute episode gets something heavier added on top of the maintenance regimen — usually an antipsychotic — and that addition comes back off once the mania is controlled.

Do not give an antidepressant alone to treat the depressive symptoms of bipolar disorder — it can throw the patient into mania. Mood stabilizers are the first medications used.
SituationWhat Is Used
Acute, severe maniaA combination: lithium plus an antipsychotic, or divalproex/valproate plus an antipsychotic — for example lithium with risperidone, or divalproex with olanzapine. Once the mania is controlled, the lithium continues and the antipsychotic is stopped.
Less severe maniaBordering on hypomania, lithium or divalproex alone may be enough.
Severe agitationBenzodiazepines — diazepam, lorazepam, clonazepam. They calm agitation and reduce aggression, panic, and insomnia, but they are sedative-hypnotics and addictive, so they are used only when necessary and only short-term.
Predominantly depressed Symbyax, a combination of the second-generation antipsychotic olanzapine and the SSRI fluoxetine — used, for example, in bipolar II where full mania does not occur.
Second-generation antipsychoticsolanzapine · risperidone · aripiprazole (for use over age 10) · quetiapine · ziprasidone · lurasidone.

Lithium Carbonate

PropertyDetail
IndicationsAcute mania and maintenance treatment. The most widely used mood stabilizer; effective in about 40% of patients with bipolar disorder.
Therapeutic level0.6 to 1.4 mEq/L.
Toxic level1.5 mEq/L and above.
Dosing1200–3000 mg/day; start low and titrate up.
Onset5 to 7 days, and may take as long as 2 weeks — faster than the antidepressants.
ActionUncertain. Crosses cell membranes, alters sodium transport, and is not protein-bound.
ContraindicationsCardiovascular disease · renal disease · brain damage · thyroid disease · pregnant or breastfeeding mothers.
MonitoringLabs 1–2 times per week at first, then creatinine, thyroid hormones, and CBC every 6 months. Kidney damage is a real risk when levels go unmonitored. Thyroid function may decrease, usually after 6–18 months — watch for dry skin, hair loss, constipation, bradycardia, and cold intolerance.

Teaching Points

  • Do not restrict sodium — maintain a normal, steady sodium intake. Losing fluid means losing sodium, which increases the potential for lithium toxicity. Keeping intake steady is also what makes the blood levels interpretable.
  • Do not pre-load sodium before habitual activity. A daily workout or an outdoor job is already the baseline the monitored levels reflect. Increase sodium ahead of an unaccustomed exertion — a sedentary patient going hiking, or running a marathon untrained.
  • Hydrate. A child may need to take bottled water to school. Hydrate more than usual before strenuous activity that causes sweating.
  • Contact the provider if illness causes fluid loss, such as vomiting from a stomach bug.
  • Intermittent blood tests are required — keep the appointments.
  • Increased risk of suicidal ideation when lithium is discontinued — assess for symptoms of depression.
Coarse hand tremor, slurred speech, ataxia, confusion, or GI upset in a patient on lithium: hold the next dose and request a lithium level. Do not treat these as an expected early reaction that will subside.

Anticonvulsant Mood Stabilizers

DrugWhat to Know
valproate / divalproex
(sodium valproate, divalproex sodium, valproic acid)		Check serum levels. Broader spectrum of efficacy against bipolar symptoms and longer periods of mood stabilization than the alternatives. All three names appear in practice for variations of the same medication.
carbamazepineCheck serum levels. Risk of low white blood cell counts — check a CBC at the start and every 6 months — and monitor hepatic and renal function. Used when there has been no response to lithium, or with secondary breakthrough mania. Rash may be life-threatening.
lamotrigineFor rapid cycling and the depressed phase of bipolar illness; used as add-on therapy in refractory mood disorders. Usually tried after lithium fails. Rash may be life-threatening.
gabapentinEffective for acute mania, mood stabilization, and rapid cycling.
topiramateAlso used as an anticonvulsant mood stabilizer.
Any rash on lamotrigine: stop the medication immediately and call the provider. It usually appears first on the chest or trunk. It is an allergic reaction that can advance to Stevens-Johnson syndrome — flu-like symptoms, sore throat, fever, chills, blisters, burning eyes — and then to toxic epidermal necrolysis, where the top layer of skin separates, which can be fatal. It usually occurs in the first few months, but can happen at any time, including after years on the drug and with dose changes. Stopping the medication almost always resolves it. Carbamazepine, phenobarbital, phenytoin (Dilantin), Bactrim, gabapentin, and methotrexate can do the same thing.

ECT, Safety & Advanced Practice

Electroconvulsive Therapy

  • Used to subdue severe manic behavior.
  • Useful in treatment-resistant mania and in rapid cycling — four or more episodes of illness per year.
  • More effective than drug-based therapy for treatment-resistant bipolar depression, and indicated for depressive episodes that are severe, catatonic, or treatment-resistant.

Teamwork, Safety & Support

  • An interprofessional staff working together minimizes staff splitting.
  • A great deal of limit setting; seclusion or restraint for hyperactivity and impulsivity.
  • Support groups through the Depression and Bipolar Support Alliance and NAMI.
  • Health teaching and health promotion.

Advanced Practice Interventions

  • Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT).
  • Interpersonal and social rhythm therapy.
  • Family-focused therapy.

How long must the mood disturbance last for a manic episode versus a hypomanic episode?
At least 1 week for mania; at least 4 consecutive days for hypomania.
What single finding makes an episode manic rather than hypomanic?
Psychotic features — with them, the episode is by definition manic. Marked impairment in functioning or a need for hospitalization does the same.
What is required for a diagnosis of bipolar II?
At least one hypomanic episode and at least one major depressive episode, and never a manic episode.
What is the therapeutic blood level for lithium, and at what level is it toxic?
Therapeutic 0.6 to 1.4 mEq/L; toxic at 1.5 mEq/L and above.
Name the early signs of lithium toxicity.

Should a patient on lithium restrict sodium?
No — maintain a normal, steady sodium intake and stay hydrated. Losing fluid means losing sodium, which increases the risk of lithium toxicity.

It can throw the patient into mania. Mood stabilizers are used first.
What must a patient taking lamotrigine report immediately?

Injury prevention.
