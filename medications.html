Antidepressant Principles

All antidepressants increase the availability of neurotransmitters and have similar efficacy in trials. They differ in adverse effects, cost, and safety. Psychotherapy combined with an antidepressant is superior to either alone.

Physiological symptoms improve before psychological symptoms. Energy returns while mood is still depressed, and that is exactly when the risk of acting on suicidal thoughts is highest. Teach patients, caregivers, and family members this.

General Education

  • Symptom improvement may not appear for 4–6 weeks.
  • Look first for improved sleep, less daytime fatigue, less crying and irritability, and increased frustration tolerance.
  • Side effects are usually handled by adjusting the dose or switching to another drug in the same class.
  • Do not stop when feeling better. That causes relapse. Medication is needed 6–9 months past symptom relief, so most patients take it 12–24 months before tapering.
  • Antidepressants are not addictive, but stopping abruptly causes withdrawal: nausea, anxiety, insomnia, flu-like symptoms.
  • No alcohol. Alcohol is a stronger depressant than any available antidepressant.

In Children & Teens

  • SSRIs carry a black box warning for increased risk of suicidality: suicidal thinking or attempts.
  • After the warning, prescribing fell and suicide attempts increased: the risk of suicide is greater in depressed children and adolescents who do not take antidepressants.
  • fluoxetine is first-line. Venlafaxine is used off label. Both have low side effects: mild nausea, headache, stomachache.
  • Full effect can take up to 12 weeks. Usually given for 6–24 months.
  • TCAs are avoided: more side effects, potential for dysrhythmias, lethal in overdose, and children and adolescents can be more impulsive.

Order of Drug Classes Tried

SSRIs / SRIs

First-line. If one fails, a second drug in the same class is tried before moving on.

Tricyclics (TCAs)

Second choice. Cheaper, but anticholinergic, cardiotoxic, and lethal in overdose.

MAOIs

Third choice. Reserved for treatment-resistant depression because of the food and drug restrictions.

← safest, tried firstmost restricted, tried last →

SSRIs & SNRIs

Drugs

  • SSRIs, selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors: fluoxetine · paroxetine · sertraline · citalopram · escitalopram
  • SNaRIs, serotonin noradrenergic reuptake inhibitors: venlafaxine · duloxetine
  • SNRI, serotonin norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor: desvenlafaxine
CategoryDetail
IndicationFirst-line therapy for depression. Block the neuronal uptake of serotonin, leaving more in the synaptic cleft. Good for depression with anxiety or with psychomotor agitation. Also treat anxiety and obsessive-compulsive disorders. SNaRIs act on both serotonin and norepinephrine and usually carry a few more side effects.
Side Effects & What to DoDry mouth: increase fluids, sugar-free gum. Sexual problems: reduced desire, difficulty reaching orgasm, or inability to maintain an erection, in up to 80%. Headache and blurred vision: symptomatic care. These four are the most common. Drowsiness: dose at night. Nausea: with food or at bedtime. Insomnia: dose in the morning. Diarrhea: increase fiber. Nervousness, agitation, restlessness, dizziness: relaxation techniques, and avoid caffeine if anxious.
Adverse EventsSerotonin syndrome: rare but potentially life-threatening. See below. Abrupt discontinuation may be mild or severe: "electrical surges" or "brain shivers" in the head or body, pins-and-needles sensations on the skin, blackouts, short-term memory loss, and feeling on the verge of unconsciousness.
Monitoring & TeachingEffectiveness takes 4–8 weeks. Most side effects diminish in 4–6 weeks. Monitor for suicidal ideation, extreme agitation, fever, increased blood pressure, and manic symptoms. Teach sleep hygiene and relaxation techniques.

Serotonin Syndrome

Rare but potentially life-threatening. 60% of patients who develop it do so within 6 hours of a first dose, a change in dosing, or an intentional overdose. If the serotonergic medication is not discontinued it progresses to worsening myoclonus, hypertension, rigor, acidosis, respiratory failure, and rhabdomyolysis, and it can be fatal through hyperpyrexia or cardiovascular shock.
SystemSymptoms
Mental statusAgitation, confusion, restlessness, lethargy, delirium, irritability, dizziness, hallucinations.
AutonomicDiaphoresis, flushing, fever, tachycardia, mydriasis.
NeuromuscularMyoclonus (muscle twitching or jerks), hyperreflexia, tremors.
GastrointestinalNausea, vomiting, diarrhea.

Treatment

  • Stop or reduce the contributing medications, depending on symptom severity and as directed by the prescriber. This is the first action.
  • Benzodiazepines (diazepam or lorazepam) for agitation, seizures, and muscle stiffness.
  • Avoid antipsychotics such as haloperidol for the agitation. They can worsen hyperthermia and contribute to a neuroleptic malignant syndrome–like reaction. The agitation here is an internal restlessness, not outward aggression.
  • Oxygen to maintain normal levels. IV fluids for dehydration and fever. Symptomatic and supportive care.
  • Tachycardia or hypertension: esmolol or nitroprusside. Hypotension: phenylephrine or epinephrine.
  • If other treatments are not working, a serotonin-production blocker: cyproheptadine HCl.
  • Symptoms usually resolve within 24 hours, but can take several weeks to clear completely.

Tricyclic Antidepressants

Deadly in overdose, through fatal CNS depression, and equally so combined with alcohol or other CNS depressants. The cardiovascular effects are the serious ones: dysrhythmias, tachycardia, MI, and heart block.

Drugs

  • imipramine
  • doxepin
  • amitriptyline
CategoryDetail
IndicationSecond choice for MDD after the SSRIs. Block the reuptake of norepinephrine and serotonin at the presynaptic neuron in the CNS, which is thought to elevate mood. Much less expensive than SRIs, and returning to use after several failed SSRI trials.
Side Effects & What to DoDry mouth: sugar-free hard candy or gum. GI upset: take with food. Diarrhea: frequent small meals. Constipation: increase fiber, fluids, exercise. Insomnia: sleep hygiene, take in the morning. Orthostasis: stay hydrated, get up slowly. Sexual side effects: erectile dysfunction medications. Urinary hesitancy: run water while voiding, measure the amount.
Adverse EventsDysrhythmias, tachycardia, MI, and heart block. Alpha-adrenergic blockade produces orthostatic hypotension and tachycardia. Dizziness and falls are the worry in older adults. Urinary retention or severe constipation needs immediate medical attention.
ContraindicationsAfter an MI or with other cardiovascular disease · narrow-angle glaucoma · any history of seizures · pregnancy. Considered risky in older adults and in anyone with cardiac disease. Avoided in children and adolescents.
Monitoring & TeachingCheck an ECG for cardiac disorder before initiating, and check for a history of seizure disorder. Results take 10–14 days to appear and full effects 4–8 weeks. Start low and go slow. Often given at night. The sedation aids sleep and the side effects fall during sleeping hours. Warn about early morning awakening, feeling worse in the morning, worry and anxiety early in treatment. These ease as the day goes on.
Anticholinergic side effects. Hot as a hare: increased temperature · Dry as a bone: dry mouth and eyes, urinary retention, constipation · Blind as a bat: mydriasis, dilated pupils, blurred vision · Red as a beet: flushed face · Mad as a hatter: confusion, delirium. Also sedation and weight gain. Usually not serious and often transitory.

MAOIs

Drugs

  • phenelzine
  • tranylcypromine
  • selegiline (Emsam), a transdermal patch, with far fewer dietary restrictions and drug interactions
CategoryDetail
IndicationUsually the third choice for depression, reserved for treatment-resistant cases. Block monoamine oxidase, the enzyme that metabolizes serotonin, norepinephrine, dopamine, and tyramine, so the mood-elevating neurotransmitters increase. Also used for panic disorder, social phobias, generalized anxiety disorders, and bulimia. Effective for unconventional depression: a depression that can lift with positive news or events, where the arms and legs feel heavy as lead, with oversleeping and overeating.
Side Effects & What to DoCommon: dry mouth · nausea · diarrhea or constipation · headache · drowsiness · insomnia · dizziness or lightheadedness · skin reaction at the patch site. Less common: involuntary muscle jerks · low blood pressure · reduced sexual desire or orgasm · weight gain · difficulty starting urine flow · muscle cramps · paresthesia.
Adverse EventsHypertensive crisis after a contraindicated substance. See below. Serotonin syndrome when combined with an SSRI.
ContraindicationsTyramine-rich foods. See the callout below. Avoid Demerol, and be very careful with OTC cold medications. Rarely used with an SSRI. The combination commonly causes serotonin syndrome. Limit caffeine, chocolate, and avocados.
Monitoring & TeachingMonitor blood pressure routinely, especially during the first 6 weeks. Crisis occurs 15–90 minutes after ingesting a contraindicated substance.

Hypertensive Crisis

Tyramine-rich foods to avoid. Aged, smoked, fermented, marinated, and processed meats (pepperoni, bologna, salami, pickled herring, liver, frankfurters, bacon, ham) · aged cheeses (blue, cheddar, provolone, brie, Romano, parmesan, swiss) · overripe fruits and vegetables (bananas, sauerkraut) · beans (fava, Italian, Chinese pea pod, tofu) · condiments (soy sauce, bouillon cubes, meat tenderizers) · beer, ales, liquors, red wine, and non-alcoholic wine or beer.
StageSigns
EarlyIrritability, anxiety, flushing, sweating, and a severe headache.
EstablishedOccipital headache, palpitations, nausea and vomiting, hypertension but with orthostatic hypotension on standing, neck stiffness or soreness, dilated pupils, photophobia, blurred vision, chest pain, tachycardia or bradycardia, disturbed cardiac rate and rhythm, dyspnea, mental status changes.

Treatment

  • phentolamine, an alpha-adrenergic blocker and vasodilator.
  • Sublingual nifedipine, a calcium channel blocker that relaxes cardiac muscle.
  • Symptomatic and supportive care.

Lithium Carbonate

Coarse hand tremor, slurred speech, ataxia, confusion, or GI upset in a patient on lithium: hold the next dose and request a lithium level. Do not treat these as an expected early reaction that will subside.
CategoryDetail
IndicationAcute mania and maintenance treatment. The most widely used mood stabilizer. Effective in about 40% of patients with bipolar disorder. Action uncertain: crosses cell membranes, alters sodium transport, and is not protein-bound.
Levels & DosingTherapeutic 0.6 to 1.4 mEq/L. Toxic 1.5 mEq/L and above. 1200–3000 mg/day. Start low and titrate up. Onset 5 to 7 days, and may take as long as 2 weeks, faster than the antidepressants.
Adverse EventsKidney damage is a real risk when levels go unmonitored. Thyroid function may decrease, usually after 6–18 months. Watch for dry skin, hair loss, constipation, bradycardia, and cold intolerance. Increased risk of suicidal ideation when lithium is discontinued.
ContraindicationsCardiovascular disease · renal disease · brain damage · thyroid disease · pregnant or breastfeeding mothers.
Monitoring & TeachingLabs 1–2 times per week at first, then creatinine, thyroid hormones, and CBC every 6 months. Do not restrict sodium. Maintain a normal, steady intake. Losing fluid means losing sodium, which increases the potential for toxicity, and steady intake is what makes the blood levels interpretable. Hydrate, and more than usual before strenuous activity that causes sweating. Do not pre-load sodium before habitual activity, since a daily workout or an outdoor job is already the baseline the monitored levels reflect. But increase it ahead of an unaccustomed exertion, such as a sedentary patient going hiking. Contact the provider if illness causes fluid loss. Keep the blood-test appointments.

Used With

  • Acute, severe mania: lithium plus an antipsychotic: for example lithium with risperidone. Once the mania is controlled the lithium continues and the antipsychotic is stopped.
  • Less severe mania: bordering on hypomania, lithium alone may be enough.
  • Suicidal ideation: low-dose lithium, about 25 mg PO daily for a few weeks.

Anticonvulsant Mood Stabilizers

Any rash on lamotrigine: stop the medication immediately and call the provider. It usually appears first on the chest or trunk. It is an allergic reaction that can advance to Stevens-Johnson syndrome (flu-like symptoms, sore throat, fever, chills, blisters, burning eyes) and then to toxic epidermal necrolysis, where the top layer of skin separates, which can be fatal. It usually occurs in the first few months, but can happen at any time, including after years on the drug and with dose changes. Stopping the medication almost always resolves it. Carbamazepine, phenobarbital, phenytoin (Dilantin), Bactrim, gabapentin, and methotrexate can do the same thing.
DrugWhat to Know
valproate / divalproex
(sodium valproate, divalproex sodium, valproic acid)		Check serum levels. Broader spectrum of efficacy against bipolar symptoms and longer periods of mood stabilization than the alternatives. All three names appear in practice for variations of the same medication.
carbamazepineCheck serum levels. Risk of low white blood cell counts. Check a CBC at the start and every 6 months, and monitor hepatic and renal function. Used when there has been no response to lithium, or with secondary breakthrough mania. Rash may be life-threatening.
lamotrigineFor rapid cycling and the depressed phase of bipolar illness. Used as add-on therapy in refractory mood disorders. Usually tried after lithium fails. Rash may be life-threatening.
gabapentinEffective for acute mania, mood stabilization, and rapid cycling.

Do not give an antidepressant alone to treat the depressive symptoms of bipolar disorder. It can throw the patient into mania. Mood stabilizers are the first medications used.

Opioid Overdose Reversal

Drugs

  • naloxone
CategoryDetail
IndicationReverses an opioid overdose: an opioid antagonist that displaces the opioid from its receptor. Also used with buprenorphine as a combination product for opioid use disorder. Do not confuse it with naltrexone, the recovery-phase antagonist that reduces cravings.
Adverse EventsSends the patient into instant withdrawal by throwing the opioid off the receptor.
Monitoring & TeachingAvailable as a nasal spray or injection: intranasal, IM, sub-q or IV. Its half-life is shorter than that of opioids, so keep watching and repeat the dose if signs return. Naloxone kits go home with patients at risk of overdose, and family or friends are taught the signs of severe intoxication and impending overdose.
Opioid overdose is an emergency. Outside hospital, call 9-1-1 and give naloxone if available. In hospital, promote breathing first: aspirate secretions, insert an airway, ventilate. Then give naloxone. The cardinal signs are coma, pinpoint pupils and respiratory depression.

Medication-Assisted Treatment: Opioid Use Disorder

Drugs

  • Opioid agonists: methadone · buprenorphine · buprenorphine & naloxone
  • Antagonist: naltrexone
  • Alpha agonist: clonidine
CategoryDetail
IndicationMethadone: the first medication marketed for opioid withdrawal. Acts like heroin, morphine and fentanyl but far more slowly, flattening the highs and the withdrawal lows. Buprenorphine: long-acting and slow, reducing cravings and withdrawal so the patient can focus on recovery. Buprenorphine & naloxone: produces a weaker euphoria than heroin or methadone, relieves cravings and withdrawal symptoms, and reduces the mortality of opioid use disorder by 50% by reducing the risk of overdose. Naltrexone: blocks the effects of opioids and alcohol, reducing cravings and preventing relapse. Clonidine: relieves withdrawal symptoms, especially heart rate and blood pressure, and the anxiety and agitation common in withdrawal from alcohol, opioids or nicotine. Used alongside drugs like methadone.
Adverse EventsMethadone carries significant overdose potential and dependence is still possible. Buprenorphine does not carry methadone's overdose potential.
ContraindicationsNaltrexone requires 10 to 14 days opioid-free before the first dose. Sooner precipitates a rapid, severe withdrawal.
Monitoring & TeachingMethadone must be monitored closely, with daily clinic visits. For replacement therapy, stabilize the patient first, then taper slowly until off methadone to prevent more severe withdrawal. Naltrexone does not treat withdrawal symptoms. Clonidine works by blocking the neurotransmitters that trigger sympathetic activity.

Buprenorphine Alone

  • Preferred for induction: starting treatment under the supervision of a healthcare provider.

Buprenorphine & Naloxone

  • The usual switch after initial treatment, once the medication is no longer taken under direct supervision.
  • Naloxone is a safety feature that discourages dissolving and injecting the medication.
  • Naloxone is absorbed only if the medication is injected instead of dissolved in the mouth as directed, and then it causes uncomfortable withdrawal symptoms.

In pregnancy: maintenance decreases dose variability and limits exposure to dirty needles and unknown additives. Dosage may be titrated upward as pregnancy progresses, with twice-daily dosing as an option, titrated against opioid withdrawal scoring, and maintained until delivery. The fetus experiences withdrawal 2–3 times as intense as the mother's, and can die from it. Buprenorphine/naloxone has largely replaced methadone as the standard.

Benzodiazepines for Alcohol Withdrawal

Drugs

  • Benzodiazepines: diazepam · lorazepam

In DTs: seizures → IV diazepam · acute agitation, tremors, impending or acute DTs, hallucinosis → oral diazepam · delirium appears → IV lorazepam · elevated BP → clonidine · dehydration → oral or IV fluids.

CategoryDetail
IndicationAlcohol and sedative-hypnotic withdrawal, given at a CIWA-Ar score of 8 to 10 or more. Goals: control agitation, decrease the risk of seizures, and reduce morbidity and mortality.
Monitoring & TeachingThe patient may be on a fixed, scheduled regimen with PRN doses for breakthrough autonomic symptoms: for example diazepam 10 mg every 6 hours for four doses, then 5 mg every 6 hours for eight doses. Diazepam is the current standard at UK HealthCare because its long half-life works better and it is particularly effective at decreasing seizures. Rescore with the CIWA whenever the patient reports worsening symptoms.
Delirium tremens is the most severe form of alcohol withdrawal and can result in death. Prevention is the goal: assess and monitor symptoms, labs, electrolytes and vital signs, hydrate, medicate adequately, and provide life-sustaining measures if indicated.

Medication-Assisted Treatment: Alcohol Use Disorder

Drugs

  • naltrexone: also used in opioid use disorder
  • disulfiram
  • acamprosate

A combination of medication with individual and behavioral therapies is what sustains recovery.

CategoryDetail
IndicationNaltrexone: blocks the euphoric effects of opioids and alcohol, so relapse produces no high, and reduces alcohol cravings. Long-acting, given IM. Disulfiram: prevents the breakdown of acetaldehyde, so drinking while taking it produces severely unpleasant flu-like illness. It does not reduce cravings. Acamprosate: reduces cravings and helps maintain abstinence.
Adverse EventsDisulfiram plus any alcohol produces severe flu-like illness.
Monitoring & TeachingNone of the three treats withdrawal symptoms. All are recovery-phase medications. Disulfiram teaching: alcohol is not only in drinks. Patients must avoid being near, touching or consuming alcohol-based products — perfumes, aftershaves, toners, mouthwash, many liquid medications (especially nighttime formulas) and paint thinners. Even splashing on aftershave can make them severely ill. Alongside these, treat withdrawal symptoms symptomatically and replace thiamine daily.

Thiamine & Wernicke-Korsakoff Syndrome

Drugs

  • thiamine: vitamin B1
CategoryDetail
IndicationReplaced daily in alcohol use disorder: the standard regimen is a multivitamin, thiamine and folic acid. Treats Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome, a neurological disorder caused by lack of thiamine (B1). Chronic alcohol use decreases intestinal absorption of thiamine by about 70%.
Monitoring & TeachingWernicke's encephalopathy: large doses, IV, 2–3 times daily for 1–2 weeks, typically about 10 days. Acute and reversible. May clear in a few weeks or progress. Korsakoff syndrome: thiamine replacement for 3–12 months, varying with symptoms, severity and whether psychosis is present. Chronic and not typically reversible, with a recovery rate of only about 20%. Rule out other causes of thiamine deficiency: eating disorders, bariatric surgery, poor nutrition, chronic infection, bowel surgery.

Typical Antipsychotics

Drugs

  • chlorpromazine
  • haloperidol
  • haloperidol decanoate, the long-acting injectable form
CategoryDetail
IndicationSchizophrenia and schizoaffective disorder. Primarily treat the positive symptoms, with little effect on the negative symptoms. Used less often than atypicals for that reason, but as effective as newer agents for positive symptoms and much less expensive. 2 to 6 weeks to become effective.
Side Effects & What to DoExtrapyramidal side effects are the most common and distressing problem, and the leading driver of non-adherence. Also anticholinergic effects, sedation, weight gain, sexual dysfunction, endocrine disturbance and orthostatic hypotension. Treat EPS with a dose reduction or medication change, and with an anticholinergic agent such as benztropine or trihexyphenidyl.
Adverse EventsNeuroleptic malignant syndrome · tardive dyskinesia · metabolic syndrome · cardiac arrhythmias · an increased risk of seizures.
Monitoring & TeachingAssess with the AIMS at least every 3 months for tardive dyskinesia. Monotherapy is recommended, with patient-specific dose adjustment. Teach that the class is unlikely to be lethal in overdose and is not addictive, and that stopping the medication almost certainly leads to relapse. Short-acting injectable forms intensify side effects and are harder to manage.

Atypical Antipsychotics

Drugs

  • clozapine
  • olanzapine
  • quetiapine
  • risperidone, also a long-acting injectable
  • aripiprazole
  • paliperidone palmitate, a long-acting injectable
CategoryDetail
IndicationSchizophrenia and schizoaffective disorder. Treat both positive and negative symptoms, which is why they are used more often than typicals. Risperidone and aripiprazole are the agents used in children and adolescents. 2 to 6 weeks to become effective.
Side Effects & What to DoSedation and sexual dysfunction. Less likely than typicals to cause anticholinergic effects, orthostatic hypotension, seizures and EPS, and side effects overall are fewer, milder and better tolerated. They are more costly, which can itself become a barrier to adherence.
Adverse EventsMetabolic syndrome, the signature risk of the class: significant weight gain, especially abdominal, dyslipidemia, increased blood glucose and insulin resistance, raising the risk of diabetes, hypertension, cardiovascular disease and certain cancers. Also hyperprolactinemia, cardiac dysrhythmias and sudden cardiac death. Less likely to cause tardive dyskinesia or significant EPS, but not exempt.
Monitoring & TeachingFor metabolic syndrome: monitor weight and girth, order an initial glucose tolerance test, monitor blood glucose regularly, and provide nutrition and activity support while considering the patient's lifestyle. Assess with the AIMS at least every 3 months. Long-acting injectables are given every 2 to 4 weeks or up to months and require transportation to the clinic.
All atypical antipsychotics carry a risk of metabolic syndrome. It is the reason weight, girth and blood glucose are monitored from the start of therapy rather than after a problem appears.

Clozapine

Drugs

  • clozapine
CategoryDetail
IndicationTreatment-resistant or refractory schizophrenia, and schizoaffective disorder, where the patient has had an inadequate response to two other antipsychotics or could not tolerate them. A clozapine trial is justified after the failure of two monotherapy trials. Refractory patients are about 30% of the thought disorder population and are prone to violence and suicide. Use has produced decreased negative symptoms, increased impulse control, reduced violence toward self and others, and improved quality of life.
Side Effects & What to DoThe atypical class effects above, including metabolic syndrome and sedation.
Adverse EventsAgranulocytosis, a potentially fatal reduction in circulating granulocytes that limits the ability to fight off infection. Greatest risk during the first months of treatment. Reversible if treated early.
Monitoring & TeachingMonitor WBC weekly for the first 18 weeks of treatment, then as directed by the provider. Discontinue if the patient develops leucopenia or neutropenia. Teach the patient to report any sign of infection, since a falling white count presents as infection rather than as a symptom of its own.
Agranulocytosis is the reason clozapine is not first-line. It is the one fact to carry about this drug: a life-threatening drop in white blood cells, monitored weekly for the first 18 weeks.

Anticholinergics for Extrapyramidal Side Effects

Drugs

  • benztropine
  • trihexyphenidyl
CategoryDetail
IndicationTreating the extrapyramidal side effects of antipsychotic medication. Benztropine is given for akathisia and pseudoparkinsonism. Trihexyphenidyl is given for pseudoparkinsonism. They treat the side effect, not the schizophrenia.
Side Effects & What to DoAnticholinergic effects, the thing the drug itself produces: dry mouth, blurred vision, dry eyes, constipation, urinary retention or hesitancy, drowsiness, dizziness, confusion, tachycardia and skin flushing from decreased sweating. Manage with fluids, fiber and eye drops, and watch for urinary retention.
Adverse EventsAnticholinergic toxicity, which the side effects can progress to.
Monitoring & TeachingTeach the mnemonic pattern so the patient can recognise the effects: mad as a hatter, blind as a bat, red as a beet, hot as a hare, dry as a bone. Adding an anticholinergic to an antipsychotic compounds the anticholinergic burden, so the combined effect is assessed, not just the new drug's.

Treating Akathisia, Tardive Dyskinesia & NMS

CategoryDetail
IndicationAkathisia: propranolol, or lorazepam or diazepam, added by the provider alongside a dose reduction and benztropine. Tardive dyskinesia: valbenazine and deutetrabenazine, the two FDA-approved agents for managing symptoms. Neuroleptic malignant syndrome: dantrolene sodium and bromocriptine mesylate to relieve muscle rigidity and reduce fever, with lorazepam for agitation.
Side Effects & What to DoBenzodiazepines are for short-term use only in akathisia. Longer use risks dependence and adds sedation to a patient already at risk of it.
Monitoring & TeachingAkathisia is easily mistaken for anxiety or agitation, and treating it as such means giving more of the antipsychotic that caused it, making it worse. Assess before medicating. In tardive dyskinesia, symptoms usually persist even after the antipsychotic dose is lowered or stopped, so these drugs manage rather than reverse it. In NMS, the first action is always to stop all antipsychotics, with ICU care, hydration, cooling and vital sign monitoring.
Neuroleptic malignant syndrome is a medical emergency with a 10% fatality rate. Stop all antipsychotics immediately. Severe muscle rigidity, altered mental status, temperature over 103 degrees Fahrenheit, hypertension, tachycardia, tachypnea, diaphoresis and incontinence. ICU care is required, and an accurate medication history is critical because it is difficult to diagnose emergently.
