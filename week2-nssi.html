Nonsuicidal Self-Injury

What NSSI Is

Deliberate and direct attempts to inflict shallow yet painful injuries to the surface of the body without intending to die. It is most often done to alleviate psychic pain or numbness — not as a means to end life, and not to seek attention.

The Behavior

  • Most commonly cutting, burning, scraping or scratching the skin, biting, hitting, skin picking, and interfering with wound healing. A person often uses multiple methods.
  • Usually inflicted with a knife, needle, razor, or other sharp object — sometimes a fingernail.
  • Minor behaviors such as picking off a scab or biting nails are not considered self-injury.
  • Common sites are the frontal thighs and the dorsal forearm — easily covered and easily reached. As it intensifies the person worries less about concealment, so injuries appear in less concealable places.

Diagnostic Features

  • A desire for relief from negative thoughts or feelings — anxiety, anger, distress.
  • Self-loathing and a belief that they are bad, defective, or deserving of punishment.
  • May injure to achieve a short but intense state of euphoria during or after the act.
  • Impaired interpersonal relationships may be the impetus.
  • To be considered significant, it typically lasts at least a year and happens repeatedly.
  • The majority do not seek help — they are usually discovered when they come to hospital for other reasons, or through school counselors, teachers, a primary care provider, family, or friends.

DSM-5 Criteria

Recognized in 2013 as a distinct clinical phenomenon and included as a condition requiring further research. These are good guidelines, but the APA has not determined that NSSI is a psychiatric disorder.

  • NSSI must occur for at least 5 days in the past year. The days need not be consecutive — any 5 days within the 12-month period.
  • Engaged in for one or more of these reasons: to relieve negative thoughts or feelings, to resolve an interpersonal difficulty, or to induce a positive feeling state.
  • Preceded by negative thoughts or feelings, conflicts with others, preoccupation with the behavior that is difficult to resist, or recurrent thoughts about the behavior.
  • Not socially sanctioned behavior — body piercing, tattooing, or part of a religious or cultural ritual do not count.
  • The behavior or its consequences cause clinically significant distress or interference in important areas of functioning.
  • Does not occur solely in the context of another mental disorder.

Epidemiology, Comorbidity & Risk Factors

AreaWhat Is Known
PrevalenceLifetime prevalence is difficult to determine accurately because the behavior is concealed. About 6% of the US population has engaged in NSSI at some point, and at least 1 million people in the US each year engage in intentionally inflicted self-harm.
Sex and ageFemale to male ratio 3 or 4:1. An estimated 13%–23% of adolescents engage in NSSI. Globally the problem peaks between ages 20–29, with 17%–35% reporting it, and declines beyond age 29 — fewer people, rather than the behavior disappearing.
ComorbidityDepression · anxiety · eating disorders · substance use disorders · borderline personality disorder. 70% of females with borderline personality and 30% with eating disorders are affected by NSSI, and eating disorders co-occur in 55% of individuals with NSSI.
Biological riskInconclusive, but serotonin, dopamine, and norepinephrine may play a role in mediating NSSI.
Cognitive riskNSSI may be a form of self-punishment — painful actions used to make up for acts that caused harm or distress to others. Positive reinforcement, in the form of a reward, may support self-injurious acts.
Environmental and societal riskCultural differences are under investigation. NSSI may be a social phenomenon — individuals often learn about it from peers who are doing it.

Each year the CDC gathers hospital data on non-fatal self-harm injuries. Of the 500,000 people who visited a hospital for self-harm injuries, the figures suggest about 12 people harm themselves for every reported death by suicide — but the way the data is collected cannot distinguish intentional suicide attempts from non-intentional ones.

Recognizing & Responding

Signs & Symptoms

  • Scars from burns or cuts; fresh cuts, scratches, bruises, or other wounds; broken bones.
  • Keeping sharp objects on hand.
  • Wearing long sleeves or long pants even in hot weather; claiming frequent accidents or mishaps.
  • Spending a great deal of time alone; pervasive difficulties in interpersonal relationships.
  • Persistent questions about personal identity — "Who am I?" "What am I doing here?"
  • Behavioral and emotional instability, impulsivity, and unpredictability; statements of helplessness, hopelessness, or worthlessness.

Take Action

  • Establish a therapeutic relationship — which may fall to a family member, friend, counselor, teacher, or primary care provider, since these patients are rarely in the ED or on a psychiatric unit for the self-injury.
  • Care for the wounds or injuries.
  • Assess along four lines: what kind of self-injury, how often it happens, what triggers the urge, and what has helped in the past. Do not ask how the behavior makes other people feel — it shifts focus off the patient and reads as judgment.
  • Psychotherapeutic interventions: assess previous coping mechanisms and build healthy coping mechanisms to use instead of self-harm.
  • CBT and DBT — both successful for this condition, inpatient or outpatient.
  • Group therapy and psychopharmacology.
NSSI and suicidal ideation are not the same thing, and the nurse has to tell them apart. When a patient shows these signs, is impulsive and unpredictable, and voices hopelessness, ask the questions: Are you having thoughts of suicide? Do you have a plan? Do you have the means to carry it out? Use the assessment data to decide which this is.

The Self-Harm Inventory is the scale used when self-harm is suspected — in a school, a pediatrician's or primary care office, the ED, or a medical unit. Mental health relies heavily on scales because there are no lab values for conditions like NSSI.

Misconceptions

All of These Are False

  • Only females self-injure · only teenagers self-injure · anyone who self-injures is part of the "Goth" or "Emo" subgroup.
  • Self-injury is a suicide attempt or a failed suicide attempt · people who self-injure only cut themselves.
  • Self-injury is just attention-seeking · people who self-injure are manipulative.
  • Anyone who self-injures is crazy and should be locked up · someone who self-injures is a danger to others.
  • All people who self-injure have borderline personality disorder · all people who self-injure have been abused.
  • People who self-injure enjoy the pain, or cannot feel it.
  • Self-injury is untreatable · someone who self-injures can stop if they really want to · there is nothing I can do to help.

Stigma is the biggest roadblock with mental illness, and every statement above is false.
