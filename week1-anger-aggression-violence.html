Anger, Aggression & Violence

Definitions

TermDefinition
AngerAn emotional response to frustration of desires, a threat to one's needs, or a challenge, from mild irritation to intense fury and rage. A normal human emotion, like anxiety, and capable of being under personal control: expressed assertively, it solves problems and drives decisions. A problem only when expressed aggressively.
AggressionVerbal or physical attack intended to threaten or injure the victim's security or self-esteem: words, fists, kicks, weapons. Virtually always designed to punish, but not always inappropriate: sometimes necessary for self-protection.
ViolenceIntentional use of force that results in, or could result in, injury to another. Always an objectionable act; that is what separates it from aggression.

Workplace Violence

Why It Matters to Nurses

  • Health care and social services workers are at higher risk of workplace violence injury than workers overall.
  • Nurses are frequent targets because they have the most direct patient care contact.
  • Workplace violence is underreported, and it impairs your ability to provide effective patient care.
  • Most frequent settings: emergency departments · psychiatric units · geriatric units · intensive care units, but it can happen anywhere.

Anxiety is the one to catch early. Anxious patients, visitors, and families grasp at anything in the environment they can control. Intervene early, and communicate; communication is how we give them back a little control.

Milieu Characteristics Conducive to Violence

  • Too hot, too cold, too loud, too quiet
  • Overcrowding
  • Staff perceived as inattentive or inexperienced
  • Staff perceived as too controlling
  • Poor limit setting: the sense that other patients are allowed to do things they are not
  • Revocation of privileges

The therapeutic milieu and its five components are taught in Introduction to Psychiatric Mental Health Nursing

How to score a Brøset or DASA is not exam material. What matters is that structured tools exist and are used to identify patients at risk. The COVR is the one used to assess risk at discharge.

Brøset Violence Checklist (BVC)

  • Developed in Norway; the most common tool in the literature.
  • A shift assessment, scored on behaviors present versus the individual's own baseline.

Dynamic Appraisal of Situational Aggression (DASA)

  • Based on the Brøset. Used at Eastern State Hospital.
  • Completed every 24 hours rather than every 8-hour shift, covering seven areas.
  • More reliable than the other tools at predicting verbal and physical aggression.

Interventions & Personal Safety

Self-Awareness

  • Nurses bring their own personal and professional history, and it affects the ability to intervene effectively and appropriately.
  • Be self-aware of your strengths, needs, concerns, and vulnerability.
  • Without self-awareness, nursing interventions become impulsive or emotion-based.
  • Watch your choice of words, tone of voice, nonverbal communication, and facial expressions.

Intervention ideally begins before any sign of escalation. Determining what the patient is feeling is already intervening, and often it is just saying you are concerned and want to listen.

Interventions

  • Acknowledge that the patient has needs, rational or not, possible to meet or not.
  • State your expectations clearly, usually through limit setting.
  • Have numerous brief, non-threatening, non-directive interactions to build the therapeutic relationship.
  • Speak slowly, in shortened sentences, with a low, calm voice: as anger escalates, the ability to process decreases drastically.
  • Never yell. Model controlled behavior: you are the reference point for the behavior you want.
  • Use open-ended statements: "You feel people are treating you unfairly?" not the challenging, "What is going on right now?"
  • Avoid asking "Okay?": it implies choices exist where they don't.
  • Offer two options: "Your room, or the quiet room?" This decreases the sense of powerlessness that often precipitates violence and moves the choice to the patient.
  • Give feedback on what you see: "You seem to be very upset" opens the feelings up and may de-escalate.
Personal space and escape route. Patients at risk for violence need more personal space: stay about one foot further than they can reach with arms or legs, so you read as less of a threat. Always leave yourself an escape route, and never let the patient be between you and the door.

Staff Safety Considerations

  • Maintain eye contact but don't glare.
  • No dangling earrings, necklaces, or scarves; a patient may grab them and cause serious injury.
  • Enough staff for backup, but only one person talks; the rest stay unobtrusive.
  • Know the layout: furniture, obstacles, hazards that could injure during a physical intervention.
  • Don't stand directly in front of the patient or the doorway; stand to the side or at an angle, and encourage the patient to sit.
  • Talk somewhere quiet but visible to other staff, and tell staff you are with the patient.

Individualized Interventions: Coping Skills

Patients arrive with their own coping skills, and the hospital takes many away: no smoking, no alcohol, a restricted diet, no caffeine.

StepWhat It Looks Like
Assess current coping strategiesWhat do they use when upset, anxious, stressed, or angry? Which have not worked?
Substitute what is unavailableA smoker gets a nicotine patch; a gamer gets a TV show; a favorite food gets an alternative.
Help them adopt new strategiesSkills they can use here and afterward.

Pharmacological Interventions

It is the nurse's role to assess the appropriateness of PRN medication. A PRN is sometimes helpful, even essential, as a patient escalates. Used in conjunction with psychosocial interventions and de-escalation, never instead of them.

Long-term treatment of anger, aggression, and violence is treatment of the underlying psychiatric disorder. The PRN handles the moment; it is not the plan.

Matching the Response to the Behavior

Intervene early, and make the staff response fit the level the patient is at. Laying hands on a patient who is merely anxious will escalate the situation.

Level 1: Anxiety

Patient Behavior

  • Anxiety, frustration, impatience, irritability; highly animated, repetitive movements.
  • A change in behavior in either direction: calm to loud, or loud to calm.
  • The patient still has some level of rationality.

Staff Response: Offer Support

  • Stay alert to changes in anxiety, anger, frustration, and nonverbal cues.
  • Approach as soon as you suspect a problem; open a dialogue and get them talking.
  • Acknowledge their feelings and needs. It is OK to guess wrong; trying to understand is the point.
  • Stay calm and in control; calm, even tone.
  • Reflect their emotion; be personable, a real human being, not cold or uncaring.
  • Avoid institutional or systems jargon.
  • Don't take the bait: don't get defensive, counterattack, or be easily offended.

Level 2: Loss of Rationality (Defensive)

Patient Behavior

  • Challenging questions: "What are you going to do if I don't go to my room?"
  • Standing in your personal space; intimidation tactics (staring, glaring).
  • Refusing: loudly and adamantly refusing requests, care, or policy.
  • Releasing: table pounding, loud sighing, throwing things but not at you, not meant to cause harm.

Staff Response

  • Isolate the interaction: move the patient, or move others away.
  • Reduce stimulation; remove people staring, laughing, or talking loudly.
  • Speak clearly and slowly, use few words, and show non-verbally what you want them to do.
  • Be firm but empathetic.
  • Allow loud verbal expressions of anger as long as they are not threatening.
  • Watch your paraverbals (tone, volume, and rhythm of speech): how you say what you say.
  • It is not a win-lose situation.
  • Again: keep an exit, and never place the patient between you and it.

Level 3: Hostility, Abuse, or Aggression

Patient Behavior

  • May begin as accusations, comments about your competence, or irrelevant personal remarks.
  • Danger signs: persistent swearing; sexist or racist comments; personal or specific threats of harm; intimidating comments; terroristic-type threats; any physical behavior directed at a person.

Staff Response

  • Call for help: coworkers, security, or law enforcement.
  • Protect yourself. Remove yourself from the situation if you can.
  • Remove objects within reach that could be used as weapons.
  • Never let the patient be between you and the exit.
  • Watch your nonverbals: don't sigh, roll your eyes, or show frustration; these escalate.
  • Keep directions to the patient short ("please stay seated," "stop, please").
  • Don't agree just to agree.
  • If they can hear you, explain that aggression will not achieve the desired goal.

Level 4: Tension Reduction

Every Situation Eventually Reaches Tension Reduction

  • Energy decreases and rationality returns: the reachable, teachable moment.
  • Goal: re-establish therapeutic rapport.
  • Assess what the triggers were, whether coping strategies were used, and why they didn't work.
  • Help them identify coping strategies for the future.
  • Revise the plan of care to include the behaviors that may result in repeat seclusion or restraint.

Seclusion & Restraint

Seclusion and restraint are always a last resort. Try everything else first: verbal de-escalation, then pharmacologic interventions. Seclusion is typically used before restraints.
TermDefinition
SeclusionInvoluntary confinement of a patient alone in a room or area they are physically prevented from leaving; may be a quiet area on the unit. Less restrictive than restraints and never punitive: the goal is safety. Only for violent or self-destructive behavior that jeopardizes immediate physical safety.
RestraintAny manual method, physical or mechanical device, material, or equipment that immobilizes or reduces a patient's ability to move arms, legs, body, or head freely. Risks staff injury and physical or emotional harm to the patient.
90–95% of patients in the psychiatric setting have a history of trauma or abuse, so holding someone down or mechanically restraining them can be traumatizing or re-traumatizing.

When It May Be Used: All Must Be True

  • As a last resort
  • Patient is a danger to self or others
  • Less restrictive interventions determined ineffective to protect the patient or others from harm
  • With a written modification to the plan of care
  • Only by safe, appropriate techniques per hospital policy and state law

What Counts

  • Physically holding a patient against their will is a restraint.
  • Four side rails up is a restraint, except for seizure precautions.
  • Tucking sheets so tightly the patient cannot move is a restraint.
  • Keeping a patient in their room by physical intervention is seclusion.

Guidelines for the Use of Mechanical Restraints

Mandated by the Joint Commission.

Monitoring a Patient in Restraints

The specific monitoring intervals are not exam material.

A patient in restraints is never left alone. Someone sits outside or inside the room with the door open, and the patient is directly observed and formally assessed at frequent intervals.

A chaotic restraint event is extremely dangerous. Act as a team with a designated team leader who tells people what to do.

Organizing a Restraint Event

  • Assign every role first: who leads · who talks to the patient · who checks the seclusion area · who clears the path and moves other patients out · who prepares and gives the medication · who does the 1:1 in seclusion · who documents · who leads the debriefing.
  • Always place a wedge pillow under the head of anyone restrained in a bed; lying flat makes breathing harder, especially with a history of breathing difficulty. An extra-elevated wedge pillow exists for patients with COPD.
  • Restraining a child is more dangerous: the trachea, intercostal muscles, and diaphragm are underdeveloped and more pliable, so a restraint device restricts them more easily. Nonviolent Crisis Intervention (CPI) teaches holding techniques for children.

Contraindications for Seclusion & Restraint

The Six Contraindications

  1. Extremely unstable medical and psychiatric conditions
  2. Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease
  3. Spinal injury
  4. Seizure disorders
  5. Pregnancy
  6. Delirium or dementia: seclusion may be intolerable because of the absence of stimulation

Critical Incident Debriefing

When It Happens

  • Immediate and mandatory after every restraint episode, with the patient and with staff who took part in or witnessed it.
  • If it cannot happen immediately, it must occur within the first couple of hours, or at least within the shift.

What It Addresses

  • Could anything have been done that would have prevented the episode? If yes, what, and why was it not done in this situation?
  • Did the team respond as a team?
  • Was safety maintained?
  • Did we follow hospital policies?
  • What could we have done differently, and what lessons were learned?
  • Areas of staff education that would help in future restraint episodes.

Teaching & Health Promotion

Role Model and Educator

  • Model appropriate responses and ways to cope with anger
  • Teach a variety of methods to appropriately express anger
  • Educate about coping skills, de-escalation techniques, and self-soothing skills to manage behavior
  • Assist in identifying triggers for anger and aggression
  • Above all, be proactive and respond early
