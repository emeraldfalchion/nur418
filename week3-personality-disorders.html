Personality Disorders

Personality & Personality Disorder

TermDefinition
PersonalityThe characteristics and behavior that comprise a person's unique adjustment to life — major traits, interests, drives, values, self-concept, abilities and emotional patterns. Personality helps determine behavior.
Personality traitsCharacteristics a person is born with or develops early in life that influence how they perceive and relate to their environment. A complex pattern largely outside the person's awareness — distinctive patterns of perceiving, feeling, thinking, coping and behaving.
Personality disorderAn enduring pattern of deviant inner experience and behavior that differs from cultural expectations, is pervasive, inflexible and stable, and leads to distress or impairment. Involves dysfunctions in cognition, impulse control, relationships and affect.

Among the most challenging disorders to treat — often because the individual does not recognize that they have a problem, or that their life difficulties are of their own making. Traits become a disorder when they deviate markedly from cultural expectation, turn rigid and inflexible, and produce maladaptive behavior, impaired functioning and distress.

Epidemiology, Etiology & Common Symptoms

FactorDetail
PrevalenceAbout 8% of the global population; about 1 in 10 American adults. Emotional dysregulation is amplified.
GeneticPersonality traits are inherited — the disorders themselves are not.
NeurobiologicalNeurotransmitters regulate and influence temperament.
Psychosocial & environmentalChildren learn maladaptive responses and copy behaviors from others. Parenting matters — childhood neglect or rejection, and divorce.
TraumaA major risk factor for personality disorders.
Diathesis-stress modelA genetic or biological predisposition plus a stressor that brings out the actual disorder.

The Four Common Symptoms

Present in almost all personality disorders:

  • Impairment in interpersonal relationships
  • Dysfunction in cognition
  • Dysfunction in affect
  • Dysfunction in impulse control

Diagnostic Criteria

A specific and notable pattern of thinking, feeling and behaving with all of the following characteristics.

  1. Persistent — enduring over time.
  2. Pervasive — across a broad range of personal and social situations.
  3. Involves both inner experience and external behavior — how the person perceives, experiences and interprets the world, and the methods they use to achieve outcomes.
  4. Differs significantly from the expectations of the individual's culture.
  5. Involves two or more areas of functioning: thinking — ways of perceiving or interpreting self, others and events · feeling — emotional range, intensity, stability, appropriateness · interpersonal functioning — the style and nature of relationships · impulse control — the ability to restrain, delay or manage impulses.
  6. Leads to clinically significant distress or impairment in social, legal, occupational, financial, safety and other important areas.
  7. Not better accounted for by another mental health condition, by the direct physiological effects of a substance, or by a medical condition.

Treatment & Prognosis

Treatment

  • Restructuring personality takes years of psychotherapy — a great deal of time and money — so short-term treatment focused on immediate problem-solving, social skills and coping skills is increasingly used.
  • Psychopharmacology · individual therapy · dialectical behavior therapy (DBT) · family education and therapy · social skills training groups · group therapy.
  • Medications are rarely indicated in long-term treatment, but may be used for a short period to keep patients safe and manage anxiety or psychotic symptoms.

Prognosis

  • Once considered grim; the current view is that personality disorders are treatable — people continue to grow and change throughout life.
  • Prognosis depends on the degree of impairment and the person's motivation for help. The patient must be motivated for change.
  • The obstacle: many do not believe a problem exists, do not see themselves as its cause, or are unaware their behavior is unusual.

The Three Clusters

The DSM-5 groups 10 personality disorders into three clusters, each sharing common traits. Symptoms overlap across them.

Cluster CAnxious or fearful — worried, anxious and fearful are the key componentsDependent · Obsessive-compulsive · Avoidant
The three clusters, as a night out. Cluster A — "PaSS on the party": Paranoid, Schizoid, Schizotypal. Cluster B — "BAHNed from the party": Borderline, Antisocial, Histrionic, Narcissistic. Cluster C — "the party is DOA" (dead on arrival): Dependent, Obsessive-compulsive, Avoidant.

Cluster A — Odd or Eccentric

These disorders sit on the adaptive end of the schizophrenia spectrum. Negative symptoms appear, as in schizophrenia, but these individuals do not lose contact with reality, their symptoms are less severe, and they can be made aware of their thinking. They segregate and remove themselves from other people.

Paranoid Personality Disorder

FeatureDetail
CoreLongstanding, intense distrust and suspicion. Suspects others are exploiting, harming or deceiving them.
RelationshipsPreoccupied with doubts about loyalty and trustworthiness; suspicious about the fidelity of spouses or partners. Jealous and controlling as adults; reluctant to confide in others.
InterpretationReads hidden or threatening meaning into harmless, benign remarks. Perceives attacks on their character or reputation that are not there.
GrudgesSevere unwillingness to forgive — bears grudges, unforgiving of any slight, insult or injury, and vindictive.
BehaviorHypervigilant, quick to anger, provokes others or counterattacks. Beneath the guarded surface they are anxious and worried about being harmed.
Distinguishing itLook at the history: paranoid beliefs must have been present by early adulthood and persist in the absence of substance use or psychotic episodes.

Nursing Care

  • Counteract mistrust — they reject treatment out of suspicion, and can be very difficult to interview.
  • Adhere strictly to promises, appointments and schedules. If you say two minutes, come back in two minutes.
  • Avoid being overly friendly — it deepens suspicion — but acknowledge feelings.
  • Set firm boundaries, especially with threatening behavior.
  • Use simple, direct language and project a neutral but kind affect.
  • Group therapy can feel threatening, but role-play and group feedback on social skills can reduce suspiciousness.

Schizoid Personality Disorder

Characteristics

  • Loners who do not desire the presence of others and are detached from social relationships. Schizoid — avoid.
  • Cold, detached, distant; avoid close relationships.
  • Poor academic performance; increased prevalence of disordered family life; rarely marry.
  • Solitary jobs or hobbies with no interaction.
  • Depersonalization and detachment — flat and removed from everything around them.

Nursing Care

  • Avoid being too "nice" or "friendly."
  • Do not try to increase socialization.
  • Assess for symptoms the patient is reluctant to discuss.
  • Protect against the group's ridicule — others on the unit may make fun of them.
  • Respect personal space while gently encouraging participation.
  • Treatment: psychotherapy or group therapy; sometimes antidepressants or second-generation antipsychotics.

Schizotypal Personality Disorder

FeatureDetail
CoreUnusual thoughts, behaviors and perceptions — less severe than schizophrenia. The difference from schizophrenia: they can be made aware that their magical thinking is not real.
Social deficitsSevere social and interpersonal deficits — acute discomfort and reduced capacity for close relationships. May have a few close friends. Anxiety in social situations, intensified because they do not blend in.
ThinkingOdd beliefs and magical thinking that influence behavior — superstitions, believing they are clairvoyant or telepathic, preoccupation with paranormal phenomena. May be vulnerable to cults and religious groups for this reason.
Ideas of referenceIncorrect interpretations of events as having special personal meaning for them.
PresentationCognitive and perceptual distortions · eccentric behavior and dress · constricted or inappropriate affect · rambling conversation · paranoia, suspiciousness, distrust · brief episodes of hallucination or delusion.

Nursing Care

  • Respect the need for social isolation.
  • Be aware of and intervene appropriately with suspiciousness.
  • Reinforce socially appropriate behavior and dress.
  • Perform a careful, in-depth diagnostic assessment for symptoms needing intervention — especially suicidal thoughts.
  • Be aware of strange religious practices or peculiar thoughts as part of their life, and withhold judgment or ridicule.
  • Treatment: low-dose antipsychotics and supportive psychological care.

Borderline Personality Disorder

The most well-known and most dramatic personality disorder. A pervasive pattern of instability in interpersonal relationships, self-image and affect. These individuals are always in a state of crisis, with mood swings that can change hourly or by the minute.

FeatureDetail
Self-harmRecurrent suicidal behavior, gestures or threats, and self-mutilating behavior. About 75% have a history of at least one deliberate act of self-harm.
Affective instabilityRapid, extreme shifts in mood — a core characteristic. Erratic emotional responses to situations, and intense sensitivity to criticism or perceived slights. They come across as moody, irresponsible and intense.
Identity disturbanceNo sense of their own identity or direction, shown through feelings of emptiness, dysphoria or boredom. Often direct their actions to fit what others wish.
AngerInappropriate, intense anger or difficulty controlling anger.
Under stressTransient stress-related paranoid ideation or severe dissociation.
RelationshipsFrantic efforts to avoid real or imagined abandonment; a pattern of unstable, intense relationships and a history of insecure attachment. They idealize others and form intense relationships that violate boundaries, then sabotage them and are rejected — producing intense shame and self-hatred that leads to self-injury.
ImpulsivityIn at least two areas that are self-damaging — spending, sex, substance abuse.
ResponsibilityView themselves as victims and do not accept responsibility for their problems.

Splitting

A defense mechanism in which the person is unable to integrate and accept both positive and negative feelings. People — including themselves — and life situations are either all good or all bad, with no grey area.

Challenging for nurses: the same nurse may be called the best they have ever had, and then, after one perceived slight, the worst.

Patients with borderline personality disorder tend to get worse when admitted. Attention-seeking behavior, boundary-pushing and relationship dynamics intensify inpatient, producing more self-harm behavior and decompensation — even though most are admitted for suicidal ideation or self-harm in the first place.

Nursing Care

  • Comprehensive assessment including risk assessment and the emotional triggers that exacerbate symptoms.
  • Build trust through consistency and reliability — be predictable and available, to counter the fear of abandonment and anxiety.
  • Empathy and validation through active listening.
  • Teach emotional regulation — DBT techniques such as mindfulness, grounding and deep breathing.
  • Encourage coping skills — journaling, distraction, creative outlets.
  • Set boundaries and help them recognize when they violate others'; teach conflict resolution without self-destructive behavior, outbursts or manipulation.
  • Safety.

Safety Plans

  • Build the plan with the patient: the steps to take during an emotional crisis or when feeling overwhelmed.
  • Names and phone numbers of a few people to call right away when self-harm urges or overwhelm hit.
  • Plus the things that help — journaling, distraction, deep breathing.
  • Keep it somewhere visible, such as the refrigerator. In crisis mode the brain cannot recall who to reach out to, which is exactly why the plan is written down.

Nonsuicidal self-injury, which co-occurs heavily with this disorder, is taught in Nonsuicidal Self-Injury

Cluster B — Antisocial, Histrionic & Narcissistic

Antisocial Personality Disorder

FeatureDetail
CoreA pervasive pattern of disregard for and violation of the rights of others. Also referred to as psychopath or sociopath. Heavily researched because of its impact on society through criminal activity.
Antagonistic behaviorsDeceitful and manipulative for personal gain, or hostile when their needs are blocked. Lying, false names, conning others — for pleasure or for profit.
Disinhibited behaviorsHigh risk-taking · disregard for responsibilities · impulsivity. Criminal misconduct and substance abuse are common.
EmpathyProfound lack of empathy, remorse or guilt. Indifferent to, or rationalizing, having hurt, mistreated or stolen from others.
Developmental historyEvidence of conduct disorder with onset before age 15. Many age out of the pediatric diagnosis into this one.
Self-viewDriven by a need to prove superiority; see themselves as the center of the universe. Irritability and aggressiveness leading to physical fights or assaults. Superficially charming but deceitful, lacking genuine warmth.

Nursing Care

  • Identify blocks to the therapeutic relationship — bargaining about rules, or breaking them.
  • Identify dysfunctional thinking patterns and develop new problem-solving behaviors.
  • Self-responsibility facilitation: hold the patient responsible for their behavior, give positive feedback when they accept added responsibility, monitor how much they assume, and discuss consequences of not meeting responsibilities.
  • Anger control assistance — meditation, anger management techniques, medications.
  • Help build a new support system — theirs is typically poor.
  • Self-awareness enhancement — encourage recognition of their own thoughts and feelings.

Antisocial vs. Borderline

  • Antisocial: typically legal involvement, more outwardly destructive, a prior conduct disorder diagnosis, and more common in males.
  • Borderline: can have some legal involvement, but is more self-destructive, and more common in females.

Histrionic Personality Disorder

Characteristics

  • Excitable, dramatic, high-functioning — frequently in the spotlight, the life of the party.
  • Bold, attention-seeking, provocative; frequently sexually seductive.
  • Self-centered with a low frustration level.
  • Excessive emotions; can appear smothering.
  • No insight — limited ability to form meaningful relationships, and no recognition that the excessive emotion and attention-seeking is what interferes. Considers relationships more intimate than they are.
  • Behaviors are motivated by an intense desire to gain nurturance and support — they are intentional.
  • Distinguishing it from mania: the excessive emotion is constant — moods do not cycle up and down as in bipolar disorder.

Nursing Care

  • Know that seductive behavior is a response to inner distress.
  • Keep interactions professional and ignore flirtations; avoid overreacting to the patient's actions.
  • Help the patient clarify their real inner feelings.
  • Model concrete language; teach and role-model assertiveness.
  • Assess for suicidal ideation.
  • Set firm boundaries for interactions with staff and with peers.
  • Frequently co-occurs with anxiety, substance abuse or other personality disorders.

Narcissistic Personality Disorder

Characteristics

  • Feelings of entitlement, superiority and exaggerated self-importance; exaggerate achievements and talents; come across as arrogant.
  • Lack of empathy and a tendency to exploit others.
  • Constant need for admiration.
  • Underneath: fear of abandonment, weak self-esteem and hypersensitivity to criticism — a fragile sense of self-worth.
  • Will only interact with other "special" people, to demonstrate their superiority.
  • Less functional impairment than the other personality disorders.

Nursing Care

  • Remain neutral and calm; do not take criticism or dismissiveness to heart.
  • Promote a stronger patient self-identity.
  • Avoid power struggles or becoming defensive, and set clear boundaries.
  • Role-model empathy.
  • A complex condition needing a thoughtful, empathetic approach alongside psychiatrists, psychologists and social workers.

Cluster C — Anxious or Fearful

Dependent Personality Disorder

FeatureDetail
CoreA pattern of submissive and clinging behavior driven by an overwhelming need to be cared for.
SeparationIntense fears of separation and abandonment; intense anxiety when left alone, even briefly. Will go to great lengths to stay in a relationship even an unhealthy or abusive one, and urgently seek a new one if it ends.
Decision-makingDifficulty initiating projects, doing things alone, or making everyday decisions — they distrust their own judgment and believe others have better ideas. Complete lack of self-confidence.
ManipulationManipulate others into taking on responsibilities they do not feel able to handle.

Nursing Care

  • Psychotherapy is the treatment of choice.
  • Help address current stressors and the fear of being alone.
  • Set limits gently, so the patient does not feel punished — for example when they manipulate others into doing things for them.
  • Be aware of strong countertransference.
  • Use the therapeutic relationship for assertiveness training.
  • Encourage independence and self-efficacy.

Obsessive-Compulsive Personality Disorder

Characteristics

  • A pervasive pattern of preoccupation with orderliness, perfectionism and control at the expense of flexibility, openness and efficiency.
  • Excessively devoted to work and productivity to the exclusion of leisure and friendships — workaholics.
  • Rigidity and inflexible standards; stubborn.
  • Excessive goal-seeking that is self-defeating or relationship-defeating.
  • Strict standards interfere with project completion — nothing is ever perfect enough — and they are fearful of delegating in case the result is imperfect.
  • They seek help when overwhelmed by their inability to keep up with tasks, lists or organization.

The Key Distinction & Care

  • OCPD does not interfere greatly with daily functioning as obsessive-compulsive disorder does — they are not stuck in a compulsive loop and can go about their lives.
  • Guard against power struggles.
  • Remember the patient has difficulty with unexpected changes to routine and structure.
  • Provide structure, but allow enough time to complete habitual behaviors.
  • Promote a calm, supportive environment and encourage group participation; group and behavioral therapy are used.
  • Medications may be needed for anxiety, depression or obsessions — clomipramine or fluoxetine.

Avoidant Personality Disorder

Characteristics

  • Low self-esteem — view themselves as inept, unattractive, inadequate or inferior, with a preoccupation with their perceived shortcomings.
  • Preoccupied with rejection, humiliation and failure; reluctant to take risks or engage in new activities for fear of embarrassment.
  • Avoid activities involving interpersonal contact for fear of criticism, rejection or disapproval.
  • Unwilling to get involved with people unless certain of being liked; very inhibited in social relationships.
  • Shyness that increases with age. Timid, lonely, overly sensitive — wallflowers.
  • Subject to depression, anxiety and anger.

Nursing Care

  • Friendly, accepting, reassuring approach — but do not push too hard.
  • Show empathy and acceptance of their fears of rejection.
  • Exercises to enhance new social skills, designed so they succeed and do not feel like failures.
  • Assertiveness training works well.
  • Individual therapy for trust building, plus group therapy.
  • Antidepressants and anti-anxiety medications are used in this cluster.
