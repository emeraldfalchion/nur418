Introduction to Psychiatric Mental Health Nursing

Defining Mental Health & Mental Illness

TermDefinition
Health (WHO)Complete physical, mental, and social well-being: not merely the absence of disease or infirmity. Includes satisfaction with, and enjoyment of, the self and the environment.
Mental health (WHO)Well-being in which the individual can realize their own potential, cope with the normal stresses of life, work productively, and contribute to the community.
Mental illness (APA)Health conditions involving changes in emotion, thinking, or behavior, associated with distress and/or problems functioning in social, work, or family activities.
DSM-5Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 5th edition: the official medical guidelines of the American Psychiatric Association for diagnosing psychiatric disorders. Criteria influenced by multiprofessional clinical field trials; the basis for developing interventions.

The Mental Health–Illness Continuum

Health is fluid, whether mental or physical. Mental health and mental illness sit on a continuum, and people move along it in both directions.

Well-Being

Adequate to high functioning. Everyday stress and discomfort do not impact daily functioning.

Emotional Problems or Concerns

Mild to moderate distress, mild impairment in functioning: trouble with concentration and appetite; insomnia and other sleep issues.

Mental Illness

Marked distress, with altered thinking, mood, and behavior.

Theories of Causation

Early 19th-Century Theories

  • Inheritance theory: "insanity" transmitted from one generation to another.
  • Moral degeneracy theory: mentally ill by virtue of a bad character.
  • Germ theory: "insanity" believed contagious, so people were segregated.
  • Septic foci theory: infection causes insanity, so remove the infection by surgery = cure. The era of bleeding and leeches.

What Is Believed Now

  • No single common cause: different mental illnesses have different theories of causation.
  • Diathesis-stress model: the most accepted explanation for mental illness. Diathesis = a biological predisposition for an illness; stress = any emotional or environmental stress or trauma. Many carry the predisposition, but it does not turn into illness unless a certain stress occurs.

History: Early Treatment & the Shift to Community Care

1800s–1900s: symptoms believed to be behavioral, under the person's control. Treatment followed from that belief.

Early Treatments

  • Physical restraint: padded helmets, hand mittens, chain restraints, straitjackets.
  • Chemical restraint: heavy medication that left people sitting around.
  • Lobotomy: stopped acting-out behaviors but drastically changed personality.
  • Insulin therapy and hydrotherapy (sedative cold wet packs, warm baths, and tanks that sprayed water at the person as a calming measure).
  • ECT, introduced later.

ECT is taught as a current treatment — full detail in Group & Physiologic Therapies

WhenWhat Changed
1845Lunacy Act: created asylums and institutionalization. Families dropped people off and often never saw them again.
1930Mental Health Treatment Act: institutions became Mental Hospitals; people began to be treated more humanely.
1963President John F. Kennedy's Community Mental Health Act accelerated deinstitutionalization: the mass movement of severely mentally ill people from state hospitals to outpatient care, closing many hospitals. Expanded the nurse's role: nurses could now give the new medications and help with treatments and therapies.
1970sMental hospitals shutting down; mental health units opened inside general hospitals, making mental health care more like acute medical care.
1990sStrong push for less time in the hospital and more management in the community.
Late 2000sIntegration of primary care and mental health care in outpatient settings.

What Went Wrong After Deinstitutionalization

  • People had been institutionalized: no coping skills, no jobs, nowhere to live, no ability to cook or care for themselves.
  • Many with good family support or assisted living transitioned successfully, especially with the new medications; many others did not.
  • Community mental health centers grew slowly and were understaffed: no job training, psychiatric counseling, or life skills training.
ConsequenceDetail
IncarcerationAbout 2 million people with mental illness are booked into jails each year. Most get no treatment, so the illness worsens and victimization risk is high. Once out, at least 83% no longer have access to health care and benefits; a criminal record makes work and housing hard to find, so many wind up homeless, in the ED, or re-arrested.
Revolving-door treatmentBegan in the 1960s with the community mental health movement: psychotropic drug development plus deinstitutionalization into communities that lacked services, leaving patients without the skills to function independently, so they returned to the ED and to repeated inpatient admissions.
Managed care1990s: inpatient a few weeks to stabilize. For the past two decades managed care companies have dictated the terms of admission: length-of-stay reductions and more stringent admission criteria.
VictimizationPeople assume mental illness means dangerous, but people with mental illness are far more often the victims. Serious mental illness (SMI) carries a 12 times higher rate of victimization than the general population: verbal abuse, bullying, threats, theft, physical assault, and rape, including exploitation and abuse by partners and family members, and abuse by health care providers. Some institutions are no safer than communities, with threats from staff and other patients, so many prefer danger in the community to being inpatient.

Advocacy, Legislation & the Courts

TermDefinition
ConsumersThe term for individuals who have mental illness. The consumer (or consumer recovery) movement advocated for their rights and fought discrimination and forced treatment; consumers resisted treatment arranged for them by providers and demanded involvement in their own treatment decisions.
NAMI (1979)National Alliance on Mental Illness: the nation's largest grassroots mental health organization.

What NAMI Does

  • Builds better services and treatment; supports research.
  • Communicates that mental illnesses are brain disorders: raising awareness and eliminating stigma and discrimination.
  • Advocates for people with mental illness and improves access to treatment services.
  • Integrates mental illness into community life.
  • Advocates for the language we use: the person is not the disease. Not "a schizophrenic"; a person who has schizophrenia.

Landmark Reports and Legislation

ItemYearWhat Came Out of It
Mental Health Parity Act1996Insurers must give equal coverage for psychiatric illness as for physical health, including deductibles, number of covered visits, and out-of-pocket expense. Also covers substance-related disorders.
Report of the Surgeon General1999First national report on mental health in the US. Finding: mental health is fundamental to a healthy life · mental health disorders are real health conditions with enormous consequences · effective treatments are available for most.
New Freedom Commission on Mental Health2003Finding: mental health care in the US was "in shambles." Called for a streamlined system with less fragmented care, early diagnosis and treatment, adoption of the principles of recovery, and increased assistance with housing and employment.
Patient Protection and Affordable Care Act2010Coverage for most uninsured Americans through expanded Medicaid eligibility; created health insurance exchanges for more choices; carried the insurance mandate.
Tim's Law (Kentucky)2017Kentucky's first assisted outpatient treatment (AOT) statute; other states call this assertive community treatment (ACT). Lets families or others in the community go to court to require an individual to attend outpatient treatment, avoiding inpatient.

The Six Goals of the New Freedom Commission

  1. Americans understand the link between mental health and overall health.
  2. Mental health care is family- and consumer-driven.
  3. Disparities in mental health care are eliminated.
  4. Screening, assessment, and referral become common practice, so problems are caught early.
  5. Principles of recovery guide care.
  6. Greater use of technology, and more housing for individuals with mental illness.

Fayette County Mental Health Court

  • October 2012: hosted by NAMI, attended by mental health and criminal justice stakeholders.
  • Multidisciplinary team: judges, prosecutors, providers, case workers, and peer support specialists (consumers), delivering treatment-oriented, person-centered care.
  • Built on therapeutic jurisprudence and restorative justice principles, to reduce re-offending.
  • Peer support specialists act as "systems navigators," matching participants to housing and support services; independent housing with access to community services is a primary goal.
  • Referral by order of diversion, probation, or contempt. Staff then screen candidates for eligibility: verifying a diagnosis of serious mental illness and evaluating which services the person can use. The candidate decides whether to accept the treatment requirements and regular check-ins.
  • Lets people with SMI obtain professional treatment and medications instead of a jail sentence.

StigmaSeeing someone in a negative way because of a particular characteristic, attribute, or personal trait (Mayo Clinic). Widespread fear and misunderstanding of mental illness, heightened by the focus on extraordinary symptoms in film and literature.

Why Stigma Matters Clinically

  • A major clinical and public health issue in its own right.
  • Worsens the course and outcomes of mental illness; a heavy burden on the individual.
  • Many people with mental illness will not go to an ER because of stigma from health care providers.

How to Fight Stigma

  • Talk openly about mental health: break the cultural barrier of not talking about it.
  • Stop criminalizing these individuals (Mental Health Court is one way).
  • Push back on media portrayals of mental illness.
  • Educate yourself and others: education decreases stigma.
  • Be conscious of the language you use.
  • Advocate for mental health reform as a nurse.
  • Encourage equality in physical and mental illness.
  • See the person, not the illness; show empathy and compassion.

Key Terms

TermDefinition
ResilienceThe ability and capacity to secure the resources needed to support personal well-being: optimism, competence, and a sense of mastery over obstacles. It determines the incidence, severity, and prognosis of mental illness and is essential to recovery.
RecoveryA process of change through which an individual improves their health and wellness, lives a self-directed life, and reaches their full potential. Recovery is possible even where cure is not: we cannot cure schizophrenia, and a person with schizophrenia can still recover.
Dual diagnosisA co-occurring mental illness and a substance use disorder (e.g. alcohol use disorder with bipolar disorder). One of the two must be a substance use disorder.
ComorbidityThe broader term for two co-occurring chronic illnesses: two mental health disorders (depression and anxiety) or a mental and a medical one (depression and diabetes). Dual diagnosis is the specific case that involves substance use.
Adherence (not compliance)Following the plan of care, and the term that replaced compliance; "compliance" carries a strongly negative connotation, so the language change is itself a way of breaking down stigma.
AnosognosiaAn individual with a severe mental illness does not believe they have it: a common reason a stabilized patient stops their medications at discharge.

Psychiatric Nursing Practice & the Treatment Team

Basic Level: PMH-RN

  • Psychiatric mental health registered nurse.
  • Requires 2 years of full-time work, 2,000 clinical hours, 30 hours of continuing education.
  • Then sit the certification exam and add BC to the RN title (RN-BC).

Advanced Practice: PMH-APRN

  • Master of Science or doctorate.
  • Health promotion, screening, and evaluation; health assessment and examination; formulates differential diagnoses.
  • Orders, conducts, and interprets laboratory and diagnostic studies and procedures.
  • Conducts individual, family, and group psychotherapy: outside the basic-level scope.
  • Prescribes, monitors, manages, and evaluates medications.
  • Provides or directs home health services; also works in general health settings.

Nursing Skills Rooted in Psychiatric Mental Health Care

  • Safety: awareness of the client's environment and the client's thoughts.
  • Therapeutic communication and the nurse-patient relationship.
  • Milieu management.
  • Relaxation therapy.
  • Motivational interviewing and the concept of self-efficacy.
  • Suicide assessment and prevention.
  • Management of aggressive behavior, including with medication.
  • Crisis intervention and disaster mental health.
  • Instillation of hope: without hope, clients do not do well.

Instillation of hope is also one of Yalom's therapeutic factors — see Group & Physiologic Therapies

The Interdisciplinary Treatment Team

Each discipline gathers data and participates in the plan of care. The treatment plan (some places call it a clinical pathway) guides care during the stay, detailing the steps that deliver evidence-based care. Nurses frequently lead these meetings, inpatient or outpatient, and the meetings focus on improving outcomes.

Who Is on the Team

  • Always: the patient, family, nurse, and provider.
  • May also include: CNS or APRN, psychologist, social worker (SW/LCSW), pharmacist, recreational (activity) therapist, and mental health associates or techs.
  • Certified drug and alcohol counselor when there is a substance use diagnosis; a Fayette County teacher on the child and adolescent units.
  • The patient is the most important member: they have self-efficacy and need to help design the plan. If they don't agree with it, they won't follow it.

What an Acute Unit Is Actually Like

  • Admission criteria: danger to self (suicidal ideation or behaviors) · danger to others (homicidal ideation) · unable to care for basic needs and/or gross impairment of judgment, placing the individual at imminent risk through an inability to protect themselves.
  • Some units also handle detoxification from drugs or alcohol.
Top 5 inpatient diagnoses: mood disorders (depressive and bipolar) · substance-related disorders · delirium or dementia · anxiety disorders · schizophrenia.

Admissions, Patient Rights & Legal Issues

Voluntary Admission

  • The patient or guardian seeks hospitalization.
  • Both the individual and the health care professional must agree treatment is needed.
  • The client signs themselves in and signs a consent for treatment.

Involuntary Commitment

  • An emergency, very temporary admission typically involving people who are confused or psychotic.
  • The physician, PA, or NP believes admission is needed but the client does not want to be admitted, and is admitted against their will on a 72-hour hold, not counting weekends and holidays.
  • During the 72 hours the patient may improve and sign themselves in, becoming a voluntary admission.
  • If treatment is still needed when the 72 hours expire, form 202A is completed by the APRN or MD and taken to the judge to file for longer commitment.

Patients admitted to a psychiatric hospital have the same rights as any other hospitalized patient.

Right or TermWhat It Means
Rights guaranteed under the lawTo have or refuse visitors, to receive or refuse treatment, and rights regarding psychiatric advance directives.
Informed consentPatient informed of the risks, benefits, and alternatives, then voluntarily accepts treatment.
Implied consentA patient is approached with a medication and indicates they are willing to take it.
CapacityA person's ability to make an informed decision.
CompetencyA legal term for the degree of mental soundness a person has to make decisions or to carry out specific acts.
Restraint and seclusionThere must be an order and documentation. In an emergency a nurse may place a patient in seclusion or restraint, then obtain a written or verbal order as soon as possible thereafter.
ConfidentialityOnly those who need to know may know, unless the client gives permission. Two exceptions: a specific threat to kill someone (you have a duty to warn and protect third parties), and suspected child or elder abuse, which must be reported.
There is never a PRN or standing order for restraint. A physician must come, lay eyes on the patient, and write the order every time.

The Therapeutic Milieu

The therapeutic milieu is the surroundings and environment of the inpatient unit, including peer and staff interactions.

ComponentWhat It Looks Like on the Unit
ContainmentBasic needs (food, shelter, safety, security) are provided on a locked unit.
SupportEncouragement, praise, and positive feedback help clients achieve autonomy, improve coping skills, and manage symptoms.
ValidationRespecting privacy, cultural needs, and feelings for holistic health.
StructureControl and limitation of maladaptive behaviors; setting limits.
InvolvementPromoting the self-efficacy of the client through self-care and stress management strategies.

What does diathesis mean in the diathesis-stress model?
A biological predisposition for an illness.
What single quality determines the incidence, severity, and prognosis of mental illness?
Resilience.
How long is an involuntary hold in Kentucky?
72 hours, not counting weekends and holidays.
Which form is filed to petition for commitment beyond the hold?
Form 202A, completed by the APRN or MD.
What distinguishes dual diagnosis from comorbidity?
In dual diagnosis one of the two disorders must be a substance use disorder.
What is anosognosia?
A person with a severe mental illness does not believe they have it.
Name the three admission criteria for an acute psychiatric unit.
Danger to self, danger to others, or unable to care for basic needs and/or gross impairment of judgment.
Name the difference between capacity and competency.
Capacity is the ability to make an informed decision; competency is the legal term for the mental soundness required to decide for oneself.
Is a chemical restraint more or less restrictive than four-point restraints?
Less restrictive; offer PRN medication by mouth before physical or mechanical restraint.
Can restraint ever be ordered PRN or as a standing order?
No. A physician must see the patient and write a new order every single time.
Which two circumstances allow you to break patient confidentiality?
A specific threat to kill someone (duty to warn and protect), and suspected child or elder abuse.
Name the five components of the therapeutic milieu.
Containment, support, validation, structure, and involvement.
