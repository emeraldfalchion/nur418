Schizophrenia & Schizoaffective Disorder

Schizophrenia, Psychosis & the Spectrum

TermDefinition
SchizophreniaA psychosis characterized by abnormalities in perception, in the content of thought, and in thought processes, plus extensive withdrawal of interest from other people and the outside world.
PsychosisA state in which the individual is experiencing hallucinations, delusions, or disorganized thoughts, speech or behavior.

A mental illness that interferes with the ability to think clearly, manage emotions, make decisions and relate to others.

The Schizophrenia Spectrum Disorders

  • Schizophrenia
  • Schizoaffective disorder
  • Schizophreniform disorder
  • Schizotypal personality disorder
  • Delusional disorder
  • Brief psychotic disorder
  • Substance-induced psychotic disorder
  • Psychosis associated with another medical condition
  • Psychotic disorder not otherwise specified

Schizotypal personality disorder sits on this spectrum and is taught in full in Personality Disorders

DSM-5 Diagnostic Criteria

  1. Two or more of the following present for a significant portion of a 1-month period, and at least one must be from the first three: delusions · hallucinations · disorganized speech · disorganized or catatonic behavior · negative symptoms.
  2. One or more major areas of social or occupational functioning (work, interpersonal relations, self-care) markedly below the previously achieved level.
  3. Continuous signs persisting for at least 6 months.
  4. Absence or insignificant duration of major depressive, manic or mixed episodes occurring concurrently with active symptoms.
  5. Not a direct physiologic effect of a substance or medical condition.
  6. Where a prior history of autistic disorder or another pervasive developmental disorder exists, prominent delusions or hallucinations must be present.

Epidemiology, Etiology & Comorbidity

FactorDetail
Prevalence1% to 1.1% of the world's population, regardless of racial, ethnic or economic background. Approximately 3.5 million individuals in the United States, and one of the leading causes of disability.
More frequently diagnosedAmong males, and in urban areas.
Peak onsetBetween 15 and 35, with 50% occurring before age 25. Men between 15 and 25, women between 25 and 35.
Age limitsNew cases are rare before age 10 and after age 40. Children can be diagnosed, though this is uncommon at 1 in 40,000.
EtiologyGenetics · environmental · brain chemistry · substance use. The vulnerability-stress model applies.

Comorbidity

Psychiatric: substance use disorders, nicotine dependence, and anxiety, depression and suicide.

Medical: diabetes, cardiovascular disease, obesity, malignant neoplasm, HIV/AIDS, osteoporosis and hepatitis C.

Children & Adolescents

Early Developmental Signs

  • Language delays
  • Late or unusual crawling, and late walking
  • Other abnormal motor behaviors such as rocking or arm flapping

Later Signs & Treatment

Hallucinations, especially auditory, delusions, lack of emotion or emotions inappropriate to the situation, social withdrawal, poor school performance, decreased self-care, strange eating rituals, incoherent speech, illogical thinking and agitation.

Treatment: risperidone and aripiprazole, individual and family psychotherapy, social and academic skills training, and hospitalization.

The Phases of Schizophrenia

PhaseWhat Happens
ProdromalMild changes in thinking, reality testing and mood, insufficient to meet DSM-5 criteria. Symptoms appear 1 to 12 months before the first full episode. Speech and thought may be odd or eccentric, with anxiety, obsessive thoughts and compulsive behaviors. Deterioration in concentration, school or work performance and social functioning, alongside distressing thoughts, suspiciousness, memory impairment and disorganization.
AcuteSymptoms range from few and mild to many and disabling: hallucinations, delusions, apathy, social withdrawal, diminished affect, anhedonia, disorganized behavior and impaired judgment and cognition, resulting in functional impairment. As symptoms worsen, coping fails and symptoms once concealed become apparent to others. Can last several months even with treatment, and may require increased support or hospitalization.
StabilizationSymptoms are stabilizing and diminishing, with movement toward a previous level of functioning. Care in an outpatient mental health center or partial hospitalization program may be needed.
Maintenance or residualThe condition has stabilized and a new baseline is established. Positive symptoms are usually absent or significantly diminished, but negative and cognitive symptoms continue to be a concern.

Positive Symptoms: Alterations in Perception

Positive symptoms are symptoms that exist but should not be there. The four main symptom categories are positive, negative, cognitive and affective. Positive symptoms cover alterations in perception, speech, thought and behavior.

Paranoia & Delusions

TypeBelief
ParanoiaAn irrational fear ranging from mild to profound, with deep mistrust or suspicion of others. May result in dangerous defensive actions, such as harming another person first. Can develop into delusional thoughts.
DelusionsFalse beliefs held despite a lack of evidence, which are not corrected by reasoning.
PersecutoryBelief that one is being watched, plotted against or ridiculed.
ReferentialBelief that events with no connection to you are somehow related to you.
GrandioseBelief that one is a powerful or important person.
ErotomanicBelief that another person desires you romantically.
NihilisticThe delusion of nonexistence: a fixed belief that the mind, body or the world at large no longer exists.
SomaticBelief that the body is changing in unusual ways.
ReligiousBelief in a special relationship with God, a mission for God, or that they are sinners.
ControlBelief that another person, group or external force controls your thoughts, feelings, impulses or behavior.

Hallucinations

Alterations in perception involving errors in how one interprets perceptions or perceives reality. A hallucination occurs when a person perceives a sensory experience for which no external source exists, and can involve all five senses: auditory (hearing voices or sounds) · visual (seeing people or things) · olfactory (smelling odors) · gustatory (experiencing tastes) · tactile (feeling body sensations).

What Hallucinations Are Like

  • Very real to the person experiencing them, and distracting during interactions.
  • Supportive or terrifying, faint or loud, episodic or constant, and sometimes attributed to a specific source such as a parent or God.
  • Accompanied by varying degrees of anxiety and distress.

Command Hallucinations

Auditory hallucinations that instruct a patient to act in a specific way. Content ranges from innocuous to dangerous, including commands to harm self or others. They must be assessed and monitored for.

Suicidal or Homicidal Themes Require Immediate Safety Measures

Ask directly: "Are you hearing a voice that is telling you to do something?" then "Do you believe what you hear is real?" If the answer is yes, the patient is at increased risk for acting on the command.

Intervening with Delusions & Hallucinations

Delusions

Do

  • Remain calm, acknowledge and accept the patient's experience and feelings.
  • Focus on helping the patient feel safe, addressing the underlying theme of fear.
  • Ask the patient to describe the delusion, and assess its intensity, frequency and duration.
  • Validate the part that is real and present reality. Identify what triggered it.
  • As reality testing improves, supportively convey doubt and clarify misinterpretations of the environment.

Do Not

  • Never debate or argue the delusional content. It interferes with the development of trust.
  • Do not focus on the delusion itself. Focusing on the fear and what helps the patient feel secure is what is therapeutic.
  • Until reality testing improves, never try to prove the delusion is incorrect. This intensifies the delusion and makes staff appear untrustworthy.

Hallucinations

Assessing

  • Observe for indicators: moving the eyes back and forth, muttering or talking to self, appearing distracted, suddenly stopping conversation as if interrupted, or intently watching a vacant area.
  • Ask direct questions about the type and content. This is considered best practice.
  • Assess when they began and how the patient experiences them: supportive or distressing, background or intrusive.

Intervening

  • Introduce yourself, approach in a non-threatening, calm manner, and reassure the patient they are safe.
  • Call the patient by name, speak clearly and keep sentences simple.
  • Do not respond to hallucinations as if they are real, and do not deny the person's experience. Offer your own perceptions and present reality.
  • Be alert for signs of anxiety, which may mean they are intensifying or are command in type.
  • Engage the patient in reality-based activities, since it is difficult to focus on both at once.

Teaching the Patient to Cope

  • Manage stress and stimulation, and use other sounds to compete with the hallucinations.
  • Check with others to find out what is and is not real.
  • Tell yourself the voices are a symptom and are not real, and tell the voices to go away.
  • Make contact with others, and develop a plan for how to cope.

Documentation

For both delusions and hallucinations, document the type, content or theme, and characteristics, in the patient's own words where possible. Record the behavior before and during the episode and any actions taken. Where the patient posed a threat to self or others, document what was done to ensure safety.

Positive Symptoms: Speech, Thought & Behavior

Alterations in Speech

TermDefinition
CircumstantialityExtremely detailed and lengthy conversation about a topic that eventually gets to the point.
TangentialityWandering off topic or going off on tangents and never reaching the point.
Looseness of associationAbsence of the normal connectedness of thoughts, ideas and topics.
Flight of ideasTopic changes repeatedly and rapidly with only superficial connections, making it difficult for others to follow.
EcholaliaPathological repetition of another's words.
Clang associationChoosing words based on their sound rather than their meaning, often a rhyme or similar beginning sound.
Symbolic speechUsing words based on what they symbolize, not what they mean. A patient reporting that demons are sticking needles in him may mean he feels a sharp pain.
Pressured speechUrgent or intense speech, with reluctance to allow comments from others.
Word saladA string of totally unconnected words, a jumble meaningless to a listener.
NeologismsMade-up words with meaning for the patient only.

Alterations in Thought

TermDefinition
Thought blockingA reduction or stoppage of thought. Mid-sentence the patient pauses and cannot remember what they were saying.
Thought insertionThe belief that someone else has inserted thoughts into the patient's brain.
Thought deletionA belief that thoughts have been taken or are missing.

Alterations in Behavior

TermDefinition

EchopraxiaInvoluntary imitation of another's movements and gestures.
Motor retardationA pronounced slowing of movement.
Motor agitationExcited behavior such as running or pacing rapidly, often in response to internal or external stimuli.

Stereotyped behaviorsRepetitive, purposeless movements peculiar to the person that serve no purpose.
Gesturing or posturingPosture held in a fixed position.
Boundary impairmentAn impaired ability to sense where one's body or influence ends and another's begins.
Impaired impulse controlA reduced ability to resist impulses.

Negative, Cognitive & Affective Symptoms

Negative symptoms are ones that should be there but are not. Positive symptoms are obvious to others and can make treatment seem more urgent, yet negative symptoms are a serious problem because they amount to the absence of what are considered essential human qualities.

Negative Symptoms

TermDefinition
Affective bluntingReduced or constricted affect.
AlogiaA reduction in speech, also called poverty of speech.
AnhedoniaA reduced ability or inability to experience pleasure or joy.
ApathyA decreased interest in activities or beliefs that would otherwise be interesting or important.
AsocialityA decreased desire for social interaction, or discomfort while doing it. Social withdrawal.
AvolitionReduced or lost motivation or goal-directed behavior: difficulty beginning or sustaining goal-directed activities.
The six negative symptoms all begin with A, except affective blunting: Alogia, Anhedonia, Apathy, Asociality, Avolition.

Cognitive Symptoms

TermDefinition
AnosognosiaInability to realize one is ill, caused by the illness itself. May result in resistance to or cessation of treatment, and is often combined with paranoia so that accepting help becomes impossible.
Concrete thinkingImpaired ability to think abstractly, resulting in interpreting things in a literal manner.
Impaired memoryImpacts short-term memory and the ability to learn.
Impaired information processingDelayed responses, misperceptions or difficulty understanding others.
Impaired executive functioningDifficulty with reasoning, setting priorities, comparing options and planning.

Affective Symptoms & Describing Affect

Affective symptoms involve an altered experience and expression of emotions. Mood may be unstable, erratic, labile (changing rapidly and easily), or incongruent (not what would be expected for the circumstances).

AffectDescription
FlatImmobile or blank facial expression.
BluntedReduced or minimal emotional response.
ConstrictedReduced in range or intensity, showing sadness or anger but no other moods.
InappropriateIncongruent with the actual emotional state or situation, such as laughing in response to a tragedy.
BizarreOdd, illogical, inappropriate or unfounded, including grimacing.

Schizoaffective Disorder

A chronic mental health condition characterized primarily by symptoms of schizophrenia, such as hallucinations or delusions, plus symptoms of a mood disorder, such as mania and depression. Lifetime prevalence is 0.3%. Men and women are affected at the same rate, but men often develop the illness at an earlier age.

Major mood episodes last days or weeks before they subside, but the schizophrenia symptoms generally persist even after they end. That persistence is what distinguishes it from a mood disorder. Many patients are initially misdiagnosed with bipolar disorder or schizophrenia, and because the condition is less well studied, many interventions are borrowed from those two.

The Two Types

Bipolar Type

Both depressive and manic episodes alongside the schizophrenia symptoms.

Increased energy to the point of feeling no need for sleep, euphoria that may not match circumstances, and risky unsafe behavior from feeling invulnerable, then tiredness that does not resolve with sleep and sadness.

Depressive Type

No episodes of mania. The psychotic symptoms plus the depressed mood of major depressive disorder, experienced as long periods of intense depression rather than highs and lows.

Lethargy, loss of pleasure in favorite activities, inability to sleep or sleeping excessively, loss of appetite, and a feeling of never being happy again.

Manic and depressive episodes are named throughout this section and are taught in full in Bipolar & Related Disorders

DSM-5 Criteria

  1. An uninterrupted duration of illness during which there is a major mood episode, manic or depressive, in addition to criterion A for schizophrenia. A major depressive episode must include depressed mood.
  2. Hallucinations and delusions for two or more weeks in the absence of a major mood episode, at some point during the lifetime duration of the illness.
  3. Symptoms meeting criteria for a major mood episode are present for most of the total duration of both the active and residual portions of the illness.
  4. Not the result of a substance or another medical condition.

Care Considerations, Outcomes & Relapse

Priority Problems

Safety Comes First

  • Risk for violence toward others
  • Risk for suicide or self-harm
  • Disturbed thought processes, and disturbed sensory perception
  • Risk for treatment non-adherence
  • Ineffective coping, risk for social isolation, risk for self-care deficits
  • Knowledge deficits, and risk for hopelessness or powerlessness

Outcomes & Interventions

Outcomes are specific and measurable with a timeframe, and are developed alongside the patient. Avoid open words like increase or decrease, and attach a number and a deadline: the patient will state 2 to 3 coping strategies prior to discharge, will state 1 benefit of taking prescribed medication this shift, will remain free from self-harm this shift.

FocusIntervention
SafetyAssess using the C-SSRS each shift, assess for thoughts of harm to self or others, and assess the environment and implement safety measures such as removing harmful objects.
OrientationAssess orientation to person, place, time and situation each shift.
CopingAssess current coping strategies, and teach one coping strategy each shift.
MedicationAdminister as prescribed, give PRN medication for acute agitation or aggression, and assess understanding of the treatment plan.
EducationEducate the patient and family about the illness, its symptoms and the importance of treatment adherence.

The C-SSRS is used here as a standing shift assessment and is taught in full in Suicide

Relapse

Non-Adherence Is the Major Reason for Relapse

  • 80% to 90% experience relapse during the course of their illness, and with each relapse there is usually a longer period to recover.
  • Stopping medication almost certainly leads to relapse, with 80% relapsing within 2 years.
  • Failure to take prescribed medication risks relapse, suicide or self-harm, violence toward others, increased mortality, hospital readmission, decline in quality of life, and social and occupational difficulty.
  • Combining medication and psychotherapy diminishes the severity and frequency of relapses.

Goals of treatment across schizophrenia and schizoaffective disorder: safety in all settings, physical care of the patient, stabilization on medications, patient and family education about the illness and its treatment, and psychosocial support of the patient and family.

Antipsychotic medications, their side effects and the treatment of those side effects are taught in full in Antipsychotic Medications

Additional Treatments & Prognosis

Additional Treatment Approaches

  • Electroconvulsive therapy, which may be helpful with catatonia
  • Cognitive behavioral therapy, important for long-term recovery
  • Group therapy and family therapy
  • Social skills training
  • Case management and support groups

Factors Predicting a Poorer Outcome

  • Slow onset of the disease, more than 2 to 3 years
  • Younger age at onset
  • Longer duration between first symptoms and first treatment
  • Longer periods of untreated illness
  • More negative symptoms

The goal is to reduce the frequency, intensity and duration of relapses, which is believed to improve long-term prognosis. For most individuals symptoms improve with medication and psychosocial interventions, and many experience a good quality of life with families and occupations. In some cases schizophrenia does not respond fully to treatment, leaving mild to severe residual symptoms and varying degrees of dysfunction or disability. A minority require repeated or lengthy inpatient care.
