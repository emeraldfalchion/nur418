Substance Use & Addictive Disorders

What a Substance Use Disorder Is

A complex brain disease marked by craving a substance, seeking it, and using it regardless of consequences — pathological use that leads to a disorder of use. The DSM-5 sets separate criteria for each substance, but all share the same shape.

The Four Symptom Groups

  • Impaired control: difficulty limiting or cutting down; larger amounts over a longer period than intended; repeated unsuccessful efforts to stop.
  • Social impairment: failure to meet major responsibilities at home, school or work; withdrawal from social, occupational and recreational activities.
  • Risky use: continued use in physically dangerous situations, or despite knowing it causes or worsens physical or psychological problems.
  • Physical effects: intoxication, tolerance and withdrawal.

What It Is — and Is Not

  • Is: chronic and treatable. Improves with medical treatment.
  • Is NOT: easy to manage · a personal weakness · a moral failing.
  • Addiction: a chronic medical condition with a strong craving, cycles of relapse and remission, and the risk of tolerance, withdrawal, disability or premature death. Many cycles may pass before abstinence holds.
  • Substances covered: alcohol, caffeine, cannabis, hallucinogens, inhalants, opioids, sedatives/hypnotics, anxiolytics, stimulants, tobacco.

Stigma, Language & Countertransference

Stigma reflects discriminatory attitudes and interferes with seeking treatment. It touches the individual, family and friends, treatment professionals, and the general public.

TermWhat It Means for Practice
Person-first, person-centered languageDescribe the illness as one part of a person's life rather than defining the person by the condition. "A person with alcohol use disorder," not "an alcoholic." "A person with a substance use disorder," not "an addict."
Stigmatizing words to avoidAlcoholic · abuser · addict · drunk. If a person uses such a term for themselves that is their right; providers still do not use it.
CountertransferenceWhen a healthcare worker unconsciously displaces feelings about significant figures from their own past onto the patient.
Preventing countertransferenceSelf-assessment — examine your own attitudes about substance use, and recognize that past experiences shape how you provide care to people with addictions.
ComorbidityTwo or more co-occurring chronic disorders that can interact and worsen each other. About 25% of people have both a mental and a physical disorder.
Dual diagnosisCo-occurring mental illness and a substance or addictive disorder. Impairment may be mild, moderate or severe.

The Biology of Addiction

Biological Risk

  • Genetic predisposition: addiction runs in families, with a higher concordance rate among siblings and children. Genes influence how drugs are metabolized, how intensely the brain responds to dopamine, and the risk of impulsivity.
  • Neurotransmitter disruption: dopamine is the most central — pleasure, reward, motivation. Serotonin — mood and emotional regulation. GABA and glutamate — drugs disrupt the calming/excitatory balance, causing sedation or anxiety. Endorphins — opioids mimic them, modulating pain and increasing pleasure.
  • Brain structures: nucleus accumbens — the main reward center, flooded with dopamine · prefrontal cortex · amygdala — links use to cues and triggers as emotional memory · hippocampus — reinforces cravings.
  • Immature brain development: the adolescent brain is not fully developed until about age 24 — poor judgment and riskier behavior.

Environmental Risk

  • Chronic stressors and socioeconomic factors — poverty and unfavorable living environments.
  • Anxiety.
  • Substance use or addiction in family or peers; parental factors and lack of supervision matter most in adolescence.
  • Exposure to physical, sexual or emotional abuse, or trauma — any trauma exposure is a major risk factor.
  • Access to an addictive substance.
  • Ineffective coping strategies — coping shifts onto the substance, so teaching positive coping is the counter.

The Reward Pathway

Dopamine Surge

The substance floods the brain with dopamine — up to 5 to 10 times the normal level, hijacking the brain's natural reward system.

Reinforcement

With dopamine elevated, the brain remembers and associates the substance with euphoric reward.

Craving & Repeat Use

Cravings develop and the person seeks the substance again to reproduce the feeling. Low dopamine between uses brings anhedonia.

The mesolimbic reward pathway

Different substances act differently: cocaine stops reuptake · amphetamines push more dopamine out · heroin makes dopamine neurons fire more · alcohol triggers more dopamine release.

The eight neurotransmitters and what each one does are taught in Neurobiology & Pharmacology

Alcohol Craving and Acetate

  • Alcohol breaks down into acetate, a metabolic byproduct — and acetate triggers a craving for more acetate.
  • In a normal drinker acetate is cleared quickly and exits the body. In alcohol use disorder it lingers, because the liver and pancreas process alcohol at only one-third to one-tenth the normal rate.
  • The first drink is barely processed out, so it triggers craving for more. The next drink adds to what is left, compounding the craving and driving roughly twice the intake of a normal drinker.
  • Buildup overwhelms self-control, control is lost, and the craving cycle has begun.
The craving effect never resets. The way acetate triggers cravings does not change over time, even with abstinence — after years without alcohol, one drink biologically reignites the accumulation cycle. This is why abstinence is critical.

Intoxication, Tolerance & Withdrawal

TermDefinition
Substance intoxicationRecent or in-process overuse or excessive use of a substance — the height of the substance in the bloodstream. Symptoms are substance-specific and can occur with a single use. Produces impaired judgment, maladaptive behaviors, impaired social and occupational functioning, and CNS changes disrupting physiological and psychological function.
ToleranceThe person no longer responds to a substance as they did initially, so they use increasing amounts over time to achieve the original level of response. Cocaine causes rapid physiological tolerance; prescription opioids may take weeks or months.
Substance withdrawalPhysiological symptoms that occur when a person stops using a substance. Specific to each substance, ranging from mild to life-threatening. The more intense the symptoms, the more likely the person is to resume use to escape them.

Opioid Use Disorder

Common opioids: oxycodone, morphine, codeine. The most common in street use now are heroin, fentanyl and oxycodone. Prescription overuse came first; when pain medications became expensive people turned to cheaper heroin, and fentanyl is now widely mixed into other drugs.

Intoxication & Overdose

Opioid Intoxication

  • Pinpoint pupils (miosis) — a major sign.
  • Bradycardia · hypotension · hypothermia.
  • Sedation and calmness; head nodding.
  • Hypokinesis — slowed movement; slurred speech.
  • Altered mood — euphoria.

Recognizing an Overdose

  • Drowsy, difficult to wake, or unconscious.
  • Respirations slow, shallow or absent.
  • Nails and/or lips blue or purple; skin pale, cool and clammy.
  • Choking, snoring or gurgling sounds.
  • Pinpoint pupils.
  • The three cardinal signs: coma, pinpoint pupils, respiratory depression.
Opioid overdose is an emergency — opioids suppress the part of the brain that regulates breathing, and it can kill. Outside hospital, call 9-1-1 and give naloxone if available. In hospital, promote breathing first — aspirate secretions, insert an airway, mechanically ventilate as needed — and give naloxone. Naloxone has a shorter half-life than opioids, so keep watching and repeat the dose if signs return. Send patients home with a naloxone kit and teach the signs of severe intoxication and impending overdose.

Withdrawal & the COWS

SystemSigns of Opioid Withdrawal
Vital signsTachycardia · hypertension · hyperthermia · increased respiratory rate — the opposite of intoxication.
Eyes, nose, skinEnlarged pupils · lacrimation (tearing) · rhinorrhea (runny nose) · diaphoresis · piloerection (gooseflesh).
MusculoskeletalMuscle spasms · bone or joint aches · hyperreflexia · tremor.
GIAbdominal cramps, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea.
Neuro-behavioralInsomnia · yawning · anxiety or irritability · restlessness.

COWS

Clinical Opiate Withdrawal Scale. Items scored: resting pulse rate, sweating, restlessness, pupil size, bone or joint aches, runny nose or tearing, GI upset, tremor, yawning, anxiety or irritability, gooseflesh skin.

Scores: 5–12 mild · 13–24 moderate · 25–36 moderately severe · >36 severe.

Technique: assess pupils in good lighting, comparing with another person's if unsure; check gooseflesh by running fingertips over the inner forearm; have the patient sit for five minutes first, since activity raises heart rate. A protocol tied to the score says when to medicate.

Medication-Assisted Treatment

MedicationRole in Opioid Use Disorder
MethadoneThe first medication marketed for opioid withdrawal; used less now. A synthetic narcotic that acts like heroin, morphine and fentanyl but far more slowly, flattening both the highs and the withdrawal lows. Significant overdose potential — must be monitored, and dependence is still possible. For replacement therapy, stabilize first, then taper gradually to prevent more severe withdrawal.
BuprenorphineLong-acting and slow, much like methadone. Reduces cravings and withdrawal symptoms so the patient can focus on recovery, without methadone's overdose potential.
Buprenorphine & naloxoneUsed together to help people reduce or quit opioids. Produces a weaker euphoria than heroin or methadone, relieves cravings well, and reduces the mortality seen in opioid use disorder.
NaltrexoneBlocks the effects of opioids and alcohol, reducing cravings and preventing relapse — and blocking the high if the person relapses. Does not treat withdrawal symptoms.
ClonidineAn antihypertensive and alpha agonist that reduces withdrawal symptoms by blocking the neurotransmitters that trigger sympathetic nervous system activity.
LofexidineA newer alpha agonist, like clonidine, used to relieve opioid withdrawal symptoms — especially on abrupt discontinuation.
Naltrexone requires 10 to 14 days opioid-free before the first dose. Starting it sooner precipitates a rapid, severe withdrawal and makes the patient very sick.

Setting: opioid withdrawal is typically managed outpatient — extremely uncomfortable, but not deadly the way alcohol or benzodiazepine withdrawal is. Psychological treatments: individual therapy, behavioral therapy, CBT, family therapy, social skills training, and support groups such as Narcotics Anonymous.

Maintenance in Pregnancy

  • Pregnant women are detoxed in hospital, or placed on maintenance: start at a dose comparable to their prior use, then titrate upward as pregnancy progresses, with twice-daily dosing as an option instead of one larger dose. Titration follows opioid withdrawal scoring. Maintained until delivery, then withdrawn.
  • Maintenance decreases dose variability and limits exposure to shared needles, unknown toxic additives and the intoxication-withdrawal swing — a more predictable outcome for mother and fetus.
  • The fetus experiences withdrawal symptoms 2–3 times as intense as the mother's, and a fetus can die from withdrawal. Maintenance reduces infant mortality and newborn withdrawal.
  • Methadone was the standard; buprenorphine/naloxone is now preferred.

Alcohol Use Disorder

ItemDetail
Blood alcohol concentrationAlcohol intoxication is a blood concentration of 80 to 100 mg ethanol per deciliter (mg/dL), also expressed as 0.08 to 0.10 g/dL. No alcohol reads as 0.00. In Kentucky the DUI threshold is 0.08.
Effects at BAC 0.08%Poor muscle coordination · altered speech and hearing · difficulty detecting danger · impaired judgment · poor self-control · difficulty reasoning. Impaired judgment is why so many drive while intoxicated — they believe they can.
DSM-5 diagnosisAt least 2 of 11 criteria within a 12-month period.
SeverityBased on the number of symptoms: mild 2–3 · moderate 4–5 · severe 6 or more.

Alcoholic Hallucinosis

The hallucinations of alcohol withdrawal are usually tactile or visual rather than the auditory ones seen in schizophrenia — commonly the sensation of bugs crawling on the skin, or seeing people or things that are not there.

Withdrawal After the Last Drink

Time Since Last DrinkWhat Appears
6–8 hoursMinor symptoms: agitation, impaired cognition, lack of appetite, nausea, vomiting, insomnia, tremulousness, anxiety, mild perceptual changes, and rising blood pressure, pulse and temperature.
8–10 hoursPsychotic and perceptual symptoms begin — alcoholic hallucinosis: visual, auditory or tactile hallucinations.
12–24 hoursWithdrawal seizures — generalized tonic-clonic seizures, if withdrawal is untreated.
Anytime within first 72 hoursAlcohol withdrawal delirium (delirium tremens) — autonomic hyperactivity (tachycardia, diaphoresis, fever, anxiety, insomnia, hypertension), delusions, and visual and tactile hallucinations.

CIWA & Managing Withdrawal

ItemDetail
What CIWA isThe Clinical Institute Withdrawal Assessment for alcohol — the tool for sedative-hypnotic and alcohol withdrawal.
Items scoredNausea/vomiting · tremors · anxiety · agitation · paroxysmal sweats · headache · tactile disturbance · auditory disturbance · visual disturbance · orientation.
Scores0–9 minimal withdrawal · 10–19 mild-to-moderate · >20 severe. Most items score 0–7; orientation scores 0–4.
FrequencyUsually ordered every 4 hours, repeated PRN — sometimes only 30 minutes later.
GoalsControl agitation, decrease the risk of seizures, and reduce morbidity and mortality.
Medication thresholdFor a CIWA-Ar score of 8 to 10 or more: chlordiazepoxide, diazepam or lorazepam. Example fixed regimen: diazepam 10 mg every 6 hours for four doses, then 5 mg every 6 hours for eight doses, with PRN dosing for breakthrough autonomic symptoms.

Practice Points

  • Assess tremor by having the patient extend the tongue and hold hands out with fingers spread; resting a fingertip on theirs makes involuntary movement easy to feel.
  • Diazepam is the current standard at UK HealthCare, replacing chlordiazepoxide, because its long half-life works better and it is particularly effective at decreasing seizures.
  • With a history of seizures, gabapentin is typically started as well — prior seizures raise the risk of withdrawal seizures.
  • Alcohol withdrawal is usually over in 3 to 5 days and peaks around 48 hours.

Delirium Tremens

DTs are the most severe form of alcohol withdrawal and a medical emergency that can result in death. Hepatitis or pancreatitis increases the likelihood, through impaired toxin filtering. Rarely seen in people in good physical health — which many with alcohol use disorder are not, since eating and self-care often stop. There is a danger of misdiagnosis as a psychiatric disorder.

Symptoms

  • Agitation · increased anxiety.
  • Gross confusion and disorientation.
  • Coarse tremors · seizures.
  • Delusions · hallucinations (visual, tactile) · paranoia.
  • Autonomic hyperactivity — tachycardia, diaphoresis, fever, anxiety, insomnia, hypertension.

Prevention Is the Goal

  • Assess and monitor closely — symptoms, labs, electrolytes, vital signs. If the patient reports worsening withdrawal, rescore immediately.
  • Hydrate and medicate adequately; follow the protocol.
  • Provide life support and life-sustaining measures as indicated — suctioning, padded bed rails.

Medication-Assisted Treatment for Alcohol Use Disorder

A combination of medication with individual and behavioral therapies is what sustains recovery. Treat withdrawal symptoms symptomatically — nausea/vomiting, anxiety, headache — and replace thiamine daily; the standard is a multivitamin, thiamine and folic acid.

MedicationRole
NaltrexoneBlocks the euphoric effects of opioids and alcohol, so relapse produces no high, and reduces cravings for alcohol. Long-acting; given IM. Does not treat withdrawal symptoms.
DisulfiramGiven to stop drinking by causing severely unpleasant flu-like illness if the patient drinks while taking it.
AcamprosateReduces cravings, helping maintain abstinence. Does not treat withdrawal symptoms.
Disulfiram teaching: alcohol is not only in drinks. Patients must avoid perfumes, aftershaves, mouthwash, many liquid medications (especially nighttime formulas) and paint thinners. Even splashing on aftershave can make them severely ill.

All three of these are recovery-phase medications — none treats withdrawal.

Aversion therapy, the behavioral therapy disulfiram belongs to, is taught in Assessment, Theories & Therapies

Wernicke-Korsakoff Syndrome

A neurological disorder caused by lack of thiamine (B1) that requires immediate treatment, consisting of two stages. Chronic alcohol use decreases intestinal absorption of thiamine by about 70%. Other causes of thiamine deficiency must be ruled out — eating disorders, bariatric surgery, poor nutrition, chronic infection, or surgery removing part of the intestine.

Wernicke's Encephalopathy

  • Acute and reversible. May clear within a few weeks, or progress to Korsakoff syndrome.
  • Treatment: thiamine replacement in large doses, IV, 2–3 times daily for 1–2 weeks — typically about 10 days.
  • Symptoms: confusion · ataxia — loss of muscle coordination, posture and balance problems, tremors · double vision, droopy eyelid, nystagmus (abnormal eye movements).

Korsakoff Syndrome

  • Chronic and debilitating, and not typically reversible.
  • Treatment: thiamine replacement for 3–12 months, varying with symptoms, severity and whether psychosis is present.
  • Outcome: most never fully recover — recovery rate only about 20%, though cognition may improve with thiamine and nutritional support. Many require residential care.
  • Symptoms: everything seen in Wernicke's, plus severe, irreversible, persistent memory impairment — including anterograde amnesia, difficulty forming new memories · confabulation, filling memory gaps with invented detail rather than lying · hallucinations, auditory and visual · repetitious speech and actions · problems with decision-making, planning, organizing and completing tasks.

Applying the Clinical Judgment Model

Recognize Cues: Screening Tools

Be open, kind and non-judgmental — most patients accept screening, are open to advice on quitting or cutting down, and show more readiness to change than expected. Read the instructions before screening a patient so the protocol is followed exactly.

ToolWhat It Covers
AUDITAlcohol Use Disorders Identification Test — a 10-item tool developed by the WHO covering alcohol consumption, drinking behaviors and alcohol-related problems.
CRAFFTSix key questions for ages 12–21: Car, Relax, Alone, Forget, Family or friends, Trouble. A score of 2 or more indicates higher likelihood of a substance use disorder and needs further evaluation; 4 or more raises suspicion of dependence.
CAGECut down · Annoyed by criticism · Guilty about drinking · Eye-opener, a drink first thing in the morning. A score of 2 or more is significant; a score of 1 requires further assessment.
CAGE-AIDThe same questions Adapted to Include Drugs, not alcohol alone.

Why It Exists

Screening, Brief Intervention, and Referral to Treatment — a comprehensive, integrated approach to early intervention for people with or at risk for substance use and addictive disorders.

Completed by any healthcare professional in any healthcare setting, screening for alcohol, illicit drugs, and prescription drug misuse.

Goal: reduce and prevent related health consequences, disease, accidents and injuries, and reduce costs and healthcare utilization.

From Cues to Action

StepWhat the Nurse Does
Analyze cuesComprehensive, holistic assessment of background · pattern of substance use · mental health symptoms · trauma history · family history of substance use or mental health problems · strengths and level of willingness to change.
Prioritize hypothesesRisk for self-harm or suicide · risk for overdose · withdrawal · impaired sleep · impaired coping · social isolation · denial · hopelessness · dysfunctional family process.
Generate solutionsRealistic short- and long-term goals, patient-centered and based on the assessment, weighing the patient's ability to recognize the problem and readiness to change. Example: for withdrawal risk, control symptoms and return to pre-withdrawal functioning; for impaired sleep, "reports fewer than two awakenings per shift."
Take actionMonitor vital signs and for withdrawal · administer medications as prescribed · develop the therapeutic relationship · gradually reintroduce healthy food and push hydration · support and encourage self-care · promote safety and sleep · assess and teach new coping skills · assist with goal setting · health teaching.
Evaluate outcomesReassess whether interventions achieved the intended goals. Outcomes are determined by the patient and mutually supported by the patient and interdisciplinary staff, so feedback from patient, family and support system is essential. Ask how recovery is going, and what barriers defeated previous attempts.

Discharge, Relapse Prevention & the Care Continuum

Discharge Planning

  • Promote participation in an outpatient support system. Many outpatient programs will not accept a patient until they have spoken with them personally, so encourage and prompt the patient to make those calls before discharge.
  • Help identify alternative sources of coping; support health promotion and maintenance.
  • Relapse prevention: teach the warning signs and develop a relapse prevention plan — ask "How will you handle stress so you don't go back to using?"
  • Involving relatives in treatment decreases relapse rates by 20 to 50 percent.

Care Continuum

  • Detoxification — medically managed.
  • Rehabilitation.
  • Halfway houses — residential, substance-free communal living that allows independent growth.
  • Partial hospitalization programs.
  • Intensive outpatient programs (IOPs) — non-residential, highly structured scheduled treatment groups with at least one regular individual session.
  • Outpatient treatment · medication management · Alcoholics Anonymous (AA) and Narcotics Anonymous · relapse prevention programs. Smartphone apps can track behavior patterns and relapse cues.

Patient & Family Education

  1. Nature of the illness — effects of the substance on the body, and the ways use affects life.
  2. Management of the illness — activities to substitute for the substance in times of stress; increasing coping skills.
  3. Problem-solving skills.
  4. Essentials of good nutrition.
  5. Relaxation techniques — progressive relaxation, tense-and-relax, deep breathing, mindful meditation, imagery, yoga.

Recovery is possible — instill hope. Encourage self-responsibility, setting life goals, improving coping skills, and adherence to the treatment plan. Participation in group, individual and family therapy, peer support through NA/AA 12-step programs, and harm-reduction strategies all matter.
