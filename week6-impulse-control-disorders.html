Impulse Control Disorders

The Three Impulse Control Disorders

Oppositional defiant disorder · conduct disorder · intermittent explosive disorder. Seen mostly in mental health care settings and the criminal justice system. These patients can be hard to care for because they resist help and are self-defeating and unkind. Remembering the tragic etiology of these disorders increases empathy and therapeutic responses, and nurse self-assessment is essential.

Oppositional Defiant Disorder

Behavior that exceeds the boundaries of what is socially acceptable, well beyond ordinary childhood defiance, with significant impairment in social, academic or occupational functioning. The criteria: a pattern of angry or irritable mood, argumentative or defiant behavior, or vindictiveness lasting at least 6 months. It may show in one or more settings, at home but not at school, or both.

Clinical Features

  • Negative, hostile, defiant, vindictive.
  • Irritable and angry mood. Swearing, loss of temper.
  • Mood lability: calm and sweet, then an angry outburst out of the blue.
  • Low frustration tolerance: cannot tolerate being told no.
  • Interpersonal conflict: argumentative, disobedient, deliberately annoys and blames others.
  • Does not see themselves as angry or oppositional, or as wrong.
  • Stubborn, resists direction, tests limits, ignores rules, unwilling to negotiate with adults.

Course and Risk Factors

  • At risk for conduct disorder, which is more severe, and for anxiety and mood disorders and early substance use.
  • Most children outgrow it.
  • Genetic component: runs in families, with a family history of mental illness.
  • Neurobiological: abnormalities in the prefrontal cortex and amygdala, decreased heart rate and basal cortisol reactivity.
  • Environment: family dysfunction, adverse childhood experiences, harsh, inconsistent or neglectful child rearing.
  • Temperament.

Treating ODD

  • Psychosocial: parent management training, which builds parenting skills that are more consistently positive and less frustrating, plus group therapy, anger management, individual and family therapy, cognitive problem-solving training and social skills training.
  • Psychobiological: the FDA has not approved any medication for ODD. divalproex sodium (valproic acid) is used to control anger and aggression.

Conduct Disorder

Repetitive, persistent patterns of behavior that violate the basic rights of others or major age-appropriate societal norms or rules. Far more severe than ODD. Onset in late childhood or early adolescence, with a guarded prognosis.

Behaviors

  • Lacks empathy and does not feel guilty. The only remorse is at being caught.
  • Aggression toward people and animals, including cruelty to animals and smaller children.
  • Destruction of property. Pyromania and kleptomania.
  • Persistent violation of the basic rights of others.
  • Risk-taking behaviors, including substance use and sexual activity.
  • Argumentative, irritable, angry, defiant, hostile. Unmanageable at home and disruptive in the community.

Risk Factors

  • Physical and sexual abuse, and exposure to violence.
  • Inconsistent parenting with harsh discipline.
  • Lack of supervision.
  • Early institutional living or out-of-home placement.
  • Association with a delinquent peer group.
  • Parental substance abuse or criminality.
  • Biologic: genetics, family mental illness, slower resting heart rate and increased testosterone.

Treating Conduct Disorder

  • No medication is FDA-approved for conduct disorder alone, so medication treats the symptoms. Six classes are used: antidepressants, mood stabilizers, stimulants, antipsychotics (for aggression), anticonvulsants and adrenergic medications (for epinephrine and norepinephrine imbalance).
  • Family supportive training, psychosocial interventions, anger management and parent management training.

Intermittent Explosive Disorder

Inability to control aggressive impulses, verbal or physical, directed at people, animals, property or the self. An overreaction in which anything can trigger aggression, and the person may attack anyone who intervenes. Mean onset 13 to 21 years, though it may start younger, and more common in males.

Tension and Arousal

Triggered by something in the environment, such as a slow driver in the left lane.

Explosive Behavior

Aggression: tailgating, cutting someone off, punching a wall, breaking furniture.

Relief and Release

Felt immediately afterward.

Remorse

Reality hits, and the person regrets the danger they caused.

Do Not Give Benzodiazepines in Intermittent Explosive Disorder

Benzodiazepines reduce inhibitions, and these patients already struggle to control their impulses.

Nursing Care for All Three

Assess Continually for Suicide and Violence

Impulsivity and aggression make suicide attempts and other-directed violence more likely. Continual assessment of suicide risk and risk of violence toward others is essential. Nursing diagnoses focus on protecting self and others from impulsive and premeditated acts, then on coping skills and self-esteem.

Psychosocial Interventions

  • Promote a climate of safety for the patient and others.
  • Establish rapport.
  • Set limits and expectations.
  • Consistently follow through with consequences of rule-breaking.
  • Provide structure and boundaries.
  • Provide activities and opportunities for achieving goals, promoting a sense of purpose.
  • Correct patterns of blaming others and denying responsibility, building mature, adaptive coping and prosocial goals.

Nursing Interventions with the Family

  • Explore the impact of the child's behavior on family life, and of the family on the child.
  • Make the home safe, especially regarding weapons and drugs. Talk to members separately if possible.
  • Discuss realistic behavioral goals and how to set them.
  • Teach behavior modification, role-playing problem situations with parents.
  • Support and encourage parents as they apply new techniques.
  • Educate about medications.
  • Refer to self-help groups and supportive services.
  • Advocate with the school if special education services are needed.

Usually treated outpatient. Inpatient hospitalization is for crisis intervention or treatment planning, and many children move to therapeutic foster care, group homes or residential treatment. Intensive programs include multisystemic therapy and community-based treatment teams.

Advanced Practice Interventions

Cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT) · psychodynamic psychotherapy · dialectical behavior therapy (DBT) · parent-child interaction therapy (PCIT) · parent management training (PMT) · multisystemic therapy (MST)

What is oppositional defiant disorder?

They do not see themselves as angry or oppositional, and blame others.
