Neurobiology & Pharmacology

Visualizing the Brain

Brain imaging began only in the 1990s, so the physiology of mental illness is still a relatively new field.

Structured Imaging

  • Shows anatomy; locates tumors, atrophy, and other abnormalities.
  • Computed tomography (CT)
  • Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)

Functional Imaging

  • Shows brain function and physiological activity: the changes that correlate with behavioral or cognitive alterations.
  • Positron emission tomography (PET)
  • Single photon emission computed tomography (SPECT)
  • Functional MRI (fMRI)
Brains with mental illness look different from brains without it. PET shows reduced brain activity (darker colors) in depression, especially in the prefrontal cortex. After treatment and recovery the entire brain, and particularly the left prefrontal cortex, is more active again.

Major Brain Functions

FunctionHow It Works
Maintaining homeostasisKeeping the internal environment stable despite the external one. The brain is the control center, regulating through the nervous and endocrine systems.
Negative feedback loopsA change is detected (low blood sugar) → the brain initiates a response (hunger) → the person eats → balance is restored.
Regulating the ANS and hormonesThe autonomic nervous system runs involuntary functions: sympathetic = fight or flight, speeds body functions up; parasympathetic = rest and digest, slows them down. The endocrine system links the brain to cardiac muscle, smooth muscle, and glands.
Biological drives and behaviorSex, hunger, the sleep-wakefulness cycle, and circadian rhythms.
Conscious mental activityThinking, memory, problem-solving, and interpretation of the external world.
Why a mental disturbance produces physical signs: anxiety activates the sympathetic nervous system, so the person doesn't just feel anxious; they get increased heart rate, increased blood pressure, sweating, and shortness of breath.

Core CNS Structures

StructureWhat It Does
ThalamusThe main relay station of the CNS: sensory pathways from the spinal cord, cerebellum, and brainstem form synapses here on their way to the cerebral cortex.
HypothalamusThe major respiratory center, holding the basic vital functions: temperature, appetite, sleep, sex drive, heart rate, and blood pressure.
CerebellumMotor coordination. The coiled structure under the occipital lobe, coordinating voluntary movement, equilibrium, and muscle tone.
BrainstemThe central core of the brain, made mostly of nerve fibers, in three areas: midbrain, pons, and medulla. Provides the main motor and sensory innervation to the face and neck through the cranial nerves; 10 of the 12 pairs come from the brainstem. Regulates cardiac and respiratory function, the CNS, consciousness, and the sleep cycle.

The Cerebral Lobes & What Damage Looks Like

LobeWhat It DoesWhat Damage Produces
FrontalWhere thought processes occur. Executive function: distinguishing good from bad, judging consequences, goal-directed behavior; planning, organizing, control of emotions and behavior, and part of personality.Inability to plan sequences; loss of spontaneity; persistence of a single thought (perseveration); mood changes (emotionally labile) and personality changes; difficulty with problem-solving and focusing; inability to express language (Broca's aphasia).
ParietalPerception, spelling, arithmetic, and reading: making sense of the world.Problems with math, reading, drawing, and naming objects; difficulty distinguishing left from right; lack of awareness of certain body parts or surrounding space; difficulty controlling movement (apraxia).
TemporalReceiving and identifying sensory information: memory, understanding, auditory, language.Difficulty recognizing faces (prosopagnosia); difficulty understanding spoken words (Wernicke's aphasia); difficulty identifying and verbalizing what an object is; short- and long-term memory loss; persistent talking; increased aggressive behavior.
OccipitalThe vision center.Defects in vision (visual field cuts); hallucinations; inaccurately seeing objects (visual illusions); inability to recognize words and drawn objects; inability to recognize movement (movement agnosia).
Illusion vs. hallucination. An illusion: something really is there but is seen incorrectly, such as a coat tree across the room perceived as a person. A hallucination: nothing is there.

Where These Show Up Clinically

  • Frontal: perseveration plays a role in OCD; mood and lability in bipolar disorder.
  • Parietal: lack of awareness of body parts or surrounding space is seen a lot in schizophrenia and in nihilistic delusions. Plaque builds up here in Alzheimer's, and people lose the ability to draw.
  • Temporal: persistent talking is what you see in mania.

Putting It Together: How a Touch Is Processed

StepWhat Happens
1. Peripheral nervous systemThe touch signal travels to the sensory cortex.
2. Sensory cortexEvaluates it: what does that feel like?
3. HippocampusSearches for a memory of being touched like this. Good or bad? What do I expect next?
4. AmygdalaAttaches the emotional connection of that earlier remembered touch.
5. Basal gangliaCauses you to act: withdraw in fear, return the touch with similar gentleness, or strike back with similar force.

The Neurotransmitters

Neurotransmitter: a substance released when a presynaptic neuron is excited, acting by either inhibiting or exciting a target cell. Released from vesicles at the end of the axons across the synaptic cleft, to receptors on the other side.

Too much or too little of these neurotransmitters is believed to cause neurological and mental illness. Drugs act on them by enhancing release, inhibiting release, or blocking synthesis; many also block reuptake, so a higher concentration stays in the synaptic gap.

There are hundreds of neurotransmitters; this course covers eight.
Inhibitory: serotonin · GABA
Excitatory: norepinephrine · epinephrine · dopamine · glutamate
Other: acetylcholine · histamine

Neuroplasticity & Synaptic Pruning

Neuroplasticity

  • The brain's lifelong ability to change with learning, reorganizing neural pathways based on new experiences.
  • As we learn and acquire knowledge and skills, new pathways are laid down; learning a fact or skill requires persistent functional changes in the brain.
  • This is why repetition of a skill or a behavior change matters.

Synaptic Pruning

  • The neurological process that removes unnecessary or damaged neuronal structures.
  • Weeds out the weaker, seldom-used synapses, improving the brain's "networking" capacity.
  • The simpler associations formed in childhood are thought to be replaced by learning and more complex structures.
  • Ongoing, but it speeds up in prenatal development through age two, the preteen years, and midlife.
The link to schizophrenia: one biological theory is over-pruning, which can result in hallucinations; the timing fits the pruning surge in late adolescence and young adulthood.

Psychopharmacology Principles

PrincipleRationale
Start low and titrate up slowlyTitrate to the patient's response; if the response is there, no increase may be needed.
Hold or switch for significant side effectsThe medication may be held temporarily, or changed.
Start only one new medication at a timeSo you can identify which medication caused a side effect or reaction.
Weigh the risks against the benefitsEspecially in older adults and in polypharmacy (many prescribed medications).
Use the lowest effective doseEnough for the benefit, with fewer side effects. Especially in older adults, who may be more sensitive, and in children.
Taper; never stop abruptlyGradually reduce to prevent withdrawal, rebound, or worsening of symptoms.

What Changes How a Medication Behaves

  • Other medications: increase or decrease a drug's metabolism.
  • Diet: affects how medications are absorbed and metabolized, including half-life (the time the body takes to halve the drug's concentration). Matters most with a narrow therapeutic index or easy toxicity; lithium, where sodium and fluid intake matter, is the key example.
  • Genetic differences: fast versus slow metabolizers, affecting both drug levels and side effects. Genetic variation differs across populations, so psychiatric prescribing has to be individualized.

Discontinuation Syndrome

Antidepressants are not addicting (people do not abuse them or crave them), but the body does get used to them. A sudden decrease in dosage, or a sudden stop of certain psychotropic medications, causes discontinuation syndrome. Teach patients that the medication has to be taken for a certain length of time, and that coming off it has to be done slowly.
What HappensDetail
Rebound or relapseThe original symptoms come back.
New symptoms appearPhysical or psychological symptoms that were not there before.
