Suicide

Epidemiology

MeasureFigure

US deaths48,824 Americans died by suicide in 2024.
Global deathsMore than 700,000 people worldwide each year — a rate of 8.96 per 100,000. Over 77% occur in low- and middle-income countries.
AttemptsFor every 1 person who dies by suicide, 20 more have attempted.
SexOver twice as high among men as women.

Why This Matters on Every Unit

  • Every area of a health care facility admits patients with suicidal thoughts, not just mental health units — and you may not know it on admission.
  • All RNs care for patients after suicide attempts; depending on method and severity, many are in ICUs or on medical units.
  • Warning signs are easy to miss when the focus is physical health, so every patient admitted or seen in the ED is screened.

Key Terms & Concepts

TermDefinition
Suicidal ideation (SI)Thinking about, considering, or planning to kill oneself.
Suicidal planThe organized plan and time frame for killing oneself.
Suicide attemptA non-fatal self-directed potentially injurious behavior with any intent to die.
Suicide / death by suicide / fatal self-directed violenceThe voluntary and intentional act of taking one's own life. All of these describe the same thing.
Suicide intentAt the time of the injury or death, the person had thoughts of killing themselves or wished to die.
Suicide survivorThe family and friends grieving a loved one's suicide — not the person who attempted.
Suicidal self-directed violenceBehavior that is self-directed and deliberately results in injury or the potential for injury to oneself, with evidence of suicidal intent.
Non-suicidal self-directed violenceThe same behavior, but with no evidence of suicidal intent.
Interrupted self-directed violence — by otherThe person takes steps to injure themselves but is stopped by another person before the fatal injury, at any point during the behavior or the thought of suicide.
Interrupted self-directed violence — by selfThe person takes steps to injure themselves but stops themselves before the fatal injury.
Suicide contagion / clusterAn increase in suicides when stories about suicide increase, are reported at length, are placed on the front page or at the beginning of a broadcast, concern a broadly known person, or carry a particularly dramatic headline. It is why mental health professionals go into a school after a student's suicide, and why press guidelines ask reporters to omit graphic details — detailing a method increases the use of that method.
Suicidal Behavior DisorderA DSM-5 condition for further study — suicide itself is not classified as a mental disorder. Criteria: an attempt within the last 24 months, not initiated in delirium or confusion, not undertaken solely for a religious or political objective, and not meeting the criteria for nonsuicidal self-injury.

Unacceptable Terms

Do Not UseWhy
Completed suicideImplies an achievement, as though it were a good thing, and reinforces stigma. Substitute death by suicide or died by suicide.
Failed attemptThe person already feels bad enough, and it invites thoughts like "I can't even kill myself — I must really be a loser."
Nonfatal suicideNo longer used.
Parasuicide / suicide gestureAn apparent attempt where the intent was to bring attention to a personal problem rather than to die — a sublethal drug overdose, for example.
SuicidalityToo broad — it covers ideation, plans, and attempts all at once.

Risk Factors & Etiology

No single factor causes suicide. It is usually a combination of factors plus a triggering event that tips the balance. Risk is assessed across the biological, psychological, and sociocultural domains.

Risk FactorWhat Is Known
Previous attemptsAbout half of those who die by suicide had a history of a previous attempt.
Psychiatric disordersApproximately 60% of suicides are by individuals who had a psychiatric illness at the time.
Alcohol or substance useAccount for 60%–90% of suicides, thought to be related to the destabilizing, disinhibiting effects of substances.
Male genderMales complete about 70% of suicides, but women make more attempts — males use more lethal means such as firearms or hanging, while females predominantly use poisoning. Rural men are at higher risk than urban men, and women's risk increases with exposure to violence or sexual assault.
Physical healthMedical illness raises the risk of chronic depression and so raises suicide risk, and early onset of an illness increases it further. About 75% of elderly patients who attempted suicide had seen their primary care provider in the month before.
Chronic painAny chronic, painful, or disabling illness — chronic headaches, arthritis — and anything that greatly reduces function.
Race85%–90% of suicides are in Caucasians, with a higher rate among American Indians and Alaska Natives ages 15–34.
Religion, marriage, professionEach also affects risk.

Biological Factors

  • Familial tendency — risk rises sharply with a family history of suicide, and concordance is higher among identical than fraternal twins.
  • Lowered SKA2 gene expression; there may also be a familial tendency toward impulsivity or aggressiveness.
  • Low serotonin levels — found in the cerebrospinal fluid of those who attempt suicide, and post-mortem.

Psychological Factors

  • Freud: aggression turned inward.
  • Menninger's three parts to suicide hostility: the wish to kill (revenge), the wish to be killed (guilt), and the wish to die (hopelessness).
  • Aaron Beck: the central emotional factor is hopelessness. When people lose hope, screening and intervention have to intensify.
  • Diathesis-stress model: a combination of suicidal fantasies and a significant loss.
Cultural GroupProtective and Risk Factors
African AmericansProtective: religion, the role of the extended family, and a strong support system.
Hispanic AmericansProtective: Roman Catholic religion and the importance of the extended family. Risk for recent immigrants: not understanding the US health care system, fear of deportation, and language barriers.
Asian AmericansAdherence to religions emphasizing interdependence between the individual and society, so self-destruction is seen as disrespectful or selfish. Belief in reincarnation may make death an honorable solution for the elderly, and rates increase with age.

Societal factors. People are becoming increasingly isolated, which sets the stage for loneliness and despair: texting rather than phoning, no longer carpooling, not meeting neighbors, and not making eye contact or greeting others. Many cultures also carry an unwillingness to seek help because of stigma.

Recognizing & Analyzing Cues

An accurate, complete assessment is the first step to suicide prevention: recognize who is at risk, use evidence-based instruments, and know the warning signs. Two tools: the Columbia-Suicide Severity Rating Scale (C-SSRS), used by UK HealthCare, and SAFE-T — the Suicide Assessment Five-step Evaluation and Triage — described in the textbook.

Recognizing Cues

  • Overt verbal cues — a direct statement of a plan and the means to carry it out.
  • Covert verbal cues — vague statements such as "maybe I could just go to sleep and never wake up."
  • Nonverbal cues — a patient who was participating and smiling on the unit stops interacting with others.

Analyzing Cues

  • Lethality of the plan and how much preparation has gone into it — if they say they will shoot themselves, ask whether they have access to a gun and whether it is in the home.
  • Self-assessment — check your own reactions.
CategoryWarning Signs
StatementsThreatening or talking about hurting or killing oneself — seek professional help immediately; talking or writing about death or dying; saying they see no reason for living or no sense of purpose.
ActionsLooking for the means — guns or pills; giving away prized possessions, including pets; increasing drug or alcohol use; withdrawing from friends and family.
FeelingsGuilt, shame, anger, or seeking revenge; feeling like a burden to others; feeling anxious, agitated, trapped, or hopeless; dramatic mood swings.

Continuing a Gentle Inquiry

  • "Have you wished you were dead, or wished you could go to sleep and not wake up?"
  • "Have you had any thoughts of killing yourself?" — ask directly. Asking about suicide does not increase the risk or plant the idea; it opens the door to help.
  • "What would you do if you had the urge to kill yourself while you are here in the hospital?"
  • "Share with me what is contributing to your thoughts of suicide."
  • "What would keep you from hurting yourself?"
  • "If you have had these thoughts in the past, what have you done to keep yourself safe?"

Suicide Is Often in Disguise

  • Motor vehicle "accidents," especially single-car crashes.
  • Suicide risk underlying other health concerns.
  • Somatic complaints in older patients.
  • Self-injurious behavior; "accidental" poisonings.

Nurse Self-Assessment

  • Health professionals experience fear, grief, anger, puzzlement, and even condemnation of suicidal feelings or intent.
  • Acknowledge those feelings to team members to avoid countertransference.
  • Nurses are at a much higher risk of suicide than the general public, so resilience and self-care are part of the work.

Outcomes, Safety Planning & Protective Factors

StepWhat It Involves
Prioritize hypothesesPrioritize by immediacy — multiple high risk factors plus current intent needs urgent intervention. Highest priority is risk for suicide; safety is always number one. Also self-care deficit, sleep pattern disturbance, low self-esteem, impaired coping, and hopelessness.
Generate solutionsDecreased or resolved suicidal ideation; identifies effective coping strategies or social supports; expresses hope for the future and a will to live; remains free from injury before discharge.
PlanInterprofessional collaboration based on risk and protective factors — mitigate the risks and bring in more protective factors. Involve significant others whenever possible and educate them before discharge.
Do not use no-suicide contracts. They are not evidence-based and must not replace safety planning.

Stanley-Brown Safety Planning Intervention

  • A written six-step plan completed together with the patient, covering warning signs, internal coping strategies, social settings and people who provide distraction, who to ask for help, and where to go in a crisis.
  • It helps the patient identify what is worth living for.
  • In a crisis, thinking is not clear — so the plan, with names and phone numbers, is kept somewhere visible such as the refrigerator or a mirror.

Levels of Intervention

Prevention

Activities that provide support, information, and education to prevent suicide.

Treatment

Treatment of the actual suicidal crisis — the hospitalization itself.

Postvention

Intervention with the circle of survivors left behind, to reduce the traumatic after-effects.

Protective Factors

External

  • Children or pets to care for in the home; family responsibilities; pregnancy.
  • A social support system.
  • Financial incentives or deterrents.
  • Effective access to and care for mental, physical, and substance use disorders.
  • Restricted access to highly lethal means, such as guns.

Internal

  • Meaning in life or a reason to live — a job, loved ones, helping others.
  • Cultural or religious prohibition or belief; spirituality.
  • Hope.
  • Skills in problem solving and conflict resolution; frustration tolerance; optimism.
  • Connectedness to others; successful past coping strategies; effective mental health care.

Internal protective factors are used to identify the patient's strengths, build individualized coping strategies, and plan for what comes up after discharge. Document them on the clinical assessment form.

Environmental Safety & Levels of Observation

Safety is the number one priority. Suicidal patients admitted to general hospitals attempt suicide more rapidly after admission and during shift changes, give fewer threats or warnings, and use more violent means.

Removing the Means

  • Remove anything usable for hanging; place the patient in a hospital gown and limit clothing, bedding, and sheets in the room; no shoelaces.
  • Remove sharps — razors — plus perfume bottles, makeup mirrors, and anything glass.
  • Remove medications from home. Lock the windows; keep the bed locked in the low position.
  • Count disposable trays and plastic utensils, and dispose of them outside the patient's room at the nurses' station.
  • Paper trash-can liners, not plastic bags. No soda cans, glass vases, or pens — pencils only if the metal eraser band is removed, and some units allow crayons only.

Environmental Safety Guidelines

  • No private room; always keep the door open. People generally will not attempt suicide if someone else is in the room.
  • Jump-proof and hang-proof bathrooms — Velcro or paper shower curtains.
  • Lock doors to non-patient areas. When a fire alarm unlocks the doors, post staff to monitor them.
  • Assess belongings and search the patient — a full skin assessment on entry to the unit.
  • Monitor for and remove harmful gifts; check all packages brought in by visitors; ensure visitors and non-clinical staff do not bring or leave harmful objects.
SettingLevel of Observation
Available levelsConstant (1:1) — within arm's reach or line of sight — then every 15 minutes, every 30 minutes, or hourly.
Locked inpatient psych unitLevel is assigned by acuity, and is usually higher at the beginning of an admission or when the patient verbalizes strong suicidal ideation.
Acute care unitUsually 1:1 constant observation by trained staff — it is very hard to monitor for suicide around tubing and equipment. Family members are not allowed to provide 1:1.
Changing the level of observation: it can be decreased only by an MD or NP order. It can be increased by the RN, who then notifies and discusses it with the physician. Patients on precautions are watched even while showering and using the toilet — uncomfortable, but it is both a safety and a legal responsibility.

Other Actions

  • Inform all unit staff of the patient's risk, and note suicide and elopement precautions.
  • Instruct the sitter or patient safety companion at the start of the shift, monitor the sitter for vigilance, teach them to observe the patient even in the bathroom, and schedule their break times.
  • RN hourly rounding with the patient; place the patient close to the nursing station.
  • Monitor for cheeking of medications — have the patient open their mouth and lift the tongue.
  • Identify access to firearms or other means of self-harm at home, and involve the family in removing them.

Interventions, Documentation & Postvention

Psychosocial Interventions

  • Therapeutic use of self; give unconditional positive regard.
  • Open, accepting, trust-building frequent brief interactions; make eye contact and lean forward.
  • Ask the question. Be direct and talk matter-of-factly about suicide.
  • Be available, spend time, stay non-judgmental, and allow expression. Discuss the current crisis in the patient's life.
  • Identify protective factors and successful past coping strategies.
  • At discharge, give the suicide hotline number — 988, or 1-800-273-TALK.

Psychobiological Interventions

  • Low-dose lithium — about 25 mg PO daily for a few weeks. The mechanism is not established, but it reduces suicidal ideation quickly.
  • Nasal ketamine — newer research supports its effectiveness for suicidal ideation.
  • ECT — a fast treatment to lift the depression.

Case Management & Teaching

  • Involve social work; make follow-up appointments before discharge — ideally the patient sees their outpatient provider within 48 hours.
  • Develop agreements and releases of information between providers to make the transition safe, and send patient health information to the referral providers.
  • Teach the family to take any cue of suicide seriously, to be good listeners, and not to leave the person alone.

Postvention for Survivors

  • Survivors feel shock, disbelief, numbness, grief, sadness, despair, confusion, guilt, and self-anger.
  • Allow discussion of these emotions and validate the feelings; provide support systems and resources.
  • Warn that holidays, birthdays, and anniversaries can be very painful, and explain their own risk of depression.
  • Discourage blaming and scapegoating; help them understand the grieving process.

Evaluation is ongoing throughout hospitalization, staying alert to changes in mood, thinking, and behavior. Positive signs: communicating thoughts and feelings more readily, a widening social network, and engaging in problem-solving. Advanced practice interventions: psychotherapy, prescribing psychobiological interventions, clinical supervision, and consultation.
