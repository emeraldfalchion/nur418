Medical Illnesses with Psychiatric Symptoms

Medical or Mental

  • The mind and body are intertwined, and a client is a holistic entity. What affects one system often has consequences on others.
  • A new medical diagnosis or illness is stressful and life changing. Patients treated for medical or surgical problems may display aggression, depression, anxiety, anger, suspicion or psychosis.
  • Identifying a medical condition is challenging, especially in a person with an existing psychiatric condition, who may not be able to report symptoms.
  • Over 100 disorders mimic mental illness, presenting as anxiety, psychosis or mood disorders.

Medical Mimics

Disorders that present with psychiatric symptoms directly resulting from an underlying medical condition. The cause of the psychiatric symptoms is the medical illness.

Diagnostic Overshadowing

Attributing symptoms of a physical illness to a mental illness. Symptoms in a patient with a history of mental illness are more likely to be attributed to the psychiatric illness, before a thorough medical evaluation is done.

Misdiagnosis can result in unnecessary or harmful treatment, delay in treatment, and increased hospital stay and costs.

Psychotic Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition

  • Hallucinations, delusions, and disorganized speech and thought patterns, expected whatever the medical condition is.
  • Symptoms of the medical condition itself.
  • May be temporary and brief, or long-term, persisting long after the associated medical condition has resolved.

Psychiatric Side Effects of Medications

Psychiatric Side Effects (PSEs)

  • Induced by the pharmacological treatment of physical illnesses.
  • Often resemble spontaneous psychiatric syndromes, such as an anxiety, depressive or psychotic disorder.
  • Occur with usual doses, intoxication, or withdrawal of treatment.
  • Range from short-lasting anxiety to severe confusion, and suicide.
  • Most are managed by stopping the offending medication and replacing it with a different one.

Risk Factors for PSEs

  • Polypharmacy
  • High doses
  • Route of administration, and faster administration by any route
  • Narrow therapeutic index
  • Metabolic conditions: hepatic insufficiency, slow metabolizer
  • Augmented permeability of the blood-brain barrier, as in meningitis or porphyria
  • Very young or elderly patients
  • Postpartum or other situations of stress, such as the ICU

The most frequent offenders are the CNS-active drugs: anticonvulsants, antidepressants, antipsychotics, psychostimulants, sedative-hypnotics, dopaminergic anti-Parkinson therapies, anticholinergics and corticosteroids.

Medications That Induce a Psychoactive State

  • Antihypertensives, such as reserpine: hallucinations, depression or agitation
  • Oral contraceptives
  • Steroids. Adrenal insufficiency can cause a severe psychotic disorder.
  • Cancer chemotherapy agents
  • Benzodiazepines: delirium when abused or stopped abruptly
  • Barbiturates
  • Histamine 2 blockers

Commonly Abused Substances

  • Alcohol
  • Opioids
  • Amphetamines: Ecstasy, meth
  • Cocaine
  • Benzodiazepines
  • Barbiturates
  • Steroids, which can lead to steroid psychosis
  • LSD

Recognizing a Medical Mimic

Medical Until Proven Otherwise

Never assume an emotional symptom has a psychosocial cause until physical causes or contributors are fully investigated. The first assumption is that symptoms are medically caused until proven otherwise.

Hallmark Risk Factors

One or more features that are atypical for the psychiatric symptoms:

  • Normal functioning prior to sudden onset of mental symptoms
  • Late onset of initial presentation. Psychosis of unknown cause usually begins in late adolescence or early adulthood.
  • Known underlying medical condition
  • Atypical presentation of a specific psychiatric diagnosis
  • Disturbance of gait, balance, or both
  • Visual, olfactory or tactile hallucinations. Psychiatric syndromes typically bring auditory ones.
  • Illicit or recent substance use

Other High-Risk Features

  • Absence of personal and family history of psychiatric illness
  • Family concern: "This is not his or her usual behavior"
  • Medication use: new medication, recent dose change, overuse
  • Abnormal vital signs (fever, tachycardia, tachypnea) or significant abnormalities on exam (pupil dilation or constriction, nystagmus, signs of physical trauma)
  • Waxing and waning mental status or abnormal mental status exam findings
  • Disorientation with clouded consciousness
  • Recent memory loss
  • History of head injury
  • Treatment resistance or unusual response to treatment: worse after an antipsychotic or anxiolytic

High-Risk Individuals

The elderly · patients with a history of substance abuse · patients without a history of psychiatric illness · patients with preexisting medical illness · patients from lower socioeconomic strata

Laboratory and Metabolic Assessments

The Comprehensive Battery

  • Pulse oximetry
  • CMP (electrolytes, blood glucose, liver function, BUN, creatinine) and CBC
  • Therapeutic drug levels
  • Blood alcohol level
  • UA and UDS
  • Thyroid function testing
  • Erythrocyte sedimentation rate and/or C-reactive protein
  • Screening for syphilis, Lyme disease, lupus and demyelinating disorders
  • AIDS/HIV screening

Additional Tests

  • CT: new-onset symptoms that are atypical or incongruent with other findings, such as delirium, headache, recent trauma or weakness in an extremity.
  • Lumbar puncture: meningeal signs or a normal head CT.
  • Blood and urine cultures: a seriously ill patient with an unexplained fever.
  • Vitamin B12 or thiamine deficiency testing: signs of dementia that are otherwise unexplained.

Nursing Implications and Interventions

Nursing Implications

  • A client is a holistic entity.
  • Be aware of medical mimics: common psychiatric symptoms often arise from medical illnesses.
  • Emotional dimensions may be overlooked in med-surg or primary care.
  • Provide reassurance and support to families and clients.
  • Be sensitive to the emotional experiences of clients with medical illnesses, such as fear, anger, depression and anxiety.

Nursing Interventions

  • Perform an accurate, thorough health history and physical exam on all clients.
  • Ask everyone about substance use, adolescents and the elderly included.
  • Ask all women presenting with psychosis about eating habits and diet pills. Check for hypokalemia and hypocalcemia to rule out starvation effects or reactions to stimulants.
  • Review current prescriptions, OTC and alternative medications, and recent changes, including running out or stopping abruptly.
  • Monitor labs and report abnormal labs immediately.
  • Consider all medications as having the potential to trigger psychiatric symptoms.
