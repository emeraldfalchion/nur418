Neurodevelopmental Disorders

Communication & Motor Disorders

The APA's six neurodevelopmental disorders: communication disorders · motor disorders · specific learning disorder · intellectual developmental disorder · autism spectrum disorders · ADHD.

Speech Disorders

Problems making sounds and in vocalization.

Language Disorders

Difficulty understanding or using words in context and appropriately. May show as an inability to follow directions.

  • Expressive language disorder: inability to develop the skills to communicate verbally or through sign language.
  • Social communication disorder: problems communicating socially, such as frequently interrupting or being minimally verbal.

Communication disorders are deficits in language skill acquisition that impair academic achievement, socialization or self-care. Motor disorders are impairments in gross and fine motor skills, mild to moderate in severity.

Motor DisorderFeatures and Treatment
Developmental coordination disorderCoordination below the child's developmental age, interfering with academics or ADLs: delayed sitting or walking, difficulty jumping or tying shoelaces. Treated with physical and occupational therapy.
Stereotypic movement disorderRepetitive, purposeless movements for 4 weeks or more that interfere with ADLs: hand flapping, rocking, head banging, nail biting, teeth grinding. Interventions focus on safety and injury prevention (helmets, mittens) plus behavioral therapy with habit reversal.
Tic disordersSudden, nonrhythmic, rapid motor movements or vocalizations, usually of the head, torso or limbs. Motor tics include tongue protrusion, touching, squatting and twirling. Vocal tics are spontaneous words and sounds.

Tic Disorders

TypeDuration and Features
Tourette's disorderThe most severe. Multiple motor tics and one or more vocal tics, throughout the day for more than a year, impairing functioning at home, with peers and at school.
Persistent motor or vocal tic disorderLasts more than 1 year.
Provisional tic disorderLasts less than 1 year.

Onset is usually at 4 to 6 years. Tics can be suppressed for brief periods, though not by everyone. Symptoms peak in early adolescence and many diminish by adulthood.

Treating Tic Disorders

  • Behavioral techniques, such as habit reversal.
  • Relaxation strategies to reduce and control tics. Children often feel anxious about fitting in, so these also help them adapt socially. A key nursing intervention to teach.
  • Medications: antipsychotics, clonidine, clonazepam (Klonopin), fluoxetine and sertraline.
  • Deep brain stimulation when more conservative approaches fail.

Deep brain stimulation is explained in Group & Physiologic Therapies

Specific Learning & Intellectual Developmental Disorders

Specific Learning Disorder

Dyslexia (reading) · dyscalculia (math) · dysgraphia (written expression). Identified in the school years when a child has persistent difficulty in one of these areas. Interventions are set out in an Individualized Education Plan (IEP) and provided through special education in public schools.

Intellectual Developmental Disorder (IDD)

Affects males more than females. Deficits in three areas:

DeficitIncludes
Intellectual functioningReasoning, problem solving, planning, judgment, abstract thinking and academic ability, compared with same-age peers.
Social functioningCommunication and language, interpreting and acting on social cues, and regulating emotions.
Daily functioningPractical aspects of daily life: work, school and self-care.

Early identification and intervention increase quality of life. Interventions ideally start around age 3, and cognitive and social stimulation can raise functioning if begun before age 5. Motivational support largely determines adult productivity and independence.

Nursing ProcessIDD
AssessmentAssess for delays and for signs of potential neglect or abuse.
DiagnosisRisk for injury, ineffective impulse control, impaired verbal communication.
OutcomesUses spoken language, engages in simple social interactions, accepts assistance or feedback without frustration, and refrains from acting impulsively or harming self or others. Many resort to self-mutilation, so no self-harm is a key outcome.
ImplementationEarly, consistent, positive interventions. Social skills training and individual, family and cognitive therapy. Motivation unique to each child, since many feel like failures.

Autism Spectrum Disorder

A non-progressive pervasive developmental disorder with symptoms in socialization, communication and behavior. The DSM-5 placed every autism-type disorder under this umbrella, so there is no longer an Asperger's diagnosis. It appears in early childhood, typically the first 3 years, and occurs more in males, but is more severe in females.

Clinical Features

  • Deficits in social interactions and relationships. May be withdrawn and react unusually to others.
  • Stereotypical repetitive speech or behaviors, such as hand flapping and rocking.
  • Obsessive focus on specific objects (fixed interest).
  • Over-adherence to routines or rituals and extreme resistance to change. Disrupted routine can trigger self-harm.
  • Hyper- or hypo-reactivity to sensory input. Poor sensory integration: absorbed in one thing, the child may not notice a fire alarm or their name.
  • May be aggressive toward others or self.

Possible Causes

  • Advanced paternal or maternal age.
  • Spontaneous genetic mutations.
  • Low birth weight and premature birth.
  • Valproic acid taken during pregnancy.
  • Genetic disorders: fragile X, Rett syndrome, Prader-Willi, tuberous sclerosis.
  • The cause is not known. There is no substantiated scientific evidence of a link to vaccines.

Repetitive behavior is managed by its effect on others and the environment. Harmless rocking is best ignored. If the behavior becomes harmful, redirect the child and use positive reinforcement.

Nursing ProcessAutism Spectrum Disorder
AssessmentIntellectual or developmental delays, communication (delayed language, pronoun reversal, abnormal intonation), social and behavioral skills, the parent-child relationship, abuse, and stereotypic behavior (rocking, hand flapping, insistence on sameness, self-injury).
DiagnosisEarly identification is key. A multidisciplinary team, a hearing evaluation, and lead screening, because lead poisoning can mimic the criteria. Screening tools: the CARS (Childhood Autism Rating Scale) and the M-CHAT (Modified Checklist for Autism in Toddlers).
OutcomesCooperation, consideration and sensitivity to others, and accurately interpreting or exchanging messages.
Psychosocial interventionsEarly intervention programs with case management, therapeutic nursery school, day treatment, special education. Behavior management with rewards. Parent teaching on structure, expectations, rewards and behavior modification. PT, OT and speech therapy. CBT once older.
Psychobiological interventionsSecond-generation (atypical) antipsychotics, SSRIs and stimulants, to reduce repetitive or ritualistic behaviors, anxiety, aggression and intrusive thoughts.
EvaluationAre there improvements in interacting with others, and use of services?

Effective Autism Treatment Programs

  • Build on the child's interests.
  • Offer a predictable schedule.
  • Teach tasks as a series of simple steps.
  • Actively engage the child in highly structured activities with a beginning, middle and end.
  • Provide regular positive reinforcement.
  • Teach early communication and social interaction skills.
  • Involve parents, a major factor in treatment success.
  • Social skills training: recognizing social cues, reducing stress, and role-playing to play like peers.
  • Speech and language therapy.

ADHD

A persistent pattern of inattention, hyperactivity and impulsiveness that is pervasive and inappropriate for developmental level, in at least 2 settings (home and school or work), causing work, social or educational difficulties for at least 6 months, before age 12. Boys are affected more than girls. About 50% of cases continue into adulthood. Substance use is increased, and children grow up with low self-esteem, so early identification matters.

Risk factors: genetic (runs in families) · low birth weight or prematurity · frontal head injury · smoking or drinking during pregnancy · lead or pesticide exposure · neurotransmitters and nerve pathways that work differently.

Three types: hyperactivity-impulsivity, inattentive, and combined. The first two each require six or more criteria present for 6 months, and the combined type has behaviors from both.

Hyperactivity-Impulsivity Type

Hyperactivity

  • Fidgets, squirms, can't sit still. Leaves seat before excused.
  • Runs about or climbs excessively or at inappropriate times.
  • Difficulty playing quietly, such as board games.
  • Always "on the go," "driven by a motor". Talks excessively.

Impulsivity

  • Blurts answers before questions are finished. Speaks before thinking.
  • Interrupts or intrudes on conversations or games.
  • Problem waiting for their turn.

Inattentive Type

  • Misses details or makes repeated careless mistakes.
  • Trouble keeping attention on tasks or play. Easily distracted.
  • Often does not seem to listen when spoken to directly.
  • Does not follow through to complete tasks.
  • Trouble organizing activities. Disorganized.
  • Avoids tasks requiring mental effort.
  • Loses things, forgetful, easily bored.

ADHD Treatment

Treatment emphasizes self-regulation, social functioning, concentration, attention and focus, and is usually outpatient: individual, family and group therapy. Hospitalization only if the child is an imminent danger to self or others. Behavioral management plus FDA-approved medication gives the best results. Stimulants are first line. Non-stimulants and antidepressants are second line.

Nonpharmacological Treatment

  • Parent management training (PMT): teaches the family to respectfully modify the child's behavior, identifies maladaptive parent-child interactions, and replaces coercive discipline with prosocial, nurturing behaviors.
  • Increasing problem-solving skills and coping mechanisms.
  • Group therapy: 8 to 12 weekly sessions with a trained therapist, aiding behavior at home, in the classroom and with peers.
  • Play therapy and CBT are not as effective as these for ADHD.

Practical Tips to Teach

  • Keep a schedule.
  • Organize everyday items: a place for everything, such as a hook for the backpack by the door.
  • Use homework and notebook organizers, write down assignments, and bring home the books needed.
  • Be specific, clear and consistent. "Put your toys in the toy box," not "clean up this mess." Make eye contact and get down to their level.
  • Give praise or rewards when rules are followed.
  • Set and reward small, attainable goals to build self-esteem, since these children often receive criticism.

ADHD Medications: Stimulants

How Stimulants Are Used

  • Improve attention and focus and decrease hyperactivity by increasing dopamine, which has a paradoxical calming, focusing effect in ADHD. In a person without ADHD they rev the person up.
  • Begin at a low dose and work up. Not weight dependent, unlike many pediatric medications.
  • At least 80% of children respond to one of the stimulants, so if one or two fail or cause side effects, a third may be tried before a non-stimulant.
  • Long-acting stimulants last 8 to 12 hours and can be taken once a day, useful when a school dose cannot be given.
  • Most are formulations of methylphenidate or amphetamine, and the trade name identifies the formulation, so know both generic and trade names.

Side Effects and Monitoring

Side Effects of Stimulants

  • Decreased appetite, headaches, stomachaches, trouble getting to sleep, jitteriness, social withdrawal.
  • Nervousness, overstimulation, tachycardia or bradycardia, hypertension, restlessness, insomnia, dry mouth, unpleasant taste, diarrhea.
  • Usually managed by adjusting the dose or when it is given.
  • A child who appears dull or overly restricted, the "zombie effect": treat by decreasing the dose or changing medication.

Drug holidays: no stimulant on weekends, holidays, vacations or summer break, when the child does not need to concentrate in class. They counteract side effects, help the drug keep working at the same level, and give the body a break.

Outpatient Monitoring on a Stimulant

At each visit check heart rate and blood pressure, weight (stimulants suppress appetite, so confirm the child is gaining at the expected rate), and a random urine drug screen. A negative screen in a child who should be taking the stimulant means it is no longer prescribed, because of abuse and diversion.
