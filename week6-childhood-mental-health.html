Childhood Mental Health

Mental Illness in Children & Adolescents

About 20% of children and adolescents in the US have a major mental illness causing significant impairment in functioning at home, at school, with peers or in the community. Because it starts in childhood, it disrupts the normal pattern of development, academically, socially and psychologically, and into adulthood.

Most Common Disorders, Ages 8 to 15

  1. ADHD, the most common.
  2. Mood disorders, with major depression the largest.
  3. Conduct disorder.
  4. Dysthymia.
  5. Anxiety disorders, then panic disorder and GAD.
  6. Eating disorders, the least common.

Barriers to Assessment, Diagnosis and Treatment

  • Stigma, felt by the child, the parents or both. Adolescents fear peers will find out.
  • Young children lack the language to describe thoughts, feelings or symptoms.
  • No consistent screening, and poor coordination among pediatrician, school and school nurse.
  • Lack of providers and the high cost of mental health care.
  • Premature termination of treatment, when parents disagree with it or cannot keep paying.

Effects of Childhood Mental Illness

  • Long-term mental disorders in adulthood if untreated. Untreated childhood depression can become dysthymia or major depressive disorder.
  • Thwarted development, decreased self-esteem and diminished productivity.
  • Conflict within the family and community.
  • Child welfare and juvenile justice involvement. About 70% of youth in national juvenile centers have a mental health disorder.
  • Special education resources needed.
  • Physical health impairments, commonly from poor self-care.

Risk Factors, Resilience & Protective Factors

Risk FactorIncludes
BiologicalGenetic predisposition, neurobiological factors, and prenatal or postnatal factors.
PsychologicalTemperament: the attitude, mood or behavior a child uses to cope with the environment.
EnvironmentalAbuse or trauma (emotional, physical, sexual, neglect), low socioeconomic status, maternal psychiatric disorders, and parenting: poor role modeling, overcontrolling or emotionally unavailable parents, parental criminal history.

Resiliency

Bouncing back from adversity, and a protective factor against mental illness. Children with high resiliency:

  • Adapt to change and adversity.
  • Rely on inner strength and healthy coping strategies.
  • Function amid strong emotions.
  • Reach out for help and support.
  • Form nurturing relationships with other adults when a parent is unavailable.

Protective Factors: Promote Mental Health by Increasing Resilience

  • Positive self-image.
  • Family cohesion and absence of discord.
  • Positive relationship with at least one parent.
  • Positive early family experiences.
  • Support from teachers, grandparents, parents or peers.
  • Academic achievement and positive feedback from educators.
  • Positive peer relationships.
  • Temperament, which good parenting can adjust.

Nurses help the child identify strengths and build on them, the building blocks of self-esteem. Most protective factors come down to support and social connectedness.

Role of the PMH Nurse

With Children, Adolescents and Their Families, the Nurse Focuses On

  • A thorough assessment.
  • Early identification is key, including early identification of depression through screening and referral.
  • Identifying family needs, and parent education on suicide risk factors.
  • Promoting children's rights in treatment settings.
  • Avoiding seclusion and restraint.
  • Notifying parents.

Seclusion and Restraint with Children

  • A last resort, as with adults, after less restrictive interventions have failed, and only for behavior dangerous to the child or others.
  • Requires continuous monitoring by trained staff.
  • Never used as punishment.
  • Notify parents or guardians whenever a PRN medication is given, a child is physically held, or seclusion or restraint is used, along with documenting and calling the provider.

Assessment & the Interview

The clinical interview is the primary assessment tool. Explain the process to the child and parents, base the interaction on the child's developmental level, and build a treatment alliance by being genuine. Assess the interaction between child and parent: is the child held, or pulling away?

Children Report Internal Symptoms Better

Mood, sleep, suicidal ideation and self-harm.

Parents Report External Symptoms Better

Behavior, getting along with peers, and relationships.

Same Format as Adults, Except

  • Children need simple phrases. They are concrete, literal, here-and-now thinkers.
  • Use direct questions rather than open-ended ones, the opposite of the adult approach.
  • Corroborate information with the adult. Ask for a detailed view of the problem, give parents time to express frustration, and stay non-judgmental.
  • Play media may be used.
  • A child may not give an accurate timeline.

Conducting the Interview by Age

Age GroupDevelopmental TraitsApproach
PreschoolDifficulty putting feelings into words, concrete thinking.Use play and conduct the assessment in a playroom. Get down on the floor and talk while playing, or have the child draw their feelings.
School-ageAble to use constructs and give longer explanations.Establish rapport through competitive games, such as short board games or tic-tac-toe.
AdolescentsEgocentric, increasingly self-conscious, fear of being shamed.Tell them what will be shared with parents, with a direct, candid approach. Suicidal statements are always shared with parents and the team.

Meet with the whole family first, then the child alone if school-age and articulate (adolescents disclose more about drugs and sex with the parent out of the room), then the parents separately, then everyone together. In Kentucky, adolescents make their own mental health decisions from age 16, and information such as sexual history is not shared with parents after 16 without the adolescent's permission. This varies state by state.

AreaCovers
Family functioningThe parent-child relationship and how the family gets along and works on problems.
Current problemNature, severity and length. How upsetting is it, and what makes it better or worse? Triggers, behaviors at home, response to discipline, empathy, violence and risks.
HistoryPrevious treatment (inpatient, outpatient, neuropsych testing, past medications), family history, developmental (pregnancy, birth, milestones), social (household, siblings, legal problems), abuse and CPS involvement, chemical, medical (head injuries, seizures, vision or hearing deficits) and school.

Physical examPart of every assessment.

Developmental Assessment

Know what is normal to tell whether a child is falling behind. It covers intellectual functioning, gross motor, fine motor, cognition, thinking and perception, and social interaction and play.

DomainAbility
CognitiveTo learn and solve problems.
Social and emotionalTo interact with others, including helping themselves and self-control. A six-week-old smiles.
Speech and languageTo both understand and use language.
Fine motorTo use small muscles, especially hands and fingers: picking up small objects, holding a spoon, using a crayon.
Gross motorTo use large muscles: sitting up, pulling to stand, skipping.

Behavior & Communication Principles

Basic Principles of Children's Behavior

  • All behavior has meaning.
  • Address the need behind the behavior.
  • Children want to behave and please those they care about.
  • Children with mental health issues have difficulty communicating their needs.

Basic Communication Guidelines

  • Treat youngsters with respect and preserve their dignity. Do not embarrass them.
  • Seek solutions, not blame, and model the behavior you want.
  • Avoid "no" and "don't". Use "do" and "let's do it this way".
  • Instill hope by helping the child communicate needs clearly, manage feelings, and learn they are competent and worthwhile.

Play Therapy

Play Is the "Language" of Children

  • Appropriate for younger children. It lets them express pain, grief and other difficult feelings they cannot yet put into words.
  • A vehicle for change, expression of feelings, trust and relationship building. The goal is not fun but finding out what the child feels and giving it an outlet.
  • Rooted in psychodynamic therapy.
  • A creative and dynamic process that cannot be standardized. Every session differs, and it is performed by trained personnel.
  • The therapist is a trusted participant, never the aberrant perpetrator. Taking a violent role could make the child re-create the traumatic event. The child may play the perpetrator, which gives an outlet for feelings about the trauma.
  • Puppets, miniatures, drawing and feeling-word games all work. A puppet can become the child's mouthpiece, drawing out what direct questioning would not.
